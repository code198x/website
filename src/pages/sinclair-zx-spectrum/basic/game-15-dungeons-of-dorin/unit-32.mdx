## What You Built

A complete RPG with five interacting systems:

**Procedural generation:**
- 16x16 dungeon grid with 3-5 random rooms per floor
- L-shaped corridors connecting every room
- Stairs, chests, fountains, and monsters placed during generation
- Different layout on every run

**Exploration:**
- Turn-based QAOP movement with wall collision
- 7x7 viewport centred on the player
- Fog of war with visibility tracking
- Status panel showing all stats and equipment bonuses

**Combat:**
- Turn-based with attack and flee options
- Dice roll formula: `INT(RND*6)+1 + stat - defence`
- Minimum 1 damage prevents stalemates
- Six monster types from rat to dragon
- Monster drops on defeat

**Equipment and inventory:**
- Weapons boost attack, armour boosts defence
- Automatic upgrade on pickup — better items replace weaker ones
- Potions heal 10 HP, maximum 3 carried
- Fountains restore full HP once
- Floor-based scaling for equipment quality

**Progression:**
- Ten floors of escalating difficulty
- Monster type pools deepen with floor number
- Victory at the Heart of Dorin on floor 10
- Death screen with run statistics
- Save and load for persistent progress

## The Five Systems

This is the most complex game the learner has built. Previous games had one or two interacting systems — movement plus collision, or a board plus rules. Dungeons of Dorin has five: movement, combat, inventory, procedural generation, and persistence. Each system touches the others:

- **Generation** creates the dungeon that **movement** traverses
- **Movement** triggers **combat** by walking into monsters
- **Combat** uses stats modified by **inventory** (weapons, armour)
- **Inventory** items come from **generation** (chests) and combat (drops)
- **Persistence** captures all four systems in a save file

This web of dependencies is what makes the game feel cohesive. No system exists in isolation. Change one — add a new monster type, adjust the potion heal amount, alter the room size range — and the effects ripple through the others.

## Turn-Based Advantage

The turn-based design made this complexity possible in BASIC. There is no frame rate pressure. The interpreter processes each turn as slowly as it needs to — checking walls, scanning monster arrays, rendering the viewport, updating the status panel. In a real-time game, this processing would create visible lag. In a turn-based game, it is invisible. The player waits for input, the game processes, and the result appears.

This is a deliberate lesson. Complex systems are viable in any language when you remove the clock. The constraint is not processing power — it is time pressure.
