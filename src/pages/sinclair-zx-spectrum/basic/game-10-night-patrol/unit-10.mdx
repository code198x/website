## How It Works

**Lines 95-97** set up the objective and exit. `o` and `p` are the objective's row and column. `w` and `x` are the exit's row and column. `j` tracks whether the objective has been collected — 0 means not yet, 1 means collected.

**Lines 105-106** draw the objective as a yellow `*` and the exit as a blue `X`. The exit starts in blue (INK 1), barely visible against the dark background — it's there, but not inviting.

**Line 199** checks for objective collection. If the player is on the objective cell and hasn't collected it yet (`j = 0`), set `j = 1`, play a positive beep, redraw the exit in green (INK 4) to show it's now open, and update the HUD.

**Line 200** checks for the exit. If the player is on the exit cell and the objective is collected (`j = 1`), the floor is complete.

**Lines 885-886** update the HUD message based on `j`. Before collection: "Find the * objective" in yellow. After collection: "Go to X exit!" in green. The player always knows what to do next.

## Gated Progression

The objective gates the exit. This creates a natural path through the map: first go to the objective (often in a dangerous area), then navigate to the exit (often requiring a different route back). Two trips through the guard's patrol zone, doubling the risk.

This is a common game design pattern. Collect the key, then reach the door. Get the flag, then return to base. The gate forces the player to engage with the environment rather than beelining for the goal.

## Visual Feedback

The exit colour change (blue to green) is critical. Without it, the player wouldn't know the exit is ready. The HUD text reinforces the message. Together, they make the game state obvious at a glance.

## Try This

**Swap positions.** Put the objective near the exit and the player's start far away, or vice versa. How does this change the route the player has to take?

**Sound on exit.** Add a longer victory beep when reaching the exit. The moment of escape deserves a fanfare.
