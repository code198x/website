Two players, two ships and a way to shoot. That describes a contest, but it leaves a great deal undecided. Can you fire continuously? Can you always escape? Does letting go stop you? Can the other player understand what you've just done?

Spacewar!'s rules give us concrete answers to examine. We can change one of them on paper and ask what the players would gain or lose.

## A shot spends an opportunity

In the 3.1 edition used by [our study](/catalogue/editorial-study/), ammunition is limited and the firing tube has a reload delay. Holding fire sends successive shots when the tube is ready. The source keeps a separate torpedo count and reload state for each ship. [Spacewar! 3.1 source listing](https://www.masswerk.at/spacewar/sources/spacewar3.1_complete.txt)

Firing can threaten the opponent immediately, but it also uses something you may want later. The reload delay gives the other player a little space between shots. Even if you still have ammunition, you can't place every projectile wherever you want at once.

Make ammunition unlimited in an imagined version. What changes? Players might spend less effort choosing a shot and more effort maintaining a stream. That could be enjoyable, but it asks a different question of them. Keep the limited supply and remove the reload delay instead: now they might empty it in a burst. The two limits do different jobs.

A **resource** becomes interesting through the choices it constrains. A number on its own doesn't create a decision. What matters is what spending it lets the player do and what they give up by spending it now.

## A manoeuvre has consequences

Thrust changes velocity. Releasing thrust doesn't cancel that motion, and gravity keeps acting. So a player who commits to an approach may need time and further thrust to change it.

That's a form of **commitment**: an action leaves consequences the player must work with. It gives the opponent something to notice and respond to. An approaching ship can become a more predictable target, even while its pilot is trying to line up a shot.

Imagine a control that replaces the ship's velocity instantly in any direction. Escapes and reversals become easier. Some of the prediction shifts from judging a developing course to reacting to abrupt changes. Our published [Game Feel](/craft/game-feel/) material lets us compare movement rules; the Spacewar example adds another person who can exploit their consequences.

## An escape can create a new problem

Hyperspace gets a ship out of immediate danger, but in version 3.1 re-entry changes its position and velocity and can destroy it. The program also keeps a recharge state and increases the danger associated with successive uses. [Spacewar! 3.1 hyperspace routines](https://www.masswerk.at/spacewar/sources/spacewar3.1_complete.txt)

The decision depends on the present threat. A risky escape can be attractive when a collision seems imminent and much less attractive when the ship still has room to manoeuvre.

That is a **risk–reward choice** we can explain through the action itself. Compare two imagined changes: a completely safe jump with a long wait between uses, and a dangerous jump available more often. Both can limit escape, but they ask different things of the player. Which would suit the sort of duel you're trying to make?

## Let the other player read the contest

The ship's outline tells us which way it's facing. Exhaust shows a thrust input. A burst of points shows destruction. This is **feedback**: the game makes the consequences of actions perceptible.

It also supplies information to the opponent. If one player can understand a developing threat, they have a chance to respond. We can call that response **counterplay**. A powerful move can remain interesting when the other player has a way to anticipate, avoid or answer it.

The playing arrangement matters as well. Russell remembered quiet switches preventing an opponent from hearing unsuccessful attempts to fire after ammunition ran out. Information could leak through the physical controls. [Russell's 2008 interview, pp. 15–16](https://archive.computerhistory.org/resources/access/text/2012/08/102746453-05-01-acc.pdf#page=15)

## Change a rule, then follow it through

Choose one rule from the study. Describe a moment in which it matters, then change it. Follow the result through both players' choices. A safe escape changes the attacker's prospects as well as the defender's; more readable shots change aiming and avoidance together.

The [Vault's Game Design article](/vault/design/game-design/) follows designers discussing the decisions their games give people. Here we have a particular game whose decisions we can examine closely. We don't need to declare every original choice perfect to understand what it contributes.
