Digitised sprites are photographs or video frames of real people and objects, captured, cut out and reduced to a machine's palette, then used as game graphics in place of drawn ones. The look was in use on 8-bit home computers by 1988, reached arcade fighting games with Atari's *Pit-Fighter* in 1990, and became a house style at Williams/Midway with *Mortal Kombat* and *NBA Jam*. It faded when rendered 3D models and then real-time polygons offered the same realism with smoother motion.

## Fast facts

- **Earliest uses held here:** *MicroLeague Wrestling* and *The Three Stooges* on the C64 (reviewed 1988); Dynamix's *David Wolf: Secret Agent* on PC (reviewed 1990).
- **Arcade:** *Pit-Fighter* (Atari Games, 1990), "the first beat 'em up to use digitised characters" (*Megatech*, 1991); Williams' own claim of *NARC* (1988) as its first digitised game.
- **Defining use:** *[Mortal Kombat](/vault/games/mortal-kombat)* (Midway, 1992) and its sequels; *[NBA Jam](/vault/games/nba-jam)* (1993).
- **Not the same thing:** pre-rendered CG sprites (*[Donkey Kong Country](/vault/games/donkey-kong-country)*, *[Killer Instinct](/vault/games/killer-instinct)*) are 3D models rendered offline, not photographs.

## Digitised versus rendered

Two techniques of the same years get confused because both produce sprites that look "more realistic than hand-drawn".

| Type | Source | Examples |
|------|--------|----------|
| **Digitised** | Real actors or objects on video or film, frames captured and palette-reduced | *Mortal Kombat*, *Pit-Fighter*, *NBA Jam*, *MicroLeague Wrestling* |
| **Pre-rendered** | 3D models rendered on workstations, output as sprites | *Donkey Kong Country*, *Killer Instinct* |

*Edge* described Rare's pipeline in 1994: "Everything in the game — the sprites, the backgrounds and the 6,000+ frames of animation — is rendered", using "Alias' PowerAnimator software running on Silicon Graphics equipment". No camera was involved. Hand-drawn sprites (*Street Fighter II*) are the third category, and the one *Edge* preferred: "Mortal Kombat II's digitised fighters lack the personality of their more cartoon-like counterparts in Street Fighter II."

## How Mortal Kombat did it

*Nintendo Power* visited Williams during *Mortal Kombat 3* and got the process from [Ed Boon](/vault/people/ed-boon) and [John Tobias](/vault/people/john-tobias).

**Casting.** "Some of the actors are martial artists and some of them aren't," Tobias said. "Basically they just have to be real ... They have to be able to withstand eight hours of jumping, ducking, kicking, hitting and flying across the screen."

**Shooting.** Moves were acted out first by Boon and Tobias for the performers — "We show them how to fly across the screen," Boon joked — and "many moves are unplanned and come from improvization during the shooting sessions".

**Digitising and clean-up.** "Once the raw video was shot and digitized, the frames got individual attention. Character frames were limited to 64 colors, but John Tobias and Steve Beran bumped certain colors to enhance clarity and spent endless hours retouching everything by hand." The backgrounds were not photographs at all: one artist "uses 3-D rendering programs to create some of the elements", another "puts together the finished scene using programs like Photoshop".

**Budget.** *MK3* took "10 months and seven people" and "18 Megabytes of ROM ... more than twice as much memory as the original Mortal Kombat". Frame count is where the memory goes, which is why the technique was always tighter on home machines than in the arcade.

## Economies

**Palette swaps.** One actor in one costume, recoloured, is several characters. A 1994 review of the Amiga conversion complained that arcade fans "will quickly notice such shortcuts as using the same frames of animation for Scorpion and Sub-Zero" with only a colour change.

**Fewer frames.** Video runs at 25 or 30 frames a second; a cartridge cannot hold that for every move of every character, so frames are dropped. On the Amiga the result was that *Mortal Kombat*'s visuals "may look good in still pictures — but when seen in action, the jerkiness and poor animation is immediately apparent."

**Scaling hardware.** *Pit-Fighter*'s coin-op paired its "256-colour digitised graphics" with "specialist hardware to shrink and scale the sprites and backgrounds so that the viewer's 'camera' can follow the fighting action". The home conversions had no such hardware; Teque's Peter Jefferies stored the sprites as 64×64 16-colour images and called the scaling "without doubt the hardest aspect".

## Before the arcades

The technique did not begin with *Pit-Fighter*. *MicroLeague Wrestling* on the C64 "features Hulk Hogan and other stars of the Stateside ring", with "digitised interview sequences between 'Mean Gene' Okerlund and each wrestler" — *Zzap!64* found the graphics "well-timed but sometimes indistinct". [Cinemaware](/vault/companies/cinemaware)'s *The Three Stooges* had "superb digitised graphics" the same month. Dynamix's *David Wolf: Secret Agent* on PC used "actors and digitised graphics", which a reviewer in early 1990 said "set it apart from every other game of its kind" and gave it "a very adult atmosphere". Williams' Roger Sharpe told *Nintendo Power* that *NARC* (1988) was "the first game, to use digitized graphics"; it was at least the first at Williams, and *NBA Jam* five years later was "using digitized graphics extensively", to the point that "some of the actual players who are featured in the game have made comments like 'It would be great if I really could shoot three-pointers like that!'"

## Reception and decline

The realism was the point and the problem. It made *Mortal Kombat*'s violence look like violence, in the years when Senators Lieberman and Kohl were sponsoring "video game rating legislation" and Sega was printing its own ratings on boxes. And it dated quickly: by late 1994 *Edge* could assume readers had "railed against poorly digitised actors laid on top of unimaginative rendered backgrounds". Rendered sprites, then real-time polygons, gave the same photographic ambition with animation that did not stutter, and [motion capture](/vault/techniques/motion-capture) took over the job of getting real performers into a game.
