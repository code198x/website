In June 1988 Sierra On-Line built a small cinema at the Consumer Electronics Show. Every couple of hours an invited audience watched the opening of *King's Quest IV* on a 46-inch colour monitor, driven by a 386 PC and a Roland MT-32 playing an orchestral score, and John Williams remembered a woman crying as King Graham fell to the ground. What they were watching was a new system, the **Sierra Creative Interpreter (SCI)**. It doubled the resolution of Sierra's older [AGI](/vault/techniques/agi-engine) games, gave them music written for sound cards, and replaced AGI's fixed set of adventure commands with an object-oriented language in which programmers could write a game's behaviour themselves. SCI went on to run Sierra's adventures for most of the next decade, from *King's Quest IV* through the icon-driven games of the early 1990s to *Phantasmagoria*.

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** [Sierra On-Line](/vault/companies/sierra); designed by Jeff Stephenson, Sierra's systems engineer.
- **First game:** *King's Quest IV: The Perils of Rosella* (1988).
- **Graphics:** 320×200 in 16 colours at first, "double the graphics resolution of previous Sierra adventures"; 256 colours from 1990; 640×480 with SCI-32 by 1994.
- **Sound:** IBM's eight-voice music card, the AdLib card and the Roland MT-32 for *King's Quest IV*; "over ten (10) different sound cards" by *Code-Name: Iceman* (1990).
- **Interface:** a typed parser at first; icons and a pointer from *King's Quest V* (1990).
- **Machines:** MS-DOS first, then Atari ST, Amiga and Macintosh; never the Apple II or IIGS.
- **Today:** ScummVM runs SCI games, from *King's Quest IV* to the SCI-32 titles.

## Why Sierra built it

The trigger was *King's Quest IV*. According to *Wireframe*, Ken and Roberta Williams asked Jeff Stephenson for new features for the game, among them more colours than the 16 AGI supported, and support for CD-ROM drives and sound cards. Stephenson used the request to throw out AGI's adventure-game language altogether. In AGI, he explains, almost any new behaviour "first had to be programmed into the game system itself before it could be used to write the game". With a general-purpose language, "the game developers could write the behaviour themselves – in the game, without any work in the engine."

John Williams, writing Sierra's ten-year history in 1990, put SCI at the end of a chain: AGL (Adventure Game Language) for *Mystery House* and *The Wizard and the Princess*, then "a dozen different versions of AGI", then, "in the spring of 1988", SCI. It had better graphics and animation than AGI and more flexibility "for programmers doing code for parsers and pointing devices". The cost was hardware: SCI games "would require a lot more memory and a fairly fast processor".

## How it worked

Sierra's Spring 1989 newsletter printed an excerpt from Donald B. Trivette's *The Official Book of King's Quest* that followed one room of *King's Quest IV*, the fisherman's shack, from Roberta Williams's sketch to the finished game. It called SCI "an object-oriented language similar to LISP; it's written in Assembler, C, and itself". The programmer set up classes of objects: an Actor for "anything that moves such as sea gulls, ogres, and Ego" (the programmers' name for the player's character), a Prop for things "that move but don't go anywhere – like a candle flame or smoke", and a View for things that do not animate at all. Scripts then defined "how the objects come together and interact with one another".

The article also printed code for the room, such as the nine lines that made the chimney smoke, from `animate.obj( smoke);` to `draw( smoke);`, and the logic for knocking on the fisherman's door. Those commands, though, are the ones fans have documented for AGI's logic language, which the 256K version of *King's Quest IV* was written in. Real SCI source looks quite different. The SCI Companion documentation describes Sierra's own syntax as "sort of a cross between LISP and SmallTalk".

The art was built with Sierra's own tools. Backgrounds were drawn in a Picture Editor and "stored as coordinates and vectors" rather than as finished pictures, because vectors took less space. Characters were drawn as pixels in a View Editor, with one colour set aside as invisible, and animated by cycling through cells like a flip book. The fisherman needed 76 drawings, and Rosella "more than one thousand". Depth came from "16 bands" of priority that the artist used "like a horizontal grid", and control lines set where Rosella could walk. Get them wrong and "Ego will walk right through" a tree. A room averaged 12,000 to 14,000 bytes; the fisherman's shack took 12,700.

Each computer had its own interpreter for the same game files. Roberta Williams explained to *START* in 1990 that "SCI is a virtual machine language which means that it will work on any machine", and that each machine's version could use its own strengths, such as the Atari ST's colour fades.

## King's Quest IV and the first wave

Sierra launched SCI as a line of "technologically superior interactive movies, complete with stereo soundtracks, fully animated cartoon sequences and double the graphics resolution". The newsletter counted over 11 man-years and a team of more than 13 on *King's Quest IV*, and 40 minutes of music by William Goldstein. The score was, John Williams admitted, "a big gamble". The game was also late. Al Lowe told *Wireframe* that in late August 1988 Sierra put every other game on hold so that the whole company could program *King's Quest IV* for 30 days.

Speed was the price. For owners of older machines Sierra released an AGI version at the same time, which in John Williams's words "paled by comparison". On a PCjr expanded to 640K, the newsletter warned, "it takes an animated character almost 23 seconds to move across the screen". The Software Publishers Association nominated *King's Quest IV* for its sound and SCI itself for technical achievement.

"It seemed only natural that Sierra's other designers would latch on to SCI," John Williams wrote: *Police Quest II*, *[Leisure Suit Larry](/vault/games/leisure-suit-larry) II* and *[Space Quest](/vault/games/space-quest) III* followed. *Computer Gaming World* found that *Police Quest II* had "doubled the graphic resolution" and added "zoom shots, split screens and film wipes", with characters "no longer virtual stick figures". *START* praised *Larry II*'s "fairly sophisticated parser".

## Machine by machine

SCI reached the [Atari ST](/vault/systems/atari-st) early. By spring 1989 Sierra listed ST versions of *Space Quest III*, *King's Quest IV*, *Police Quest II*, *Silpheed* and *Larry II*, and the ST conversion of *Space Quest III* reached shops less than ten days after the PC version. The [Amiga](/vault/systems/commodore-amiga) was harder. The conversion ran "over six months behind schedule", slowed when one of its programmers left, and the first Amiga SCI game, *Space Quest III*, shipped in October 1989. The Macintosh cost Sierra a product: its programmer of *3-D Helicopter Simulator* was moved onto SCI for the Mac, and the simulator was shelved.

The [Apple II](/vault/systems/apple-ii) line never got SCI. In autumn 1989 Ken Williams said it was "just too big and too complex for the relatively slow CPU of the Apple IIGS machine", and Sierra stopped work on it. *INFO* confirmed a year later that Sierra's new games would not appear on the IIGS or Apple II at all.

## From parser to icons

SCI began with a parser, and by *Code-Name: Iceman* (1990) *Computer Gaming World* noted "more 'point and click' interaction" alongside it. With *[King's Quest V](/vault/games/kings-quest)* (1990) Sierra dropped typing altogether in favour of a pointer and icons. *Wireframe* says the change came after Roberta Williams watched her mother struggle to play her games.

In the Spring 1992 *InterAction*, Ken Williams called it "the much discussed change to the Sierra Adventure Game interface" and put it to two designers. Al Lowe "didn't like the icon interface at first. Spent too many years learning to type fast", but came to prefer it: "less verbage = more puzzles". Roberta Williams said she had designed it "with the future in mind … especially as it relates to fancy CD-Rom based entertainment machines", because "most people don't want to take the time to learn either how to type, spell, or figure out just how you talk to a computer via an adventure game!" *Computer Gaming World* later put *King's Quest V* in its hall of fame for redefining "the graphic adventure with new parser and VGA graphics".

The same years brought 256-colour VGA graphics, which *Wireframe* credits to *King's Quest V* and *VI*. Sierra also remade its older AGI games in SCI: first the original *King's Quest* and *Mixed-Up Mother Goose*, announced in 1990, then *Space Quest* and *Leisure Suit Larry*, advertised in 1992 with "256 VGA colour graphics". Fans who study the interpreter today divide its history into versions: SCI0 for the 16-colour parser games, SCI1 and SCI1.1 for the 256-colour games, and the 32-bit SCI2, SCI2.1 and SCI3.

## SCI-32

In spring 1994 *InterAction* told customers why *Police Quest: Open Season*, *Quest for Glory: Shadows of Darkness* and *Gabriel Knight* needed four megabytes and a 386: Sierra had "debuted SCI-32, a new proprietary development system for computer games" with them. According to the SCI Wiki, the 32-bit versions added high-resolution 640×480 graphics. SCI-32 ran Sierra's CD-ROM adventures of the mid-1990s, among them *King's Quest VII*, *Gabriel Knight 2*, *Phantasmagoria* and *Leisure Suit Larry 7*.

## After Sierra

Fans took SCI apart as they had AGI. The FreeSCI project reimplemented the interpreter, and its work went into ScummVM, which added the 16- and 256-colour SCI games (SCI0 to SCI1.1) in version 1.2.0 in October 2010 and the SCI-32 games in version 2.0.0 in December 2017. Hobbyist tools such as SCI Studio and SCI Companion let fans write new games in the language.

Source code has followed. In October 2024 Digital Alchemy Studios published the source of an SCI script compiler on GitHub under the MIT licence, maintained by Daniel Arnold. Its notes say the compiler was "originally developed under proprietary ownership by Codemasters" before passing to Digital Alchemy Studios.
