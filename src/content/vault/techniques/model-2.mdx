In 1994 an arcade driving game borrowed its looks from military flight simulators. Sega's Model 2 board, which made its debut with *[Daytona USA](/vault/games/daytona-usa)*, painted its polygons with texture-mapping chips from the American firm that built simulator graphics for the defence industry, first as part of General Electric and then of Martin Marietta and Lockheed Martin. "As defence spending has been cut, we decided to take our technology into the arcades," that company's marketing manager told *Edge* in 1995. A year earlier, one of *Daytona*'s creators had told the magazine that the game "throws 300,000 texture-mapped and shaded polygons around every second". "Enough to knock anyone's socks off," *Edge* added.

## Fast facts

- **Partners:** [Sega](/vault/companies/sega) and General Electric Aerospace, from August 1992; the GE division passed to Martin Marietta, which merged with Lockheed in 1995.
- **First game:** *Daytona USA*, shown finished at the AOU show in Tokyo in February 1994.
- **Main processor:** a 32-bit RISC CPU at 25 MHz, Intel's i960.
- **Throughput:** 300,000 textured polygons a second, against about 180,000 untextured ones on Model 1.
- **Display:** 496×384, 65,536 colours from a 1,024-colour palette.
- **Revisions:** Model 2A-CRX, 2B-CRX and 2C-CRX.
- **Successor:** Model 3 (1996), built on Lockheed Martin's R3D/PRO-1000.

## How it was built

Sega's first polygon board, Model 1, ran *Virtua Racing* and *[Virtua Fighter](/vault/games/virtua-fighter)* with flat, untextured shapes. For the next board, [Yu Suzuki](/vault/people/yu-suzuki), head of [Sega AM2](/vault/companies/sega-am2), told *Edge* in June 1994, the offer came from the other side: "General Electric Co. approached us because they had realtime texture-mapping ASICs [application-specific integrated circuits] and we entered a commercial partnership with them in August 1992. We co-developed the Model 2 board with them over a period of a year, and Daytona GP took a further year to develop." *Daytona GP* was *Edge*'s early name for *Daytona USA*.

*Edge* later told a less tidy story. Sega's own engineers, it reported in October 1995, had "failed to make significant progress towards an advanced texture-mapping version" of Model 1, so the American company "was called in to lend assistance". The group sat in Daytona Beach, Florida, "across the street from the Daytona International Speedway", and had already worked with Sega on Model 1. After that board was finished Martin Marietta bought GE Aerospace, and in 1995 Martin Marietta merged with Lockheed. In *Edge*'s summary, Model 2 was "a combination of Sega's Model 1 polygon engine technology and a Martin Marietta-designed texture-mapping board". The American side supplied the texture-mapping chips and its TARGET system for building 3D databases.

## How it draws a frame

The work is split between a host and a graphics part, as *Edge* explained for arcade image generators in general: a "host" that "basically runs the game", working out collisions and telling the graphics hardware where to put the polygons, and a graphics part that draws the scene. On Model 2 the host is the 25 MHz 32-bit RISC processor in *Edge*'s 1994 specification, which MAME, the arcade emulator, identifies as Intel's i960KB. Beside it sits the geometry engine, listed by *Edge* as a "3D matrix" coprocessor, which rotates and projects each shape's corners; MAME's emulation uses Fujitsu signal processors, the TGP, on the first boards. The Sega and Lockheed Martin rasteriser then fills each polygon with texture. Alongside the polygons, two scrolling layers of flat tile graphics come from the design of Sega's older System 24 board.

*Edge*'s specification box lists what the board could do to a polygon: flat shading, texture mapping, "micro-texture" and two reflection modes, one "spread" and one "mirror". Suzuki picked out the reflection of the sky in the cars' windows, "all handled in hardware by separate algorithms". He was also open about what *Daytona* left out. It "only uses Gouraud shading in the attract sequence — the actual game uses flat shading, which is much faster". Shading the whole game smoothly would, he thought, have made it "between three and five times slower", and he was "pretty sure *Ridge Racer* has been programmed in the same way". Asked whether Model 2 beat Namco's System 22, the board behind *Ridge Racer*, he answered: "Personally, yes."

## Daytona USA

*Edge* saw *Daytona* at 5% complete in its April 1994 issue. Its source put Model 2 at 300,000 textured polygons a second and Model 1 at "about 180,000 plain polygons", and promised up to eight players in linked cabinets. Suzuki had wanted more: "Originally, I had wanted to have 40 cars linked up, just like in a real race, but it just wasn't feasible." At the AOU show in February, Sega linked 13 cabinets together, and *Edge* reported that the first reaction was that the game was "better than *Ridge Racer*". The spec box gave *Daytona* 178 of the board's maximum 248 megabits of ROM.

The gap between the board and a home console showed a year later, when *Edge* previewed the [Saturn](/vault/systems/sega-saturn) version. In the arcade, "Daytona's dedicated graphics processors generate an amazing 5000 polygons per frame, every 1/60th of a second". AM2 was aiming for 30 frames a second on the Saturn, with each car "around 160 polygons", and the coin-op's "crisp 496x384 display", its micro-texture and its reflections would all be missing.

## Virtua Fighter 2 and Virtua Cop

At Japan's Amusement Machine Show late in 1994, Sega showed two more Model 2 games. *Virtua Fighter 2* ran "at 60fps (twice the speed of the original)", and *Edge* learned that the board used for it "has been speeded up and now includes extra memory, enabling the screen to draw further into the distance. This was one of the most noticeable problems that Daytona USA suffered from." AM2's *[Virtua Cop](/vault/games/virtua-cop)*, a light-gun game, had "fantastic Model 2 visuals which put it way ahead of the pack", though some observers called it "Lethal Enforcers with polygons".

## 2A, 2B and 2C

The board was revised three times, and MAME's emulation sorts the games by revision. The original Model 2 ran *Daytona USA*, *Virtua Cop* and *Desert Tank*, the last credited jointly to "Sega / Martin Marietta". Model 2A-CRX, with Sega's SCSP sound chip in place of the Model 1 sound board, ran *Virtua Fighter 2*, *[Sega Rally](/vault/games/sega-rally)*, *Virtua Cop 2* and *Manx TT Superbike*. Model 2B-CRX replaced the Fujitsu geometry processors with Analog Devices SHARC signal processors and ran *Virtua Striker*, *Fighting Vipers*, *Cyber Troopers Virtual-On*, *Indy 500* and *Last Bronx*. Model 2C-CRX used Fujitsu's newer TGPx4 and ran *Sega Touring Car Championship*, *[The House of the Dead](/vault/games/house-of-the-dead)* and *Top Skater*.

The period press was looser with the names. *Edge* described *Indy 500*'s board as "Sega's enhanced Model 2B board (first seen in Sega Rally)", and *Sega Saturn Magazine* said *Sega Touring Car* pushed "the Model 2B arcade graphics board to its limit". Some games needed more than one board. *Virtual-On*'s designers told *Edge* in 1996 that two players on separate monitors needed "two Model 2B boards", and an optional spectator screen "uses one more Model 2 board". Sega also sold the hardware to other companies: Tecmo's *Dead or Alive*, Psikyo's *Zero Gunner* and Jaleco's *Super GT 24h* all ran on Model 2 boards.

## Model 3

Suzuki was already naming the successor in June 1994: "the only one I can mention is Model 3. Compared to Model 2, this is a very high-performance board." It was late. In October 1995 *Edge* reported that *Indy 500* had been "originally targeted for Model 3, but delays in the board's progress meant it was coded up for Model 2 instead", and that the new board "has little in common with its forerunner": it was based on Lockheed Martin's R3D/PRO-1000 rather than Model 1's engine. *Virtua Fighter 3* made its debut on it in 1996.

Model 2 carried on beside it. Sega's AM3 team, interviewed by *Sega Saturn Magazine* in January 1998 about *The Lost World*, drew the line between the boards: "Model 2 is okay for Virtual stuff like robots and zombies", but living creatures needed Model 3's colour texture maps. *Virtual-On*'s robots and *The House of the Dead*'s zombies were both Model 2 games. Nor could code simply be moved across: "The Model 3 has a different Operating System (OS) to the one we used for the Model 2. We use a Windows '95 package for the Model 3."
