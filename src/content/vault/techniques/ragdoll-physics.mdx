Shoot a guard in an older 3D game and he plays his death animation: the same stagger and slump every time, whether he was standing on a staircase or against a wall. A ragdoll replaces that stored animation with a body. The character becomes rigid limbs joined at constrained joints, and the moment it dies the physics engine takes over, so it falls the way the forces dictate: back down the stairs, over the railing, folded against the wall. The word appears in *Edge* in 1998 as a simile for what such a body does when dropped. By 2002 reviewers used it as a feature name without explanation, and by 2005 the physics vendors were trying to get past it.

## Fast facts

- **The idea in print:** Chris Hecker in *Edge*, August 1998: build a creature from "bodies and joints" and "you can drop this creature and it will buckle and fall to the ground like a ragdoll".
- **Early shipping use:** IO Interactive's *[Hitman: Codename 47](/vault/games/hitman)* (2000), whose corpses fell "in unique ways depending on where they were hit".
- **The showcase:** MathEngine's Karma engine in *Unreal Tournament 2003*, which *Edge* called "the first attempt" at ragdoll characters and which *PC Gamer* said showed off ragdoll deaths "to gratuitous effect".
- **The middleware answer:** [Havok](/vault/tools/havok) 2.0 (2003), "up to ten ragdoll models in realtime".
- **The put-down:** Gabe Newell's "flopping-around corpses".

## The word

*Edge*'s August 1998 feature on character animation gave most of a page to Chris Hecker, "a veteran coder and respected authority on ingame physics", and his argument against [motion capture](/vault/technologies/motion-capture): it "doesn't tell you anything about the dynamics of the movement. What does a motion-captured animation do if one of its limbs hits something or gets tied down? Nothing. It either continues the animation and looks stupid, or it stops playing the animation and looks stupid!"

His alternative was the constrained body. Take a lower arm, "constrain it to another body (the upper arm) and set the type of joint between them… a hinge or elbow", switch on the simulator, and "if I pull on the wrist, the arm straightens out and then drags along after me. Basically, it acts like a real arm." Build a whole creature that way and, if the simulator handles contact, "you can drop this creature and it will buckle and fall to the ground like a ragdoll." That sentence describes the whole technique. Hecker was clear that the falling-down part was the easy bit: "the real hard part" was "a controller that acts as the creature's muscles and makes it stand up and act like it's alive". Games would ship the easy bit first.

## First bodies

One game had already tried the hard part. DreamWorks Interactive's *Trespasser: Jurassic Park*, reaching shops at the end of 1998, built its dinosaurs from the inside out. *Computer Gaming World*'s preview in 1997 explained that Seamus Blackley "developed skeletal models for each of the dinosaurs" and "connected the skeletons with artificial muscle connections", each part given weight and strength, so that the model handled "skeletal and muscle movement according to authentic physical principles", down to the recoil when a dinosaur was hit. That was physics for living creatures, not corpses, and it was not called a ragdoll.

The corpses came two years later, in *Hitman: Codename 47* (2000). Thomas Jakobsen, who wrote its physics at IO Interactive, described the system in "Advanced Character Physics", presented at the Game Developers Conference in 2001. It moved cloth and plants, and made "dead human bodies fall in unique ways depending on where they were hit, fully interacting with the environment". A *Hitman* corpse was a stick figure: points joined by fixed-length "sticks", with angular limits for the joints and extra constraints between the knees so that "the legs never cross". Each stick collided with the scenery as a capped cylinder, and the limbs the player saw were fitted to the points afterwards, "making certain that knees and elbows bend naturally". The method was cheap because it did not solve the constraints exactly: it nudged each one back into place in turn and let the result settle over a few frames. Jakobsen noted that the press had called the result "lifelike death animations", which he thought an oxymoron.

## Flopping-around corpses

Looking back from 2005, *Edge* credited "the first attempt" at physics-driven characters to "the integration of MathEngine's Karma engine into *Unreal Tournament 2003* to drive the motion of ragdoll characters". *Hitman* had shipped physics-driven bodies two years earlier, so *Edge*'s "first" is best read as the first time a game sold itself on ragdolls. *PC Gamer*'s 2002 review found the feature already "much-touted": Bombing Run's "spiky girder-filled pits" were "a device that shows off the Math Engine ragdoll physics to gratuitous effect", and dying meant "a realistic and ghastly sequence" of a body tumbling. It "only modelled the impact of forces and collisions on inanimate skeletons", in *Edge*'s words, and that is what Valve's Gabe Newell would later call "flopping-around corpses".

The demand was there regardless. Havok's Steve Collins told *Edge* in March 2003 that "70 per cent of top ten games are about characters" and that "gamers aren't happy seeing characters sliding along with their shoulder or arm embedded in the wall anymore". Havok 2.0 was re-engineered so it could "run the dynamics of up to ten ragdolls" where its predecessor "struggled with just one at a time", and Remedy was using it for "collision detection and character controllers" in the *Max Payne* sequel.

## In games

*Max Payne 2* arrived at the end of 2003 with ragdolls that "may not match the flying limbs and broken faces of *Soldier of Fortune*", but whose "throwaway approach to life and death is genuinely shocking". A year later two games in the same issue of *Edge* showed where the technique was going. [*Half-Life 2*](/vault/games/half-life-2) was praised as "a game in which physics are more than just an excuse for ragdoll deaths": the gravity gun that could "drag objects, pick them up and carry them about" was "the core tool", and the corpses were incidental. *FlatOut*, "a racing game with physics as a weapon", went the other way and made the ragdoll the entertainment: bonus events that "involve ejecting your drivers through the windscreen at the optimal moment (and angle) to get them to fly as far or as high as possible. Watching them twist and flop through the air is as hilarious a pleasure as it is a guilty one."

## Beyond the ragdoll

By the Game Developers Conference of 2005 the vendors were describing the ragdoll as a starting point. James Golding, "a one-time MathEngine employee who moved to Epic to help with the initial Karma work" and now worked on physically based animation in the Unreal engine, wanted to "'direct' your scene instead of meticulously controlling the movement of every bone": "Our crates now move as good as they look - it's time to start doing the same for our creatures." Havok's Collins put the target more simply ("We've all seen ragdolls being blown up and expect them to be able to get up again"), and the company's newest product was Havok Animation, "physics-based simulation for believable in-game character motion". Seven years after Hecker's interview, the industry was working on his "real hard part".
