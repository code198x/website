Early computers and consoles offered kilobytes, not gigabytes, of memory. Developers created sophisticated techniques to fit games into impossibly small spaces. Bank switching accessed more ROM than addressable. Compression squeezed data smaller. Clever data structures represented complex game states in bytes. These constraints bred creativity that modern developers study for optimisation insight.

## Bank switching

Accessing more than addressable:
- CPU can only address limited memory.
- Switch "banks" of ROM/RAM into view.
- NES mappers enable large games.
- C64 could switch out BASIC/Kernal ROMs.

Example:

- Switch banks as needed during play.

### On the Commodore 64

Commodore's Programmer's Reference Guide poses the problem as a riddle: 64K of RAM, 20K of ROM and a 4K block of I/O "on a computer with a 16-bit address bus, that is normally only capable of addressing 64K". The answer is the 6510's own I/O port at location 1. Its bottom three bits — LORAM, HIRAM and CHAREN — bank the 8K BASIC ROM, the 8K KERNAL ROM and the 4K character generator in and out of the address space, with RAM appearing underneath each. Writes always go to the RAM under a ROM, whatever is banked in, and the guide points out what that is for: "this allows a hi-resolution screen to be kept underneath a ROM, and be changed without having to bank the screen back into the processor address space." A game that switched both ROMs out had the full 64K to itself.

How that 64K was spent was a negotiation, and ZZAP! 64's 1986 interview with Chris Butler records two rounds of it. Elite's *Commando* conversion shipped without its third level because "when Rob Hubbard came down he said that he needed about 6K for the music, and there wasn't room". Planning *Space Harrier*, Butler expected to "fill at least 64K with graphics and then find you've got no memory left", and his answer was to draw the objects as character graphics rather than sprites, "and that should save a hell of a lot of memory".

## Streaming and paging

Loading content dynamically rather than holding it all in RAM:

- **Disk-based games** load sections on demand. *Wing Commander* (1990) streams mission scenes from floppy as the player progresses; *Ultima VII* uses an extensive virtual-memory scheme to swap world chunks. The Amiga's *Pinball Dreams* fits each table on its own disk.
- **Cassette games** were more constrained — once a section was loaded, reloading a previous one meant rewinding. *Lords of Midnight* used a compressed text-graphics approach to fit the entire game in memory rather than streaming.
- **Cartridge bank-switching** is logical streaming without disk I/O — see [Bank Switching](/vault/techniques/bank-switching). The cartridge holds the data; the mapper paging it into the CPU's window is conceptually the same as a disk read.
- **Modern level streaming** in 3D games (*Grand Theft Auto III*'s LoadOnDemand, Unreal Engine's World Composition, Bethesda's cell-loading) is the same idea at gigabyte scale.
