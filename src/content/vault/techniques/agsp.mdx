The Commodore 64's [VIC-II](/vault/hardware/vic-ii) can scroll the screen by 0 to 7 pixels in each direction, and no further. Past that, a game has to move the screen itself: 1,000 bytes of character codes and 1,000 bytes of colour for a text screen, or 8,000 bytes of pixels plus the colours for a bitmap, all before the picture is drawn again. **AGSP**, "Any Given Screen Position", avoids the copy altogether. It persuades the chip to start reading the screen from a different place, so the picture can sit at any position, sideways and up and down, while the data stays where it is.

AGSP is two older tricks working at right angles. The Codebase64 wiki's example puts it in one line: "A VSP ... is used for the horizontal position and a line crunch is used for the vertical position." Christian Bauer, whose 1996 description of the VIC-II is the standard reference, had already seen what the combination would allow: "you can scroll complete graphics screens without using much computing time by almost arbitrarily large distances in all directions."

## How it works

The VIC-II keeps its place in screen memory with a counter that moves on by 40 for each row of characters. It reads each row's character codes and colours on the first raster line of the row, a **badline**, which happens when the bottom three bits of the raster line number match YSCROLL, bits 0 to 2 of `$D011`. AGSP moves the screen by putting that counter out of step on purpose.

- **Across: [VSP](/vault/techniques/vsp).** Writing `$D011` so that a badline starts partway along a raster line, between cycles 15 and 53, makes the chip fetch fewer than 40 characters on that line. Every row after it starts from the misaligned count, so in Bauer's words "the whole screen seems to be scrolled to the right by as much characters as the number of cycles the $d011 access was done after cycle 14". One cycle of delay is one character, eight pixels.
- **Up: [linecrunch](/vault/techniques/linecrunch).** Starting a badline and then taking the condition away "before cycle 14" makes the chip show only the last raster line of a row and then move on to the next row. Each crunched row takes one raster line instead of eight.
- **The last few pixels: fine scroll.** The ordinary scroll bits, bits 0 to 2 of `$D016` for X and of `$D011` for Y, cover the distance between whole characters.

Because the bitmap modes address their pixels through the same counter, the trick moves bitmap pictures as well as text, and because the colour data is read through the counter too, the colours move with the picture. That matters on the C64, whose colour memory at `$D800` is a fixed chip that cannot be switched to a second copy the way screen memory can.

The screen memory becomes a loop. A row pushed off the right-hand edge by VSP reappears at the start of the row below, and the counter wraps back to the start of screen memory when it passes the end. A scrolling game therefore only has to write the new column or row of its map that is about to come into view, not the whole screen.

## What it costs

Both halves are timing tricks, and both have to land on the right cycle every frame.

**A stable raster.** The VSP write must happen on an exact cycle chosen by the horizontal position, so it sits on a [stable raster](/vault/techniques/stable-raster) routine that removes the jitter of a raster interrupt. The Codebase64 example then works out how many cycles to wait from the scroll position and branches into a run of `NOP` instructions at the right point, by writing the branch's own offset into the code: a piece of [self-modifying code](/vault/techniques/self-modifying-code). The example is for PAL machines only. For NTSC, its notes say, "you have to do some minor timing modifications for each scan-line".

**Lines at the top.** Each crunched row still uses one raster line, and those lines pile up at the top of the picture. Bauer suggested hiding them with "one of the invalid graphics modes", which draw black, and the Codebase64 example does this, switching on extended colour and bitmap mode together while it crunches and switching them off at a fixed raster line below. Its comments describe the result as a way to recognise the technique: "if you see any demos with the top few lines blanked out and the screen below is moving at a fast rate then you can probably guess it is using a similar method."

**Seven, not eight.** A crunched row moves the rest of the screen up by seven raster lines, not eight, because the row still shows one line. The Codebase64 article on smooth linecrunching notes this as "a slight bug" in its own example and warns that it matters "if you want to use it as the starting point for an AGSP routine or anything else that requires precise positioning". The vertical fine scroll has to make up the difference.

**The VSP crash.** On a minority of machines, VSP corrupts memory. Linus Åkesson traced the cause to the DRAM in 2013; the [VSP](/vault/techniques/vsp#the-vsp-crash) entry explains the fault and the workarounds. Kodiak64 lists *Fred's Back*, alongside *Creatures* and *Mayhem in Monsterland*, among the programs the fault makes "dangerous software" on affected machines.

## First appearances and games

The Codebase64 wiki's list of demo firsts records the combination under its parts rather than its name: "LineCrunch + VSP", in the demo *Bonanza*, released in November 1988 and credited there to Exilon of Microsystems Digital Technologies and Horizon. That was a year after the first recorded VSP, in October 1987, and sixteen months after the first recorded linecrunch, in July 1987. The list's compilers warn that "some of these issues are subject to dispute".

In games, the name is linked with one series of platformers. The developer Kodiak64 writes that "it is reported" that VSP, combined with linecrunch, "forms part of the AGSP scrolling ... used in the Fred's Back series of platformers"; the third game was reviewed by *Commodore Format* in October 1994. Kodiak64 names *Creatures* and *Mayhem in Monsterland* as users of VSP for their "full-screen, full-colour scrolling" (see [VSP in games](/vault/techniques/vsp#in-games)), without saying that they used the vertical half as well.
