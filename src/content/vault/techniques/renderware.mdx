In early 1994 a room of PC and IT journalists watched a laptop do something they did not believe. It was drawing 3D scenes in real time on an ordinary 486 processor, with no graphics card to help, and *Edge* reported that one of them "was only convinced that RenderWare was for real when he saw the endorsement from Paul Allen, co-founder of Microsoft." RenderWare was a 3D library from Criterion Software, a small company in Guildford owned by the Japanese camera maker Canon. Over the next ten years it went from demos of spinning Mandelbrot sets to the engine underneath *Grand Theft Auto III*, *Tony Hawk's* and *Pro Evolution Soccer*. By 2004 Electronic Arts reckoned Criterion's middleware was in about a quarter of all games in development. Then EA bought the company.

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** [Criterion Software](/vault/companies/criterion), Guildford, a wholly owned subsidiary of Canon.
- **Announced:** early 1994, for Mac, PC and Sun workstations.
- **Speed at launch:** 40,000 polygons a second on a 50MHz 486, and a minimum of a 386SX with 4MB under Windows 3.1.
- **RenderWare3:** rewritten from scratch for the PlayStation 2, Dreamcast and PC generation; in use at more than 170 studios by October 2000.
- **Best-known users:** [DMA Design](/vault/companies/dma-design) for [*Grand Theft Auto III*](/vault/games/gta-iii), [Konami](/vault/companies/konami) for [*Pro Evolution Soccer*](/vault/games/pro-evolution-soccer), and Activision's [*Tony Hawk's*](/vault/games/tony-hawks-pro-skater) games.
- **End:** [Electronic Arts](/vault/companies/electronic-arts) bought Criterion in 2004; other publishers could not get new versions.

## A 3D engine with no 3D hardware

In 1994 serious real-time 3D meant serious money. *Edge* priced it for its readers: a £150,000 dedicated machine for a realistic flight simulator, a £15,000 workstation for most jobs, and £6,000 of hardware at the very least. RenderWare did it in software on the PC a reader already owned. The figure Criterion gave was 40,000 polygons a second on a 50MHz 486DX, measured with a 4,000-polygon model "transformed, lit, clipped, painted, and software double-buffered to the screen in a 400x400 viewport". It predicted 100,000 on a Pentium.

The library was built to be one part of a larger program. "RenderWare is not an application; it's a component, designed to exist within applications," *Edge* explained. "It doesn't take over the machine; in effect it coexists with 2D graphics as a service module, on which the application developer draws." Programmers needed only C and a grasp of RenderWare's scripting language, not a specialist in 3D maths. The same program would run faster on a faster processor in the same family without being rewritten, which *Edge* compared to what PostScript had done for printing. Ports to other platforms would be "easy", according to its creators, and Criterion's business model was a royalty "on every software unit with RenderWare graphics". *Edge* thought that would add "less than a dollar or two" to the price of a game.

The first product to use it was not a game. It was Live, from True-D Software of Oxford, which let users of the mathematics package Mathematica turn their formulae into 3D shapes and spin them in real time. Criterion also showed a "teleshopping" demo, a 3D shopping mall where you could walk around the goods and check their prices.

## Canon's research lab

Criterion came out of Canon's European research. *Edge* told the story in 1996: asked to set up a European research and development centre, David Lau-Kee, then working at Canon Research, founded Criterion Software instead, and was its managing director. His own work had been interactive 2D image processing, which led to 3D image processing and then to 3D graphics. "The games thing is more of a Criterion than a Canon thing," he said. "If you want to see the best use of 2D, 3D, image processing, sound, then it's all in games."

RenderWare had competition from the start. In 1995 *Edge* listed Argonaut's BRender and RenderMorphics' Reality Lab as its rivals; Argonaut claimed BRender needed only 100K of memory. By 1996 *Edge* said RenderWare was used by over 800 developers, and Criterion had opened its own game studio in January of that year to show what it could do. Its underwater adventure *Sub Culture*, published by Ubi Soft in 1997, ran on an upgraded version of the engine.

## RenderWare3

The version that mattered was the one Criterion threw everything away to make. "RenderWare3 doesn't share a single line of code with the previous versions," Adam Billyard, Criterion's technical director, told *Edge* in October 2000. "We threw the whole thing away and started again." *Edge* reported the result was being used "to create games in more than 170 studios worldwide". Interplay and UbiSoft had signed up. ASCII and Jaleco were shipping the first RenderWare PlayStation 2 games in Japan. Konami was using it "in all its Japanese studios, with the notable exception of Hideo Kojima and his Metal Gear Solid 2 team."

What sold it was time. A new console generation was arriving, publishers wanted each game on several machines at once, and some gave developers only nine months to finish a title. Billyard was careful about what cross-platform meant: "It absolutely isn't just press a button and out pops a Dreamcast version." A good game should still play to each machine's strengths: more polygons on the PlayStation 2, perhaps more textures or transparency layers on the Dreamcast.

## Grand Theft Auto III

The best-known RenderWare game was made in a hurry by a studio that had just lost most of its people. [DMA Design](/vault/companies/dma-design) had plenty of 3D experience, from *Body Harvest* and *Wild Metal Country*. But when Take-Two moved the studio from Dundee to Edinburgh, *Edge* reported in its Christmas 2001 middleware feature, "most of the staff left", and "middleware was required to bring the team up to speed". "Waiting 12 months for the development of a game engine and tools wasn't an option." The game was [*Grand Theft Auto III*](/vault/games/gta-iii). Billyard could hardly hide the upside: "if a studio's engine team goes walkies, that's great for us."

RenderWare did not build Liberty City on its own. When *Edge* looked back at the game in 2011 it noted that, though *GTA III* "was built using RenderWare", it was DMA Design's own streaming technology that delivered the city. The series stayed with the engine; in 2010 *Edge* called *GTA*'s loyalty to RenderWare "remarkable given the engine's technical and aesthetic limits", and credited its flexibility with letting *Vice City* follow only a year after *GTA III*.

The same 2001 feature named Konami, Midway and THQ among RenderWare's clients, captioned *Rayman 2* and *Pro Evolution Soccer* as RenderWare games, and found its main rival, NDL's NetImmerse, "more popular in the States".

## From library to platform

RenderWare grew into a suite that Criterion called the RenderWare Platform. In 2002 *Edge* reported version 2.0 of RenderWare Audio for the PlayStation 2, which could stream sound through the console's input/output processor instead of its main Emotion Engine CPU and added Dolby Pro Logic II surround sound to gameplay rather than just cut-scenes. In 2003 Criterion bought the rights to MathEngine's Karma physics and sold it as RenderWare Physics, and RenderWare Studio was the framework that tied the parts together.

## Electronic Arts

In 2004 [Electronic Arts](/vault/companies/electronic-arts) bought Criterion, for what *Edge* later called "a rumoured $100 million". *Edge*'s news pages that October argued the deal "boils down to RenderWare". With the library "locked into the development pipeline of some hugely profitable franchises such as Rockstar's GTA, Activision's Tony Hawk's skating games and Konami's Pro Evolution Soccer, the problem for publishers is that these titles are now dependent on intellectual property owned by their biggest rival." EA's own studios had already used it, on *Madden NFL*.

Both companies promised nothing would change. Criterion's chief executive, David Lau-Kee, said: "We always have, and we always will, provide precisely the same technology internally as we provide externally. That won't change because we are part of EA." Bruce McMillan, head of EA's studios, said that "Criterion's middleware is used in roughly 25 per cent of all games in development", and predicted other developers would "want continued access". The same issue looked forward to "the highly optimised RenderWare 4" on the next generation of consoles.

## What happened next

It did change. Looking back on 2005, *Edge* wrote that rival middleware firms had a "huge opportunity for sales when it became clear EA wouldn't let other developers use new versions of RenderWare." Codemasters had planned to build on it. "We were all lined up for buying Renderware 4 and it obviously got taken away," Gavin Cheshire told *Edge* in 2010, and the company wrote its own engine, first called Neon and later Ego, so that no other publisher could do the same to it again. Rockstar told *Edge* the same year that EA's purchase had pushed it to consolidate its engines into its own technology, which first appeared in *Rockstar Games Presents Table Tennis* in 2007.

Criterion's own games were among the last showcases. *Edge* found *Burnout Revenge* (2005) throwing "the complete RenderWare manual" at the screen, and called its shooter *Black* (2006), like *Revenge*, "a last hurrah for last-gen RenderWare". Frank Gibeau, president of EA's labels, looked back on the episode in 2013 as a mistake EA had learned from before the next console generation: "you may remember the situation with RenderWare? That was kind of EA's Vietnam."

Criterion carried on as an EA studio in Guildford, making *Burnout* and then *Need for Speed* games; the [Guildford games cluster](/vault/culture/guildford-games-cluster) entry follows its later story.
