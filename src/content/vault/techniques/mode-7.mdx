Nintendo's own developer manual introduces it in one sentence: "Mode-7 is a screen, which can rotate, enlarge or reduce." It is one of the eight background modes of the Super NES picture processor, and the only one in which the layer is not drawn straight from its tilemap but through a 2×2 matrix. The SNESdev wiki's description is the modern one: the layer is "effectively a 1024x1024 pixel square" given "an arbitrary 2D affine transformation" — "conceptually similar to texture mapping a single quad on a modern GPU."

## Fast facts

- **Where:** Super NES PPU, background mode 7. One layer (BG1), 256 8×8 characters, a fixed 128×128-character (1024×1024-dot) map.
- **Colours:** 256 of 32,768 per dot; in EXTBG mode, 128 colours with a per-dot priority bit.
- **Matrix:** registers M7A–M7D at `$211B`–`$211E`, each a 16-bit signed value with 8 fractional bits, written as two bytes.
- **Centre:** M7X and M7Y at `$211F`–`$2120`, 13-bit two's complement, inside or outside the visible area.
- **Bonus:** M7A and M7B double as a free 16×8-bit signed multiplier, result read from `$2134`–`$2136`.
- **Per-line change:** H-DMA rewrites the matrix each scanline, which is how a flat rotating map becomes a perspective floor.
- **Name in the press:** "Mode Seven", from Nintendo Power's 1991 explanations of F-Zero and Pilotwings.

## What the layer can do

Chapter 5 of the Book I manual, "Rotation/Enlargement/Reduction", lists the functions plainly. The centre of rotation "can be set at a point either outside or inside of the display area". Rotation angle and horizontal and vertical magnification are independent. The layer can be flipped horizontally and vertically. And because the map is a fixed 1024×1024 dots, the manual has to say what happens past its edge: "one of three choices can be selected" — the backdrop colour, "repetition" (the map wraps), or a single character (number 0) repeated forever. The EXTBG variant, which the manual says "is originally provided as a function for the purpose of the LSI BG expand", trades half the colours for a priority bit per dot, so parts of the rotated layer can sit in front of sprites.

The mode has one background layer only. Priority for it is fixed ("in case of Mode 7, the priority is 0 at BG1"), and sprites are untouched by the matrix — Nintendo Power's 1993 pitch for the Super FX was precisely that "not only can large backgrounds be rotated, as with the Super NES's Mode 7, but even sprites can be manipulated individually".

## The matrix

The manual gives the four parameters as a rotation matrix scaled by the reduction rates:

- A = cos γ × (1/α)
- B = sin γ × (1/α)
- C = −sin γ × (1/β)
- D = cos γ × (1/β)

where γ is the rotation angle and α and β are "Reduction Rates" for X and Y. Each is written to its register as two bytes, low then high: "the value down to a decimal point should be set to the lower 8-bit. The most significant bit of the upper 8-bit is for the signed bit" — an 8.8 fixed-point number. The SNESdev wiki reads the same four numbers as texture-mapping vectors: (M7A, M7C) and (M7B, M7D) are the steps through the map for one pixel to the right and one pixel down, and "(cos ϴ, sin ϴ) (-sin ϴ, cos ϴ) form a standard rotation matrix". The centre coordinates X₀ and Y₀ are 13-bit two's complement.

## The free multiplier

Working out sines and cosines on a 65816 is slow, and the PPU offers help. Chapter 10, "Complementary Multiplication", explains that "the 2's complement multiplication will be performed with high speed. For example, to calculate the rotation parameter in mode 7, it will lighten the burden of the CPU processing." Writing a 16-bit value to M7A and an 8-bit value to M7B gives a signed 24-bit product "with 'no-wait'", readable from `$2134`–`$2136` — a multiplier that exists because mode 7 needed one, and that programs use whether or not they are in mode 7.

## Per scanline: the perspective trick

A single matrix rotates and zooms a flat plane; it cannot tilt it. Perspective comes from H-DMA, the manual's "special DMA which can transfer data automatically, synchronizing with the H-Blank", by which "the S-PPU settings can be varied by each horizontal scan line". Feed it a table of matrix values that shrink the map more for each line up the screen and the plane recedes into the distance. The SNESdev wiki: "the affine transformation can be changed every scanline via HDMA, allowing versatile perspective and distortion effects." The manual also records where the work is done: "PPU1 is used to generate background character data, rotation, and scaling; while PPU2 performs special effects like windows, mosaic, and fades."

## "Mode Seven" in 1991

The name reached players through Nintendo Power. In June 1991 a preview said Asmik's Dimension Force "utilizes the Super NES 'Mode Seven,' which allows for realistic three dimensional scrolling". The next issue's feature on the new console's capabilities explained the two halves. Rotation: "one of the programs that most effectively uses Mode Seven Rotation is F-Zero, a futuristic driving game. The effect is nothing short of dramatic when the player-controlled car crashes and the 'camera' view moves forward briefly, then rotates 180 degrees to show the burning wreckage." Scaling, contrasted with the old way of drawing an object "at several different sizes and then present the pictures of the object one at a time like the frames in a movie": "With Mode Seven Scaling, though, an object is drawn only once and then manipulated by the Super NES to grow or shrink as it moves in relation to the 'camera' view. Pilotwings, a flight simulator, uses scaling to show approaching targets."

By October the magazine was pointing it out level by level — Super Ghouls 'N Ghosts' cave, where "the entire background rotates when you jump on the platforms ... You may very well forget which way is up!" Developers' opinions of the rest of the machine were more mixed. Neil Jackson of [Argonaut](/vault/companies/argonaut) wrote to Super Play in 1993 that the Super NES had "Mode 7, which is brilliant", but that on "the down side, it's actually slower than a Spectrum, and stupider than a C64" — the gap the [Super FX](/vault/techniques/super-fx-chip) was built to close.
