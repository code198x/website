Base building is the half of a real-time strategy game that is not fighting: laying down structures that gather a resource, turn it into units, and defend the plot they stand on. The reviewers who watched the form arrive described it in the same terms each time — *[Sim City](/vault/games/sim-city)* with a war attached — and by 1998 *Computer Gaming World* was calling the whole genre "base-building" games.

## Fast facts

- **First described:** *[Dune II](/vault/games/dune-ii)* ([Westwood](/vault/companies/westwood-studios), 1992), previewed by *Computer Gaming World* as "Sim City In A Sandbox".
- **Before it:** *Herzog Zwei* ([Techno Soft](/vault/companies/technosoft), [Mega Drive](/vault/systems/sega-mega-drive), 1990) had bases and outposts but read to *The Games Machine* as "really a multi-direction shoot-'em-up with one or two tactical possibilities".
- **The template restated:** *[Warcraft](/vault/games/warcraft)* (1994) and *[Command & Conquer](/vault/games/command-and-conquer)* (1995) were both reviewed as *Dune II* with the nouns changed.
- **Where it went:** *[Total Annihilation](/vault/games/total-annihilation)* (1997) added metal and energy and a 3D terrain; *[StarCraft](/vault/games/starcraft)* (1998) gave its three races different rules for where they may build.

## Herzog Zwei

*The Games Machine* reviewed Techno Soft's *Herzog Zwei* in March 1990 as "an unusual product for the 16-bit Sega console: a strategy game." The description has most of the later pieces: "Both sides have a base to work plus a number of outposts, depending on the type of game selected. As in many games, the aim is simply to capture the enemy flags before he takes yours, but in Herzog Zwei your sprite is a Transformer-style robot that can become a jet aircraft. Other forces can be despatched from your bases to defend from attack." The reviewer did not think it a strategy game at heart — "it has to be stated that it's really a multi-direction shoot-'em-up with one or two tactical possibilities" — and gave it 75%.

## Dune II

*Computer Gaming World* previewed a beta of *Dune II* in December 1992 under the headline "Sim City In A Sandbox", and Chuck Moss's description is the earliest account held here of base building as a mechanic:

> Dune marries the development/financial management dynamic of Sim City to the tactical battle challenge of The Perfect General. Players build their bases by pouring concrete (a lot of concrete gets poured. So much that one begins to feel like an old Soviet Empire techno-commissar), and constructing spice refineries, windtrap power stations, storage silos and various military installations. Development is handled as in Sim City, with building choices made on a separate screen menu, paid for in credits, and placed via mouse.

Each scenario starts the player with "a construction base, a small military force and a starting amount of monetary credits", and the loop is "a tense grope in the dark to find and harvest the spice, a frantic race to build, and a wild melee of shooting, burning, exploding units". Moss's bottom line: "It is strictly a build, fight, and make money game with a Dune hook".

*Amiga Format*'s review of the Amiga port in September 1993 set the dependency out as a rule — "To fight battles and mine Melange, you must have troops and vehicles. To build vehicles and train troops, you must build structures. Take one or more concrete slabs, and add one of the following" — and its unit list names the piece later games made central: the "Mobile Construction Vehicle — A scout vehicle transforms itself at will into a fully-functional construction yard."

## Warcraft and Command & Conquer

The two games that fixed the pattern were both reviewed as copies. Chris Lombardi's *Computer Gaming World* review of [Blizzard](/vault/companies/blizzard)'s *Warcraft: Orcs & Humans* in January 1995 said so directly: "Replace the overhead graphics of orcs and humans with Atreides and Harkonnen, substitute Warcraft's resources (lumber and gold) with Spice, and rename the various buildings that you must construct in a simplified Sim City style, and you have Westwood Studio's Dune 2." What *Warcraft* changed was who does the building. "Starting with the barest makings of a town (usually a town hall and a few farms), you must build up your plot of land into a well-oiled war machine capable of out-producing and out-researching the opponent. From an overhead perspective, you build roads, place buildings, and muster new units, all financed by peasants who must seek out timber from forests and gold from mines." The peasants are the bottleneck: "to support new peasants, you've got to have farms, which must be built by the same peasants you just ordered to the mines."

*Edge* gave Westwood's own *Command & Conquer* eight out of ten in October 1995 while calling it "an only slightly improved version of a previous Westwood Studio game, the excellent Dune 2, which appeared over three years ago": "Those familiar with Dune 2 will remember constructing factories and sending troops off into uncharted enemy terrain to fight and capture buildings, while harvester units mined spice for money and returned it to base in order to finance further expansion. Substitute spice for Tiberium and you have Command & Conquer." The sidebar build panel was already the fixture the genre kept: "Buildings appear in the left-hand column and available troops in the right-hand one".

## Total Annihilation and StarCraft

By January 1998 Martin Cirulis could open his *Computer Gaming World* review of [Cavedog](/vault/companies/cavedog-entertainment)'s *Total Annihilation* with "After playing endless variations on the Command & Conquer theme" and summarise the form in a sentence: "Gather a couple of resources (in this case metal and energy), build a base, and create enough units to kick your opponent's butt all over the high-res terrain." He called it a "base-building game" — the phrase in use as a genre label — and rated its variety of land, sea and air units against Interplay's *M.A.X.*

Robert Coffey's review of *StarCraft* in July 1998 records the next refinement, making the building rules themselves differ by side: "Terrans split the difference in cost and effectiveness. But, they can build anywhere, unlike the Zerg, who must build on their own excreted slime, or the Protoss, who must build and maintain an energy grid."
