## Overview

The **Guild of Software Houses** — **GOSH**, and the acronym was deliberate —
was the British games industry's first attempt to regulate itself. It was set up
in 1983, in *Computer & Video Games*' words, "in response to public demand for a
little more discipline from the software houses".

The founding members were the labels that had been in the business longest:
**[Bug-Byte](/vault/companies/bug-byte)**, **New Generation**,
**[Quicksilva](/vault/companies/quicksilva)**, **Salamander**, **Silversoft**,
**Softek** and **Virgin Games**. By late 1984 it had **34 members**.

## The Customers' Charter

The Charter is the document, and it is worth reading as a list of what the
industry knew it was doing wrong. Members undertook to:

1. Publish their terms and conditions of trade on request.
2. **Maintain promised mail-order delivery times.**
3. **Replace defective stock**, and deal with complaints promptly — normally
   within **five working days**.
4. **Avoid advertising products as available before this is in fact the case.**
5. Keep customers informed where genuinely unforeseen difficulties occurred.
6. Guarantee high standards of business integrity.

A parallel **Dealers' Charter** added delivery-date guarantees to shops and a
commitment to "a high technical standard of quality".

Clause 4 is the one that dates the period exactly. In **November 1983**, the
British software industry collectively agreed in print that advertising things
that did not yet exist was the practice most in need of stopping. Two months
later [Sinclair Research](/vault/companies/sinclair-research) announced the
[QL](/vault/systems/sinclair-ql) and took **£5 million against 13,000 orders it
could not fill**, and the Advertising Standards Authority ended up criticising the
company. Sinclair was a hardware manufacturer and not a GOSH member, so it broke
no code it had signed — but the industry had named the disease in the same season
that its most famous case broke out.

## Piracy, and a fighting fund

GOSH's more visible work was against copying, and the numbers its members gave the
press are worth recording precisely because they cannot all be right.

**Nick Alexander** of Virgin Games, who chaired the Guild, told *Acorn User* in
1984: "For every legitimate tape sold, **10 or 12 copies are made**." Chris
Holland of Salamander put it "nearer six", but added that "between **£100m and
£150m** is lost in this country alone each year". Alexander also attributed "50 to
60 per cent" of Imagine's July 1984 collapse to copying, and cited a Salford CID
raid that seized **10,000 counterfeit copies of Imagine games** at once.

To back that up, GOSH raised a **£50,000 fund** from its members so that
individual companies could take pirates to court — money that went, among other
places, to support Artic's action against an alleged pirate in Hull.

Two more things it did are worth keeping, because they show a trade body behaving
like one:

- It **objected to the Ministry of Defence** placing a secrecy order on J.L.C.
  Data's newly developed software protection system in 1984. The MoD classifying a
  copy-protection scheme, and the games industry publicly protesting, is not a
  sentence anyone would predict.
- It **counted the industry**. In December 1984 GOSH estimated that "more than
  **500** companies dotted the software map just one year ago. This year the figure
  is more like **350**, and it's likely to slip even further in 1985."

That last figure is the most useful thing GOSH produced. The British software
boom is usually described from the inside, by the people who survived it. GOSH was
in a position to count the ones who did not, and reckoned a third of them had gone
inside twelve months.

## The end

GOSH did not survive the 8-bit era it was formed to police. By May 1988 *ACE* was
reporting an industry meeting in a Jersey hotel — Elite, Hewson and Electronic
Arts among those present — calling for a new publishers' association, and
explaining why: "**since the demise of GOSH there has been no effective
representative body**" for games companies.

## Why it matters here

Self-regulation is one of the few places where an industry writes down its own
faults in its own words. The Customers' Charter is a better primary source on what
buying British software in 1983 was like than any retrospective: people
were waiting too long for post, receiving tapes that would not load, getting no
reply when they complained, and being sold things that had not been finished.

Every one of those is a clause. And the fact that the guild that wrote them was
gone within five years, leaving nothing behind it, says something too.
