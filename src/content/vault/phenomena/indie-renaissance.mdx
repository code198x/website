In 2010 Mike Bithell was a designer at Blitz Games, one of a big team, and he was watching other people's games. "I'd been in the game industry for about three years," he told *Edge*. "I was a guy making stuff in a big team, and that story of one or two people making something on their own was very attractive." He went home and, in his evenings and weekends, taught himself to program. The result, *Thomas Was Alone*, a platform game about a group of coloured rectangles, came out in 2012.

Bithell had seen what *Edge* called "the explosion that was happening in the indie scene". For a few years from 2008, games made by one, two or a handful of people sold to hundreds of thousands of players, won awards and were written about as seriously as the work of big studios. *Wireframe*, looking back on the decade, dated it from *Braid*, the 2008 game that "helped pave the way for the indie renaissance". This entry covers those years. The longer history of independent games, back to the mail-order programmers of the early 1980s, is in [Indie games](/vault/culture/indie-games).

## Fast facts

- **When:** from about 2008 to the middle of the 2010s.
- **Where the games were sold:** Xbox Live Arcade and its cheaper sibling Xbox Live Indie Games, [Steam](/vault/distribution/steam), Apple's App Stores, and later Sony's PlayStation stores.
- **Tools:** [Game Maker](/vault/tools/game-maker), and [Unity](/vault/tools/unity), free from October 2009.
- **Money:** download sales, pay-what-you-want bundles and, from 2012, crowdfunding on Kickstarter.
- **Games of the time include:** *Braid* (2008), *Spelunky* (2008), *Super Meat Boy* (2010), *Limbo* (2010), *[Minecraft](/vault/games/minecraft)* (finished 2011), *Fez*, *[Hotline Miami](/vault/games/hotline-miami)* (2012), *Thomas Was Alone* (2012), *[Shovel Knight](/vault/games/shovel-knight)* (2014).

## Games without a box

Download services made the difference. Microsoft's Xbox Live Arcade, Valve's Steam and Apple's App Store sold small games with no disc, box or shop, and a game made by two people could sit on the same menu as one made by two hundred. Jonathan Blow's *Braid*, a puzzle-platform game about rewinding time, was an Xbox Live Arcade hit in 2008. Playdead's *Limbo*, a monochrome platform game in which a small boy dies again and again in traps and is chased by a giant spider, was "a widely lauded title that helped to cement the value of downloadable games on console platforms", in *Edge*'s words. *Super Meat Boy*, the work of two people, Tommy Refenes and Edmund McMillen, sold more than a million copies in less than two years; *Wireframe* called its success on Xbox Live Arcade the moment that "crystallised a new era of small, independently made games that could find a mass audience".

Getting onto those stores was not easy. "Steam is the Holy Land for indies," the designer Randy Smith wrote in *Edge* in 2012. "It's notoriously hard to get into, but epically rewarding if you do." Microsoft's cheaper Xbox Live Indie Games service was built for hobbyists. James Silva of Ska Studios told *Edge* in 2010 that he was "completely charmed by the concept of an official platform for bedroom coders to be able to put games on a console", but the magazine found that games on all the smaller download services struggled to be noticed. Valve opened its doors a little in August 2012 with Steam Greenlight, which let players vote on which games Steam should sell, for a one-off fee of $100. Smith described it as combining "the unpoliced opportunity of Apple's App Store with the democracy of Kickstarter".

## Tools anyone could afford

The new games were cheap to make as well as to sell. Derek Yu made the first *[Spelunky](/vault/games/spelunky)*, released free in December 2008, with Game Maker. Unity made its basic version free in October 2009; two students told *Edge* in 2011 that their prize-winning game *GLiD* had started "with Unity, because I found out it was free". Bithell built *Thomas Was Alone* in Unity too, and his folder for it, he said, was still called "Teaching Myself Unity".

## The developers become the story

The developers themselves became famous. James Swirsky and Lisanne Pajot's documentary *Indie Game: The Movie*, paid for on Kickstarter, followed Team Meat finishing *Super Meat Boy* and Polytron showing *Fez* in public for the first time in four years, with Jonathan Blow talking about *Braid*. It won an editing award at the Sundance Film Festival in January 2012, and HBO and the producer Scott Rudin took it up to adapt for television. "These are extreme stories," the film-makers told *Edge*. "A lot of indie games get made without the drama that you see in this film."

By 2013 the console makers were competing for these developers. Sony made a point of inviting them onto its PlayStation platforms. "I think indies are moving to Sony's platforms for one simple reason: because they asked us," Bithell told *Edge*. Shahid Ahmad, whose small team in London had signed *Hotline Miami* and *Spelunky* for the PlayStation Vita, said his biggest success had been getting the industry, "particularly the indie community", "so firmly behind PlayStation".

## Players as backers

In February 2012 Tim Schafer's Double Fine asked players on Kickstarter for $400,000 to make a point-and-click adventure. By the end of the campaign 87,142 backers had pledged $3,336,371. *Edge* called crowdfunding "gaming's latest gold rush", and others followed. Yacht Club Games asked for $75,000 in 2013 and raised $311,502 for *Shovel Knight*, a new game in the style of a NES platformer. In 2015 Koji Igarashi, the former producer of Konami's *Castlevania* games, raised $5,545,991 for *[Bloodstained](/vault/games/bloodstained)*.

## An echo of the 1980s

The comparison with the home computer years was hard to miss. Like the [bedroom coders](/vault/phenomena/bedroom-coder) of the early 1980s, the new developers were often one or two people, selling directly to players, and many of their games went back to old forms: *Spelunky* to the [roguelike](/vault/genres/roguelike), *Shovel Knight* to the 8-bit platform game. One company spanned both eras. [Team17](/vault/companies/team17), which grew out of a public domain software library in the Amiga years, went back to publishing other developers' games in 2013, and by 2019 *Wireframe* was calling it "a British indie publishing powerhouse".

## Too many games

Success brought crowding. Valve replaced Greenlight with Steam Direct in 2017, letting any developer put a game on Steam for a fee of $100. In 2019 Haley Uyrus, marketing manager at Mediatonic, wrote in *Wireframe* that indies faced a store where "over 200 games release each week on Steam alone", while big publishers' games were filling the streamers' schedules and the quiet release months that small games had relied on. "Is this the dreaded 'indiepocalypse' everyone's been talking about?" she asked. Her answer was more marketing. Independent games had become a permanent part of the industry, and the hard part was no longer getting a game into a shop but getting anyone to notice it there.
