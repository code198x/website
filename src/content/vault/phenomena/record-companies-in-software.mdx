In February 1984 *Sinclair User* reported that Shakin' Stevens fans who bought his album *The Bop Won't Stop* on cassette would find a bonus on the tape: a game for the 48K Spectrum in which you drove a car into "Shaky's 'ole house" while dodging vampire bats. The record company, CBS, said it had chosen the Spectrum because it was the most popular home computer in Britain. That autumn The Stranglers went further, putting an adventure game, *Aural Quest*, at the end of the cassette of *Aural Sculpture*. For a few years the British record industry treated home computer software as part of its business, and several of its companies set up software labels of their own.

## Record sales down, software up

*Your Computer* explained the attraction in June 1983. "In the palaces of the pop industry," its editorial began, "even the razzmatazz cannot hide the fact that sales of records have been in decline since peaking in the late seventies." Meanwhile one estimate put the British market for Spectrum software alone at £1 million a month:

> Given these two contrasting tales of gloom and boom you need not be an industry pundit to grasp why major record companies such as Virgin and K-tel are moving into the lucrative business of selling software. Virgin Records has launched Virgin Games, and K-tel attended the Midland Computer Fair with £1 million in its pocket to negotiate deals with the software makers.

The magazine then guessed what might follow: "can we expect to see national software charts — and the hyping that goes with them — computer programmers as stars". It expected the first effect to be duller. Most software was then sold by mail order, and "the music companies have the distribution networks to put software in the shops". That, it warned, could spell the end of the mail-order business and raise the price of entry for small publishers.

A year later the *Sinclair User Annual* counted the newcomers in passing: "Other companies from the record and publishing world — Thorn-EMI, CBS, Virgin — attracted by the large potential market, have also joined the contest."

### Virgin

Virgin Games was started in 1983 by Nick Alexander, who had worked for EMI and HMV and then in Thorn EMI's home computer software division, with Richard Branson's backing. Its first tapes were sold like records. According to Alexander, each came with a photograph and biography of its young author on the inlay and music by Steve Hillage or another Virgin act on the other side of the cassette. From 1985 Virgin sold compilations called *Now Games*, named after the *Now That's What I Call Music* albums. See [Virgin Games](/vault/companies/virgin-games).

### K-tel

K-tel, "renowned for its TV-advertised compilation albums", arrived with money and vans. *Your Computer* reported in June 1983 that its first deal was a £150,000 order for software from DK'tronics, and that its software manager, Adrian Moulds, preferred partnership deals to buying copyrights. The programmers kept the rights; K-tel distributed the tapes "through the vans that service the 5,000 outlets for its record business". Its own games came as "Doublesiders", advertised in *Computer and Video Games* in January 1984 at £6.95: "When you've finished playing one side simply flip it over (just like a music cassette) and move off on a second totally different, equally gripping game." By March the range had six tapes, among them *It's Only Rock 'n' Roll* and *Dracula's Tomb*.

### Thorn EMI and Creative Sparks

Thorn EMI, the electronics and entertainment group that owned EMI, was publishing games as Thorn EMI Video in 1983, when *Personal Computer News* reviewed its *Orc Attack* cartridge for the Atari. In spring 1984 the games took a new name. "Creative Sparks is a new software company in name at least," *CRASH* wrote in May 1984, "in fact it's really Thorn EMI with a new front." Its games included *Orc Attack* on the Spectrum, which *CRASH* made a Smash, and a run of *Danger Mouse* games. A separate business, Thorn EMI Computer Software Distributors, handled American labels such as HesWare. The label did not last. By the spring of 1986 Mastertronic was reissuing games "bought from Creative Sparks" at budget prices, and in October 1987 *CRASH* summed up its "roller-coaster existence": it had ended as Creative Sparks Distribution, "which recently went into receivership".

### CBS

CBS was already in the trade by early 1984, when *Your Computer* reported that [Bug-Byte](/vault/companies/bug-byte)'s British distribution had moved to CBS Records in London. After the Shakin' Stevens tape came The Stranglers' *Aural Quest*, a 48K Spectrum adventure written with [The Quill](/vault/tools/the-quill) by Mike Turner of Star Dreams, from a plot by the band's keyboard player Dave Greenfield. *Computer and Video Games* asked why Stranglers fans got an adventure rather than an arcade game. "For Shakin' Stevens fans, an arcade game was right," Turner said. "Stranglers' fans are thinking people." In the winter of 1984–85 CBS Software began selling Commodore 64 games under its own name, starting, *Your Commodore* said, "rather modestly", with a few titles such as Epyx's *Breakdance*. Its Brian Hyams admitted that Epyx's *Summer Games* had gone to Quicksilva instead because CBS Software "was not ready at the time".

### Ariolasoft

Ariolasoft came out of CBS. Its managing director Ashley Grey and its marketing man Frank Brunger had worked on "the embryonic CBS software, with such memorable exploits as a Shakin' Stevens computer game", *Your Computer* reported in 1986, before Bertelsmann, whose interests ran "from Ariola records to Luxembourg Radio and magazine publishing", approached them. Ariolasoft was formed in September 1984. It launched at the Hippodrome in London's West End with twelve American games from [Electronic Arts](/vault/companies/electronic-arts) and [Broderbund](/vault/companies/broderbund), and *Your Commodore* reported the arrival of CBS and Ariolasoft under the headline "Off the record". When Electronic Arts set up on its own in Britain in 1987, Ariolasoft's British business went into decline. See [Ariolasoft](/vault/companies/ariolasoft).

## Record-company methods

The newcomers said openly that they were running software like records. "We have attempted to ally quite closely to the record industry, in the packaging, advertising etc., because it very much suits the market," Brunger told *Your Commodore* in March 1985. "The days of the cottage industry are passed," he said, and Grey agreed: "There has to be a new professionalism." The magazine noted that, "like all good record companies", Ariolasoft and CBS Software were "not just chasing the hits but building a broadly based catalogue". A year later Brunger was still saying it. He wanted Ariolasoft to be "a catalogue-based company, like CBS and EMI": "We don't budget for big hits. You will never get a number one with a Country and Western record, yet they are still released." He also called software "the worst, most unprofessional industry I have ever encountered".

## Gallup's chart

The software chart the trade settled on came from the music business's own pollster. In the same March 1985 interview, *Your Commodore* reported that the Ariolasoft men expected one chart to become the standard, and that "the Gallup one which appears in *Your Commodore* for the first time this month is the one that gets his vote". Gallup's software chart was compiled for the trade paper *Computer and Software Retailing*, and *Sinclair User*, which printed it too, said in June 1985 that Gallup sampled sales "at 250 retail outlets, including high street chain stores and independent computer shops". Gallup already compiled the music charts; *Your Sinclair* was still reminding readers in 1991 that it was "the folk who do the BBC music charts amongst other things". Before Gallup, *Computer and Video Games* had commissioned its own games chart from the pollsters NOP, jointly with the *Daily Mirror*. See [Reading the charts](/vault/culture/reading-the-charts).

## Most of them left

The market did not reward them for long. At the start of 1984 shops that had over-ordered for Christmas stopped buying, and for a few months that year most of Virgin Games' staff were lent to Branson's new airline, Virgin Atlantic. Looking back at the end of 1984, *CRASH* described big business moving in "with names like Thorn EMI (Creative Sparks) who, like Virgin and K-Tel made a reassessment of what they were doing earlier this year". K-tel's name faded from the software press, Creative Sparks ended in receivership, and Ariolasoft's British arm lost its American catalogue in 1987.

Virgin was the exception. It bought Leisure Genius in 1986, bought 45% of [Mastertronic](/vault/companies/mastertronic) in 1987 and the rest in 1988, and as Virgin Mastertronic took on Sega's distribution in Britain, France and Germany. It went on to publish *Aladdin* and *Cannon Fodder* and to own Westwood Studios, and the Virgin name stayed on games until 2009.

## It ran both ways

The traffic was not all from music into games. In April 1987 *CRASH*, listing how far Mastertronic had spread, counted "all the Mastertronic labels, Bulldog, the new Mastersounds record label and Mastervision videos, and the Arcadia arcade machine specialists". A year later Virgin, the record company that had gone into games, owned Mastertronic outright.
