Between 1983 and 1985 the British record industry decided that software was its business, and for about three years it was right.

**Virgin Records** launched **Virgin Games**. **Thorn EMI** launched **Creative Sparks**. **K-Tel**, the television-advertised compilation-album company, arrived at the Midland Computer Fair with a million pounds to spend. **Ariola** — the German record group behind Arista — launched **Ariolasoft**, at the Hippodrome in London's West End, and *Your Commodore* filed the story under the headline "Off the record".

By 1989 all of them had gone except Virgin.

## The press saw it coming

*Your Computer* set the whole thing out in **June 1983**, before most of it had happened:

> You need not be an industry pundit to grasp why **major record companies such as Virgin and K-tel are moving into the lucrative business of selling software**. Virgin Records has launched Virgin Games, and K-tel attended the Midland Computer Fair with **£1 million in its pocket** to negotiate deals with the software makers.

And then, having named the cause, it guessed the consequences:

> Now that some of the **music industry's marketing and distribution power has been turned on the software business**, can we expect to see **national software charts — and the hyping that goes with them — computer programmers as stars** […]

All three arrived. Britain got national software charts, it got chart hyping, and it got programmers as stars — [Matthew Smith](/vault/people/matthew-smith), [Jeff Minter](/vault/people/jeff-minter), [Andrew Braybrook](/vault/people/andrew-braybrook), [Rob Hubbard](/vault/people/rob-hubbard) — with the press machinery to sustain them. The forecast was not a lucky guess. It was a description of what happens when an industry is annexed by one that already knows how to do those things.

By the following year the category was established enough that the *Sinclair User Annual* could list its members in passing: "other companies from the **record and publishing world** — Thorn-EMI, CBS, Virgin — attracted by the large potential market, have also joined the contest."

## Who they were

| Label | Parent | British trade presence |
|---|---|---|
| **Virgin Games** | Virgin Records | 1983 onward — never left |
| **Creative Sparks** | Thorn EMI | 1984–86, "best known for the Dangermouse titles" |
| **K-Tel** | K-Tel (compilation albums) | 1983–85 |
| **Ariolasoft** | Ariola / Arista | 1985–88 |

*Sinclair Programs* described Creative Sparks in May 1985 as "**Thorn EMI's contender in the software publishing market**. Started in April 1984, they have already produced forty two games." Ariolasoft's opening catalogue was, per *Personal Computer Games*, "their first 12 games… all for the Commodore 64", licensed from **Broderbund** and **[Electronic Arts](/vault/companies/electronic-arts)** — which is how a British buyer of *M.U.L.E.* or *Pinball Construction Set* came to be buying from a record label. See [Ariolasoft](/vault/companies/ariolasoft).

## The shared plumbing

The crossover was not a metaphor. The two trades ran on the same equipment.

Both sold **cassettes**, duplicated by the same kind of plant, racked in the same shops — Woolworths, W.H. Smith, the newsagent counter — at prices set by the same impulse-purchase logic. And both were measured by the same people: when the British software trade settled on a single standard chart, it settled on **Gallup's**, the firm that compiled the record charts. *Big K* introduced them to readers in March 1985 as "the Gallup (**those lovely chart people**) software chart".

That is the institutional link under everything else on this page, and it outlasted the record companies themselves. See [Reading the charts](/vault/culture/reading-the-charts).

## It ran both ways

Games companies went the other way at the same time. *CRASH* in April 1987, cataloguing how far [Mastertronic](/vault/companies/mastertronic) had spread, lists "all the Mastertronic labels, Bulldog, the new **Mastersounds** record label and **Mastervision** videos, and the **Arcadia** arcade machine specialists". A budget games publisher started a record label in the same decade that record labels started games publishers.

## What it left

The record companies mostly left. What they brought did not.

The **album-format packaging** and the language of games as authored works; the **chart** as the measure of a game's life; the **hype cycle** around a release date; the **named creator** as a commercial asset. British games publishing learned these from the record business between 1983 and 1986, and kept them after the record business went home.

⚠ This entry measures what the British press printed, not what the companies did. A label can trade quietly and a magazine can lose interest; the counts above track trade attention rather than turnover or catalogue.
