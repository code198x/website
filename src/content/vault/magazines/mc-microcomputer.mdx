**MCmicrocomputer** (usually just *MC*) was one of Italy's longest-running computer magazines: a monthly from Rome that ran for twenty years, from September 1981 to June 2001. It began with a first issue that tested an Atari 800 and a Corvus hard disk, and it ended with a special on digital cameras. Along the way it covered the arrival of the [Commodore 64](/vault/systems/commodore-64) in Italy, opened its own online service, and outlived its first publisher.

## Seventy days from desks to newsstand

*MC* was the work of **Paolo Nuti**, its *direttore*, and **Marco Marinacci**, his *condirettore*. They had worked together before. In September 1979 Nuti had started *Micro & Personal Computer* as a supplement to the hi-fi magazine *Suono*, from the Gruppo Editoriale Suono, with Marinacci among its contributors. Nuti's editorial in *MC*'s first issue wondered whether to treat it as a first issue at all, or as "the continuation of work begun two years ago".

The new publisher, **Technimedia**, was founded on 21 May 1981 in Rome, at Via Valsolda 135. It launched two magazines at once: *MCmicrocomputer* and the hi-fi title *AUDIOreview*, which went on sale beside it on 3 September. Issue 1, dated September 1981, cost 3,000 lire.

Nuti's first editorial, "In caso di bisogno fate un fischio" ("If you need us, whistle"), is dated by its own story. On Saturday 1 August, with his windsurfing kit packed, he telephoned Marinacci to read him the editorial ("you never know, this co-director has something to say about everything"), left it on Giovanna Molinari's desk and went off for a week of rest after "two months and ten days of frantic work". Seventy days earlier, he wrote, "we had not yet ordered the desks". The magazine's purpose, as he put it, was to carry on "demystifying white-coat computing" and bringing computing within reach of "everyone who knows how to use their brain".

The first issue tested an Atari 800, a Watanabe plotter with ten pens, a 5-megabyte Corvus hard disk and a Texas Instruments RPN simulator, and carried a special report from Tokyo. Its contributors included Bo Arnklit, credited with *ricerca e sviluppo* (research and development), and Corrado Giustozzi, who would stay with the magazine for years.

## Watching the home computers arrive

*MC* reported on business machines and home computers alike, and its news pages give an Italian view of the early 1980s. In May 1982 it reported that Commodore's Italian company had become fully operational on 12 May, and in December 1982 it printed its first look at the Commodore 64, which had been shown in Italy for the first time at the SMAU exhibition in Milan, "not working, behind a glass case". Five years later Nuti could write in his end-of-year editorial of "the million C64s sold in Italy", and of a boom in cheap home PCs that he could compare only to "the boom of the Commodore 64". The [C64 across borders](/vault/culture/the-c64-across-borders) entry follows that story.

The magazine grew with the market. The cover price rose to 5,000 lire by 1987, 6,000 by 1988 and 9,000 by 1996. By the late 1980s its masthead listed a large team of contributors, among them Andrea de Prisco, Raffaello De Masi, Valter Di Dio and Massimo Truscelli.

## MC-Link

In September 1986 *MC* went online. Its issue for that month announced, with "trumpet blasts and drum rolls", that from 1 September **MC-Link** had begun its official trial: "a public and free telematic service created within MCmicrocomputer as an interactive extension of the magazine". Anyone with a modem and a communications program could dial in, 24 hours a day, to send and receive messages, take part in conferences, read small ads and consult the indexes of *MC* and *AUDIOreview*, and download the listings of the programs printed in the magazine. Corrado Giustozzi explained the service to readers in a feature that month. By 1994 Marinacci was signing letters as MC-Link's *direttore responsabile*, and the service later became an internet service provider and a company in its own right.

## Pluricom and the end

At the end of the 1990s Technimedia got into trouble. In February 1999 *MC* printed a notice from the company's liquidator, Filippo Varazi: the restructuring of "Technimedia srl in liquidation" was nearly over, and from the following month the magazine's publisher would be the newly formed Pluricom srl. From the March 1999 issue *MC* was published by Pluricom, at Viale Ettore Franceschini 73 in Rome. Marinacci wrote the editorials, as he had since June 1998, and Nuti still contributed articles.

The last issue, number 218, is dated June 2001. It gives no sign that it is the last: Marinacci's editorial is about giving up the darkroom for a digital camera, and ends by advising readers to buy one. According to later accounts, Pluricom ceased trading that summer, and a 219th issue for July and August 2001 was laid out but never printed for lack of money.
