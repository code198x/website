**Atari Club Magazin** was the magazine of Atari's members' club in West Germany.
This library holds seven issues, from **1982** to **1984** — small things, seven
to twenty pages, addressed to *liebe Clubmitglieder* and written from what they
call the *Clubzentrale*.

It is the earliest German title in this library, and the second manufacturer's
house organ in this Vault after
*[Club Nintendo](/vault/magazines/club-nintendo)* — which it precedes by seven
years.

## Selling E.T. in December 1982

The undated 1982 issue gives most of a page to **E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial**,
cartridge **CX 2674**, with Spielberg's earlier films listed in German to
establish the pedigree and the film called the most successful in cinema history,
having broken every American record in a matter of weeks. A December 1982 date is
given for the cassette.

The value of that page is entirely in when it was written. This Vault already
records the American side of the same product: Atari paid twenty to twenty-five
million dollars for the licence in **July 1982**, gave
[Howard Scott Warshaw](/vault/people/howard-scott-warshaw) five weeks to finish
it by 1 September for Christmas manufacturing, and the result became the emblem
of the [1983 crash](/vault/phenomena/1983-crash).

Here is the same cartridge, a few months later, being sold to German club members
as an event. Nothing in the page suggests the writer knew anything was wrong. A
house organ is a document of what a company was saying at a date, and that is the
whole of its value as evidence.

⚠ The scan of that page is column-interleaved and the day of the December date
does not survive legibly. Month and year only.

## Research that happened to help

The January 1983 issue passes on findings from *das BAT-Forschungsinstitut* in
Hamburg, from a survey it says was conducted in **autumn 1982** on a
representative sample of the population: video games *"fördern die Kommunikation
und bauen Aggressionen ab"* — promote communication and reduce aggression — and
**96%** of video-game owners most like to play with family or friends.

That is a manufacturer's own promotional magazine reporting research favourable
to the manufacturer, and it should be read as exactly that. What it establishes
is not that the finding was sound but that the argument was being had in West
Germany in 1982, and that Atari thought its members would want ammunition for it.

## A club, not just a magazine

The rest is club business, and it is the part that dates it most precisely. The
January 1983 issue announces a relaunch — a new look for the new year, plenty of
colour, more to read — and apologises that the rack for VCS cassettes has sold
out, along with the shirts and the pullovers. A member writes in asking why there
are no club badges or pins. There is a *Freundschaftswerbung*, a member-get-member
scheme, with 1983 promised as the year of the *Superpreise*.

## Does it fit the house-organ pattern?

Not quite, and the difference is worth stating rather than smoothing over.

*Club Nintendo* is a manufacturer's magazine **produced once and localised
outward** — the English, German and Dutch editions are the same twenty pages.
*Atari Club Magazin* is a manufacturer's magazine too, but this library holds no
other national edition of it to compare against, and its contents read as locally
made: the advertising campaign is placed in *Stern* and *HÖRZU*, and the
editorial describes games its own staff were shown in America.

So the category [manufacturer's house organ](/vault/culture/magazines-across-borders)
contains at least two arrangements, and a first-party magazine is not
automatically a centrally produced one. On the evidence here this is a German
magazine written for German members by an American company's German operation.

⚠ That reading rests on seven short issues and on the absence of a second
edition to compare. If Atari ran the same club magazine in other countries, this
library does not hold it, and absence here is not evidence it did not exist.
