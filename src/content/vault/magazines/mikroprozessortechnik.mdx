**Mikroprozessortechnik** — *Zeitschrift für Mikroelektronik, Computertechnik,
Informatik* — began in **East Berlin in 1987**, published by **VEB Verlag
Technik** on Oranienburger Straße for the **Kammer der Technik**, the GDR's
official engineering chamber. This library holds the complete run of four annual
volumes, 1987 to 1990.

It is the first East German entry in this Vault, and it is deliberately kept
apart from the West German ones. *[Amiga Joker](/vault/magazines/amiga-joker)*
and this are not two versions of a German computer press.

## Licensed, in the literal sense

The colophon carries a line no other magazine in this Vault carries:

> "**Lizenz-Nr. 1710 des Presseamtes beim Vorsitzenden des Ministerrates der
> Deutschen Demokratischen Republik.**"

Licence number 1710, from the Press Office at the Chairman of the Council of
Ministers. A magazine existed because the state's press office numbered it.

Set that beside *[ST Magazine](/vault/magazines/st-magazine)* in France, whose
December 1986 editorial reports the **Commission paritaire** stripping it of
accreditation for being too narrow to count as journalism. Two European states,
three years apart, and two opposite postures toward a computer magazine: one
declining to recognise it as press, the other constituting it as press by
numbered licence.

Neither of those things could happen in Britain, which is why a Vault built from
British holdings would not know the question exists.

## What it was for

The first issue's lead article states the purpose in its title — Felix Meier,
**"Mikroelektronik: Schlüsseltechnologie für die dynamische Entwicklung unserer
Volkswirtschaft"**: microelectronics as the key technology for the dynamic
development of *our national economy*.

The rest of the issue is engineering, not consumer press: the **U5200** CMOS
gate-array system, the **RTS8000** real-time operating system, an intelligent
process-coupling module. Behind it sits an advisory board of some two dozen
professors and doctors.

Its programming courses, held here alongside the volumes, are the clearest
statement of where this computing sat: courses on **K1810WM86** — the Soviet
8086 clone — and on **REDABAS**, the GDR's dBASE. Not localised Western products,
and not clones described from outside, but the domestic stack taught to the
engineers expected to use it.
