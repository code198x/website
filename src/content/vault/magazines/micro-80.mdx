**MICRO-80** was an Australian monthly for the **TRS-80**, published from a post
office box in **Goodwood, South Australia**, beginning in **December 1979** at
$2.50. This library holds 49 issues, running to January 1984.

It is the earliest Australian title held, and it precedes the earliest British
one — *Your Computer*, June 1981 — by eighteen months.

## A magazine that bought from its readers

Its editorial states the model without ornament:

> "**WE WILL BUY YOUR PROGRAMS!** Most of the information we publish is provided
> by our readers, to whom we pay royalties. An application form containing full
> details of how you can use your TRS-80 to earn some extra income is included in
> every issue."

The monthly contract is equally specific: at least one applications program in
Level 1 BASIC, one in Level 2, one in Disk BASIC, plus utilities in Level 2 and
machine language, and a hardware modification or construction article at least
every second issue. Advertising rates are printed openly — $120 a full page, $30
a quarter, $8 for a fifty-word classified.

It also disclaims the obvious assumption: *"MICRO-80 is in no way connected with
the TANDY organisation."* A single-machine magazine published independently of
the machine's manufacturer, which is the opposite arrangement to
*[Club Nintendo](/vault/magazines/club-nintendo)* or
*[Atari Club Magazin](/vault/magazines/atari-club-magazin)*.

## The cassette, in 1979

Every issue's programs could be had on tape:

> "…in each month's issue are available on a cassette for an additional $3.50.
> [You] can order a 12 month subscription to the magazine PLUS cassette for
> $60.00, only $5.00 per month."

This is not a [cover tape](/vault/distribution/cover-tapes) — nothing is attached
to the cover, and the cassette is bought separately or bundled into a
subscription. But it addresses the same problem a decade earlier and on another
continent: the reader who wants the programs and not the typing. The British
cover-mounted model this Vault describes emerges in earnest around 1989.

## An anti-copying appeal that does not threaten

The most striking passage in the first issue is its copyright notice, which
starts conventionally and then changes register entirely:

> "…**Being practical, if you copied a program onto cassette and gave it to a
> friend who is not a subscriber to MICRO-80, we would be unlikely to sue you,
> even if we found out.** However, that's a pretty good way to put us out of
> business, then there would be no more programs to copy and no more money to be
> earned when we publish your programs! Think about it. If a friend asks you for
> a copy of a MICRO-80 program, why not get him to take out a subscription
> himself, that way everyone will benefit."

A publisher conceding in print that it will not enforce against casual copying,
and arguing from shared interest instead — in December 1979, years before the
British trade's anti-piracy campaigns. The argument it makes is circular by
design: copy this and there will be nothing to copy, and nothing to be paid for
when it is your program next time.
