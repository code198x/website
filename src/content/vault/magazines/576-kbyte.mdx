**576 KByte** was a Hungarian computer-games monthly. This library holds **140
issues** and 8,003 pages, running from **January 1990** to **December 1999**, and
it is the only Hungarian title held.

Its first issue is dated the month after Hungary's political transition. That
timing is the reason it is worth an entry, and also the reason to be careful
about what it can be made to say.

## What issue one is about

It is about Western games, without preamble. The contents run to *Iron Lord* —
described as UBI Soft's new historical adventure, preceded by months of
advertising — *The Champ*, *Boxing Manager*, *Oil Imperium*, *Dragons of Flame*,
*Persian Gulf Inferno* and *Fighter Bomber*. There is no editorial about a new
era, no notice of the year the country had just had. There is a magazine about
games.

The editorial addresses the reader as the owner of the thing:

> "**Tisztelt 576 KByte Tulajdonos!** Először is szerkesztőségünk nevében
> köszönjük, hogy megvette lapunkat!"

Dear 576 KByte Owner — first of all, on behalf of our editors, thank you for
buying our paper. It claims to be *az első színes, számítógépes programokkal
foglalkozó újság*, the first colour paper concerned with computer programs, and
promises the same quantity of information in the middle of every month.

Its stated purpose is unusually specific. The aim is to publish complete and
detailed descriptions of every program whose *kezelése vagy játszhatósága* —
handling or playability — would otherwise be difficult or perhaps impossible, and
a shorter review where that is not needed. The issue delivers on it: the *Iron
Lord* coverage is a walkthrough naming the menu options in English as the reader
would see them on screen — GIVE, BUY, HERBS, DISCUSS — inside Hungarian prose.

## The transition, counted in company forms

The magazine cannot be made to narrate the change around it, but its advertising
can be measured, and there the change is unambiguous.

Hungarian companies carry their legal form in their name. **Gmk** —
*gazdasági munkaközösség*, an economic working collective — was a semi-private
form the country's pre-1989 economic reforms had allowed. **Kft** and **Rt** are
the limited company and the joint-stock company.

| Year | Gmk | Kft | Rt |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1990 | 21 | 5 | 3 |
| 1991 | 43 | 5 | 8 |
| 1992 | 36 | 8 | 5 |
| 1993 | 21 | 1 | 22 |
| 1994 | 18 | 2 | 24 |
| 1995 | **0** | 3 | 16 |
| 1999 | **0** | 36 | 40 |

The old form is the common one in the magazine's first five years, and appears in
no issue held from 1995 onward. One company makes the point on its own: the
advertising contact printed in issue 1 is **COMGAME Gmk**, and COMGAME is a Gmk
in every issue held from January 1990 to **September 1994** and a **Kft** by
**April 1997**.

⚠ Bracketed, not dated — no issue held between those two names it, so the
conversion happened somewhere in that gap. And these are counts of *pages
containing an abbreviation*, not of companies. What they establish is when a form
stopped appearing in this magazine, not why it stopped: whether that reflects a
legal deadline, a tax change or ordinary commercial churn is not something this
library can answer.

## What this entry does not claim

The gap between a games magazine's first issue and the month the political system
changed is suggestive, and suggestive is all it is. Nothing in these pages
explains why the magazine launched when it did, who founded it, or what came
before it in Hungary.

⚠ This library holds no Hungarian title from before 1990 at all — nothing
Polish, Czechoslovak, East German, Bulgarian, Romanian or Yugoslav either, and
nothing from any of them predating 1989. So this entry describes a market
already opening and cannot describe what it opened from. That is a limit of the
collection, not a finding about Hungary.
