In July 1983 COMPUTE! Publications printed 175,000 copies of the first issue of a new magazine, more than twice the 75,000 it had planned. *Compute!'s Gazette* was for owners of the Commodore VIC-20 and 64, and its editor-in-chief, Robert Lock, explained the hurry in his first editorial: Commodore, he estimated, was "currently selling over 100,000 VIC-20s and 64s a month." Most of those buyers were beginners, and the Gazette promised them tutorials, games and applications in every issue. Above all it printed programs, pages of them, for readers to type in at home.

## Fast facts

- **First issue:** July 1983, $2.00; $20 for a year's subscription.
- **Publisher:** COMPUTE! Publications, Inc., of Greensboro, North Carolina, which also published [COMPUTE!](/vault/magazines/compute-magazine). It belonged to ABC's publishing division from 1983 and to General Media from 1990.
- **Editors:** Tom R. Halfhill (1983–84), Lance Elko (1984–90), Tom Netsel (from October 1990).
- **Machines:** [Commodore 64](/vault/systems/commodore-64) and [VIC-20](/vault/systems/commodore-vic-20) at first, later also the [Commodore 16 and Plus/4](/vault/systems/commodore-16) and the Commodore 128.
- **Disk edition:** every issue's programs on disk, from May 1984.
- **Run:** monthly magazine to 1990; a section of *COMPUTE* from October 1990 to December 1993; disk only from January 1994 to early 1995.

## Typing it right

A [type-in listing](/vault/distribution/type-in-listings) is only as good as the typing. One wrong character in a page of BASIC, or one wrong number in a block of machine code, and the program would not run. The Gazette's answer came from its program editor, Charles Brannon. "The Automatic Proofreader", in the October 1983 issue, sat in memory while the reader typed and showed a checksum after each line, to compare with the one printed beside it. In December 1983 came MLX, an entry program for the long lists of numbers that made up machine-language programs, which checked every line as it went in.

With those in place the Gazette could print programs far longer than anyone would type unaided. The January 1984 issue carried Brannon's SpeedScript, a word processor "with all the major features of commercial word processors", written entirely in machine language for the 64 and the expanded VIC-20.

## The disk, and an argument about copying

For readers who would rather not type, the Gazette began selling a monthly disk with the May 1984 issue: all the programs from each issue, for $69.95 a year or $7.95 a copy. Lock had raised the idea of protecting the disk against copying, and the readers wrote back in their hundreds. In the May issue he reported the decision: "initially at least, it will not be protected." The most common argument, he wrote, was that readers wanted to make backups, group programs onto their own disks, and "alter, enhance, and generally have a good time 'fiddling' with the code", which was the point of the magazine in the first place. He added a reminder that printing a program did not put it in the public domain.

## How many readers

American magazines printed a sworn circulation statement once a year. The Gazette's show average paid sales of about 238,000 copies an issue in the year to September 1984, rising to about 295,500 in 1985, the highest of the statements printed from 1984 to 1988. The figure was about 293,700 in 1986, then fell to about 253,600 in 1987 and 238,000 in 1988.

The magazine changed with Commodore's range. Looking back in 1993, Tom Netsel wrote that its coverage had expanded to the Plus/4, the 16 and the 128 as they appeared, and then narrowed again once the 64 and 128 "emerged as the survivors".

## Sold, merged and moved to disk

In 1990 Capital Cities/ABC sold its consumer magazines, and General Media bought the COMPUTE! titles. The last separate issue of the Gazette was dated June 1990. From October 1990 it continued as a section inside the Commodore 64/128 edition of the merged magazine, *COMPUTE*, under a new editor, Tom Netsel. Lance Elko, who had edited the Gazette since early 1984, had decided "the time had come for him to pursue other interests". Netsel promised the type-ins would stay: "The type-in programs have always been an integral part of the Gazette concept."

The paper Gazette lasted until December 1993. "This is the last issue that will be printed on paper," Netsel wrote. "Next month, Gazette moves entirely to disk." He promised that the columns by Jim Butterfield, Fred D'Ignazio, Larry Cotton and Steve Vander Ark would carry on as text files on a double-sided monthly disk, at $49.95 a year, with documentation that readers could read on screen or print out. *COMPUTE* itself ended with its September 1994 issue, and the Gazette disk continued into 1995; the last disk in the public archive is dated February 1995.

## The name returns

In 2025 COMPUTE! Publications, Inc., which records itself as the copyright holder of the COMPUTE! and Gazette archive for 1979 to 1995, revived *Compute!'s Gazette* as a monthly retro-computing magazine in print and digital editions. Unlike the original, it covers other classic machines besides Commodore's, including Atari computers and the Amiga.
