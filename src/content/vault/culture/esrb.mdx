The **Entertainment Software Rating Board** is the body whose letters appear on game boxes in North America: E for Everyone, T for Teen, M for Mature. It was not the government's idea. The American games industry created it in 1994, within a year of the day two senators, Joe Lieberman and Herb Kohl, held a hearing on *[Mortal Kombat](/vault/games/mortal-kombat)* and *Night Trap* and warned that Congress would act if the industry did not (see [the 1993 congressional hearings](/vault/culture/congressional-hearings-1993)). The trade body the companies formed, the Interactive Digital Software Association (IDSA), set up the ESRB as an independent board, and its first ratings reached the shops for Christmas 1994. What it offered Congress was information for parents instead of a law.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1994, by the Interactive Digital Software Association
- **First director:** Dr Arthur Pober, formerly of the Children's Advertising Review Unit of the Council of Better Business Bureaus
- **First ratings:** Christmas 1994
- **Categories in 1994:** Early Childhood (3+), Kids to Adults (6+), Teen (13+), Mature (17+), Adults Only (18+)
- **Changes:** Kids to Adults became Everyone (1998); Everyone 10+ added (2005); Early Childhood retired (2018)
- **Legal force:** none; it is voluntary, enforced by console makers and shops

## How it started

Before the ESRB, Sega's games carried ratings and Nintendo's did not. In the summer of 1993 [Sega](/vault/companies/sega) set up its own Videogame Rating Council, a panel of experts in education, child development, psychology and film, whose labels (GA for general audiences, MA-13, and MA-17 for "young adults and those mature enough to appreciate adult themes") appeared on Sega's boxes and in its advertisements. Sega expected fewer than 1 per cent of games to be rated MA-17. [Nintendo](/vault/companies/nintendo) relied on its own content rules instead, which is why its *Mortal Kombat* had grey "sweat" where the arcade game had blood. At the Senate hearing of December 1993 Sega's Bill White used that difference against Nintendo's [Howard Lincoln](/vault/people/howard-lincoln): Nintendo had taken out the blood but sold the fighting to all ages, while Sega's version carried a rating for 13 and over.

After the hearing the rivals had to work together. Their committee, chaired by Jack Heistand of [Electronic Arts](/vault/companies/electronic-arts), promised the Senate on 4 March 1994 that a rating system would be ready by the end of the year. The IDSA was formed in April with members including Sega, Nintendo, Sony, [Acclaim](/vault/companies/acclaim), Atari, [Capcom](/vault/companies/capcom), Crystal Dynamics, Electronic Arts, [Konami](/vault/companies/konami), Philips, Viacom New Media and Virgin Interactive, and on 29 July it presented the ESRB to a joint Congressional hearing. Under Pober, each new game would be assessed by three raters "who have no ties to the industry", and the board would assign a rating symbol and a description of who the game was suitable for. *Sega Visions* told its readers the ratings would be in place by Christmas, and that about 45 per cent of the Christmas releases might carry them. [Nintendo Power](/vault/magazines/nintendo-power) printed the five categories with its own glosses: Early Childhood was "Gerber meets Barney", Kids to Adults allowed "mild violence, Koopa stomping", and Adults Only covered "sex, drugs, profanity and goat worship".

## A rival scheme for computer games

The ESRB began as a console industry project, and many PC publishers did not like it. *[Computer Gaming World](/vault/magazines/computer-gaming-world)* complained in June 1994 that the plan had been drawn up "without significant input by personal computer-based publishers" and that reviewing every game in full before release would delay disk-based games and cost players money. The magazine had been calling for voluntary content labels since 1990, and it preferred a different model. In October 1994 the Recreational Software Advisory Council (RSAC) launched ratings that gave no age at all. Publishers answered a questionnaire, and the label showed a "thermometer" from 1 to 4 for violence, nudity and sex, and language, with a short description. At the launch *[Doom](/vault/games/doom)* was the example: 3 for violence ("Blood and Gore") and 1 for language ("Mild Expletives"), for its use of the word "hell".

The ESRB won. By July 1999 *Computer Gaming World*'s editor wrote that RSAC's labels were used on "less than 5 percent of new games" and that most games were rated by the ESRB, whose age categories he thought told parents too little about what was in the box.

## Changing the categories

The ratings have changed several times since 1994:

| Year | Change |
|------|--------|
| 1994 | Five categories: EC (3+), K-A (6+), T (13+), M (17+), AO (18+), with content descriptors |
| 1998 | Kids to Adults renamed **E**, Everyone, still for ages 6 and up |
| 2005 | **E10+** (Everyone 10 and older) added between E and T |
| 2013 | The International Age Rating Coalition (IARC) incorporated; its ratings reached online shops such as the Nintendo eShop and Google Play in 2015 |
| 2018 | Early Childhood retired: so few games needed it that E covered them |
| 2020 | An "In-Game Purchases (Includes Random Items)" notice added for games that sell randomised items |

*Maximum PC* explained in 2005 that E10+ filled "the gaping chasm" between Everyone and Teen, for games such as "racing games with extreme crashes". The ESRB said of Early Childhood that there were "SO few games that fit the criteria" and that E could almost always be applied to them.

The ratings carry no legal force, but the top one works almost like a ban. The *Official U.S. PlayStation Magazine* noted in 1999 that console manufacturers had "yet to allow an AO-rated game", and by 2005 major American retailers, *EGM* reported, would not sell Adults Only titles at all.

## Hot Coffee

That became the ESRB's biggest test in 2005. Patrick Wildenborg, a modder, found a sexual minigame, "Hot Coffee", in the code of [Rockstar](/vault/companies/rockstar)'s *[Grand Theft Auto](/vault/games/grand-theft-auto): San Andreas*, which was rated M. After first deflecting questions, Rockstar acknowledged that the scenes, though ordinary players could not reach them, were on the disc. Senator Hillary Clinton called for an enquiry into why the game had not been rated AO, and members of Congress asked for a Federal Trade Commission investigation of Rockstar's parent company. The ESRB investigated and re-rated the game AO. Shops took it off their shelves, Rockstar stopped production and began work on a version without the code, and the parent company cut its quarterly earnings forecast by about $50 million.

The ESRB's president, Patricia Vance, explained the decision to *EGM*: "The Hot Coffee minigame is explicit, with or without the nudity." The rules stayed the same with one addition: publishers now had to disclose all content on a disc that bore on the rating, "playable or not".

## Britain: voluntary labels and the law

Britain's system grew out of the same pressure in the same year, but took a different form. In April 1994 the European Leisure Software Publishers Association (ELSPA) introduced voluntary age labels in four bands, 0 to 10, 11 to 14, 15 to 17 and 18 plus, which its 60 member companies could print on their boxes. "The classification is not legally binding," *[ST Format](/vault/magazines/st-format)* noted, "so it is up to the individual retailers to prevent younger children buying games which are unsuitable." The magazine suspected the scheme was a way to put off a compulsory one.

Unlike America, Britain already had a law that could apply to games. Most games were exempt from the Video Recordings Act, but one with gross violence, sexual activity or other content the Act named had to be classified by the British Board of Film Classification like a film, and its certificate was legally binding. *Edge* noted in 1998 that *Grand Theft Auto* and *Carmageddon* had both been given 18 certificates by law. A reader of *PC Zone* summed up the arrangement in 2001: "The ELSPA rating is advisory and voluntary. The BBFC is legally binding."

In April 2003 ELSPA's labels gave way to PEGI, the Pan European Game Information system, used in sixteen countries and administered by the Netherlands Institute for the Classification of Audiovisual Media, with pictograms for content: *Edge* listed a spider for horror, a syringe for drugs and a clenched fist for violence. *Edge* stressed in 2004 that PEGI was voluntary too: "It is neither compulsory for a publisher to apply for a PEGI rating nor carry the rating on packaging." That changed on 30 July 2012, when the Video Standards Council took over rating games in the UK under PEGI and the 12, 16 and 18 ratings became legally enforceable. Selling a game rated 12, 16 or 18 to a younger child became a criminal offence. No such law survived in America: in 2011 the US Supreme Court struck down a Californian law against selling violent games to minors, ruling that games are protected speech under the First Amendment.
