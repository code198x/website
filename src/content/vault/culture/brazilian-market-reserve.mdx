Brazil's home-computer market did not run on imports. It ran on machines built
in Brazil, under a policy of **reserva de mercado** — a market reserved for
domestic manufacturers — and this changed what the machines were, not only who
sold them.

The clearest statement of it in this library is not a policy document. It is an
interview with a manufacturer's commercial director, **Oscar Alegre** of
[DISMAC](/vault/companies/dismac), in the first issue of
*[Micro Sistemas](/vault/magazines/micro-sistemas)* in October 1981.

## The reasoning, in the industry's own words

Asked whether the government would keep the market reserve, he does not defend it
on grounds of national pride:

> "**O Brasil não tem outra opção.** O Brasil não tem petróleo e possui uma
> dívida muito elevada, cujo serviço é pesado."

Brazil has no other option; Brazil has no oil and carries a very high debt whose
servicing is heavy. The policy is presented as a balance-of-payments measure —
every imported component is foreign currency leaving a country that cannot spare
it.

The interview's pull-quote puts the same argument in a domestic image:

> "É preferível **comer em um prato de lata de fabricação nacional** do que ficar
> devendo um prato de porcelana inglesa. Quanto mais se deve, mais dependente
> se é."

Better to eat off a domestically made tin plate than to be in debt for an English
porcelain one. The more you owe, the more dependent you are.

## What it cost, stated plainly

He does not claim the machines were better, or even as cheap:

> "Sabemos que um gravador importado custa menos que o que tem nosso aparelho.
> Mas com esses componentes não gastamos fora, não nos endividamos mais. **O
> diferencial de custo é um preço a pagar pelo desenvolvimento.**"

We know an imported cassette recorder costs less than the one in our machine.
But with these components we spend nothing abroad and take on no more debt. The
cost difference is a price to be paid for development.

Argentina is named as the counter-example: there the policy of importing
manufactured goods was adopted, "e os resultados foram desastrosos para o país".

## It reached down into the hardware

The detail worth keeping is a design decision. Asked about adding a printer to
the D-8000, he says they would not fit a thermal one —

> "para não depender de **papel importado**"

— so as not to depend on imported paper.

A machine's peripheral specification, decided by the balance of payments. This is
the kind of thing that makes a machine unintelligible to anyone reading its
specification alone: a thermal printer was the cheap, obvious choice everywhere
else, and here it was ruled out by the consumable it needed.

The same interview names the market's other consequence — you could not simply
buy a peripheral. In the United States, he says, you buy a micro and then go to
any shop for peripherals; in Brazil "por enquanto o problema dos periféricos é
grave", and DISMAC would have to build its own memory expansion because nobody
else would until enough machines existed to make it worth doing.

## On copying

The interview is equally direct about where the designs came from:

> "**O Brasil ainda copia.** Mas se a roda está inventada, para que inventá-la de
> novo? … Não vale a pena investir no desenvolvimento de um hardware original ou
> na criação de uma linguagem brasileira. **Pesquisa quem pode, e não quem
> quer.**"

Brazil still copies; if the wheel is invented, why invent it again — research is
done by those who can, not those who would like to. Asked whether Brazil should
copy Japanese machines instead of American ones, he answers that Japan's virtue
is copying American secrets at half the cost, but that the ideas are American.

⚠ **This is one executive's account, in one interview, in 1981.** It is a
first-hand statement of the reasoning by someone inside the industry the policy
protected, which makes it good evidence of how the policy was argued for and poor
evidence of whether the argument was right. This library holds no Brazilian
economic source, no government document, and no critic of the reserve. Its later
effects — on prices, on the machines available, on when the policy ended — are
not documented here and are not claimed.
