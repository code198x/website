Games disappear. Disks rot, cartridges fail, companies lose source code, servers shut down. Game preservation fights this entropy through emulation, archiving, and documentation. Community efforts preserve what corporations often neglect, ensuring future generations can experience gaming history firsthand.

## Why it stalls

The obstacles this entry lists are decay and obsolescence. In practice the binding
ones are ownership and money.

**Ownership: the *Manic Miner* case.** The National Videogame Archive wanted the
game, "and it finally tracked down the game's developer, Matthew Smith, for
permission, only to discover he didn't actually hold the rights." Finding the author
is not finding the rightsholder — the same wall the
[abandonware](/vault/distribution/abandonware) entry runs into, and the same one that
stopped Ian Bell releasing *Elite* on his own.

**Loss: publishers binned things on a defensible judgement.** Games "had long been
wiped or lost because past publishers didn't believe their commercial life would
extend much past a year or so." That is not indifference to an asset; it is a
reasonable-at-the-time conclusion that there was no asset.

**Money: the threat the list omits.** The National Videogame Archive was hosted at
the National Media Museum in Bradford with industry backing. Then, per Iain Simons:

> After the coalition government came to power, there was a massive bloodbath and
> loads of people involved in that project lost their jobs. The collection still
> exists, but there was no capacity within the museum to continue actively
> collecting. It was paused — although not formally — because there were simply no
> resources.

A collection does not have to be destroyed to stop being preserved. It can simply
stop growing.

## What an archive actually holds

The methods below are all about the software. A working archive collects around it:
"prototypes, early sketches, box art, advertising, and even mods and fan art", with
the aim of placing them "with their historical, social, political, and cultural
contexts".

Simons's summary of the shape of the problem: "Video game preservation can only
succeed in the long term as a really large group effort. It's a massive scale
problem. Some issues are technical; some are legal."

## Fast facts

- **Threats:** media decay, format obsolescence, untraceable rights, and funding withdrawal.
- **Methods:** Emulation, ROM archiving, documentation.
- **Challenges:** Legal ambiguity, corporate resistance.
- **Importance:** Cultural heritage at risk.
