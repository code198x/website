Getting a computer into a country and getting a game onto that computer were different problems. Software could travel on a cassette or disk, but it could also arrive as a printed listing for a reader to type. A translation or a version for another machine could take it to a new audience.

Period magazines show some of those journeys directly. They tell us which games editors described, which programs they printed and what dealers offered. Each kind of evidence answers a different question about how software moved.

## A listing becomes an Italian program

Roy Harper's *Meteor Storm* appeared in the American magazine *SoftSide* in November 1980. The article named its author and supplied a program for readers to enter. On 15 June 1984, the first issue of Italy's [*PAPERsoft*](/vault/magazines/papersoft) included *Meteor Storm* with a credit to R. Harper and a separate credit to M. Cerofolini for translation and adaptation.

That second credit matters. The Italian edition involved work on the presentation of the program, rather than simply mentioning an American game. The reader received software through a local publication, with an adapter named alongside its original author. It is a concrete route across a language boundary; it does not establish the licensing terms between the publications.

## British games in a Polish magazine

The October 1986 *[Bajtek](/vault/magazines/bajtek)* readers' chart included *Impossible Mission*, *Bomb Jack*, *Saboteur* and *Enigma Force*. The accompanying text said that readers across Poland supplied votes. This was a readers' poll, not a sales chart.

On the same page, the magazine identified London's Electronics Export as the supplier of games offered as prizes. Here the evidence goes beyond a familiar title appearing in print: a named British company was supplying software to the Polish magazine. It still tells us nothing about the proportion of Polish players who bought imports, swapped copies or obtained games another way.

## Robbo travels in the other direction

Polish-developed software also reached foreign coverage. In February 1994, *Bajtek* interviewed the Xland team. Janusz Pelc explained why they had brought *Robbo* from Atari to PC: he wanted to revisit his earlier game and reach computer owners who had not played that version.

The following month, Germany's *PC Player* reviewed PC *Robbo*. Its product panel named Xland/Adamik, gave an approximate price of DM50 and identified German instructions and game text. Thomas Werner's review also described German speech announcing collected objects.

The appraisal was critical of the game, but its presence is unambiguous. A Polish team's work had become a product described for German readers, with localised material. That example rules out any account in which games travelled only from west to east.

## What coverage can establish

A review is evidence of coverage. An advertisement is evidence of an offer. A translated listing supplies a program. None alone measures total sales or proves that a game was available to everyone in a country.

The reverse inference is weaker still: failure to find a title in selected magazines does not show that it never crossed a border. Different names, versions, languages and publication dates can obscure the same game's journey. Following named products and their credits gives a firmer history than treating every title match as a sale, or every missing match as a closed border.
