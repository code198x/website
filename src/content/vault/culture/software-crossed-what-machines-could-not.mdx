This Vault has established that [machines were confined by their
markets](/vault/culture/the-c64-across-borders) — the Galaksija appears only in
Yugoslav pages, the PMD-85 only in Czechoslovak ones, and the countries with a
free import trade have no such machines at all.

Software behaves in the opposite way, and asymmetrically.

## Western games went everywhere

Counting how many of the thirteen territories held here mention each title at
all:

| Title | Territories | Mentions |
|---|---:|---:|
| **Elite** | **12** | 4,924 |
| Pac-Man | 11 | 1,187 |
| Boulder Dash | 11 | 515 |
| Winter Games | 11 | 347 |
| Digger | 11 | 86 |
| Knight Lore | 10 | 435 |
| Manic Miner | 10 | 286 |
| Bruce Lee | 10 | 391 |
| Tetris | 9 | 1,492 |

*Elite* reaches the press of Argentina, Brazil, Czechoslovakia, West Germany,
Spain, France, Britain, Greece, Hungary, Italy, Poland and Yugoslavia. Twelve of
thirteen.

No machine in this library does that except the ones that were freely traded. A
game is a cassette, a listing, or a copy; it crosses a border a computer cannot.

## But almost nothing came back

The traffic is one-directional.

| Title | Mentions | Distribution |
|---|---:|---|
| **Robbo** (Polish) | 41 | **Poland 97%**, Britain 2% |
| **Novotrade** (Hungarian software house) | 258 | **Hungary 98%** |
| **Galaksija** | 276 | **Yugoslavia 100%** |

*Robbo* was among the best known Polish games — *Bajtek*'s 1990 readers' poll
describes it as the one title breaking out of the standard, and it is still being
indexed in the magazine in 1994. It appears twice, in total, outside Poland.

So a Polish reader in 1987 could read about *Elite*, *Knight Lore* and *Boulder
Dash*, and a British reader in the same year had essentially no way to hear about
what Polish programmers were writing.

## Measured against the catalogue, not against three names

Three titles are an anecdote. The TOSEC software catalogue makes a census
possible: it names 703,399 releases for the six 8- and 16-bit machines this
library covers, and tags each with the territory it was released in.

Two filters turn that into a usable list of *local* software. The first is
period: releases dated 1982 to 1995, which removes a large modern homebrew
scene — two thirds of the Hungarian entries are dated 1996 or later. The second
is origin purity: a title counts as local only if every entry it has in the
catalogue carries one local tag and it never appears untagged. Without that
second filter the lists fill with imports, because the tag records where a
release came *out*, not where it came *from*.

That leaves 250 titles from Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, the Yugoslav
successor tags, Argentina, Brazil and Greece whose names are built from words
that do not occur in the Anglophone corpus at all — *piskvorky*, *budżet domowy*,
*adatállomány*, *návrh plošných spojů*, *velika akcija*. Against them, 250
British-published titles of the same period, drawn at random.

Every territory's corpus was sampled to the same 8.0 MB of text first, so that
Britain's 1,386 issues could not out-vote Poland's 149. And every apparent
crossing, in both groups, was then read on the page — because a title is only a
string until you look at the sentence around it.

| Matched sample, 250 titles each | Local-language titles | British titles |
|---|---:|---:|
| Appear in their **home** press | 19 | 17 |
| Cross a border, before reading | 6 | 32 |
| Cross a border, **after reading** | **0** | **27** |

The two groups are found at home at the same rate, which is what makes the last
row mean anything: the method finds local titles in Polish and Czech magazines
as readily as it finds British ones in British magazines. It then finds the
British ones in ten other territories — Greece, Australia, West Germany,
Yugoslavia, the United States, France, Poland, Hungary, Italy and Brazil — and
the local ones in none.

Reading is what separates the two rows. All six local crossings dissolved:
*Hydraulik* in the East German press is the German word for hydraulics,
*Inspektor* in Yugoslav pages is *Inspector Gadget*, and *fotografie*,
*kilometry* and *cubo magico* are ordinary words in the languages that printed
them. Five of the thirty-two British ones dissolved too — *Flight Simulator* is
Microsoft's, the *Evil Dead* mentions are a 2000s console game, "Pro Sound
Designer" is a French audio card, and "terrorists" and "treasure hunt" are
prose.

The twenty-seven that survive are unmistakable. *Bajtek* explains that Paul is
a member of the Enigma Force. A Hungarian chart lists *Graeme Souness Soccer
Manager* at number 30. A Greek dealer prices *Samurai Warrior* under Firebird,
a Yugoslav one lists *Operation Thunderbolt*, and an Australian reviewer
compares a game unfavourably with *Cybernoid II*.

⚠ Only 19 of 250 local titles are found even at home, and 17 of 250 British
ones. Most catalogued software of any nationality never reached print anywhere.
The survey measures whether titles that reach print at all cross borders; it
cannot speak for the rest.

## What this means for the record

The asymmetry is not evidence that local software was worse. It is evidence
about **who was writing the magazines that survive**, and about which direction
the distribution machinery ran. Western publishers had export businesses, trade
press and advertising budgets. A Polish or Hungarian program reached its market
through a magazine listing, a club, or a copied cassette — mechanisms with no
outward reach at all.

That has a direct consequence for a Vault built on magazines. A history assembled
from any one national press will contain the international titles and its own
local ones, and will systematically miss every *other* country's local ones. The
absence of Polish games from British magazines is a fact about British magazines.

⚠ Territory counts are "appears at all", which a single passing mention
satisfies. They measure reach, not popularity.
