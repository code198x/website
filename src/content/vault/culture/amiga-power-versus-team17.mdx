## Overview

*Amiga Power* built its identity on scoring games lower than everyone else and saying so. Its long-running argument with **[Team17](/vault/companies/team17)** is the clearest case, and the magazine documented it in its own pages — including a regular feature that printed rival magazines' scores in a table beside its own.

## The Disseminator

*Amiga Power* 56 carries the feature under a hard-boiled framing — "Slack reviewers and **THOSE WHO LIE** cower in their grimy rooming-houses. The Disseminator is abroad" — above a line implying the competition took money, phrased as a joke about libel: "it's probably libellous to say they took silly [money] so we won't".

The table itself is the argument:

| Game | *The One* | *Amiga Action* | *Amiga Power* |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| **ATR** | 85% | 88% | **38%** |
| **Fears** | 87% | 92% | **40%** |
| **Brutal** | 69% | 90% | **27%** |
| **Kingpin** | 78% | 84% | **47%** |
| Rise of the Robots | 59% | 92% | — |
| Sensible Golf | 81% | 90% | — |
| Behind the Iron Gate | 78% | 52% | 55% |
| Citadel | — | 67% | 67% |

Two of those — *ATR* and *Kingpin* — are Team17's, and they carry the widest gaps in the table: **fifty and thirty-seven points** below what a rival gave the same game.

Note the two rows at the bottom. *Behind the Iron Gate* and *Citadel* show AP agreeing with its rivals, which is what makes the feature an argument and not a boast. The claim is not "we always score lower"; it is "**we score the good ones the same and the bad ones honestly**".

## What happened next, in AP's account

By 1996, reviewing *Alien Breed 3D 2*, the magazine sets out the escalation itself. This is *Amiga Power*'s telling of a dispute it was party to, and is recorded here as such:

- **AP's writer Jonathan Nash called Team17 "whining childish hatemongers™"** — the magazine's own summary of how the row was conducted.
- A threatened **lawsuit** over the *ATR* and *Kingpin* reviews, which AP characterises as ridiculous.
- A campaign, as AP describes it, to have sister magazine *Amiga Format* sign documents pledging to **withhold review copies from AP** before being permitted to review Team17 games.
- Public abuse on Usenet, with AP pointing readers at a **Dejanews archive URL** so they could read the exchange for themselves.

That last detail is the period's tell. In 1996 a magazine could settle an argument by printing a link and letting readers judge — the first time the press's own conduct was independently checkable by its audience.

## Which Team17?

⚠ **The Team17 of 1996 was not commercially the Team17 of 1992**, and the feud ran across both.

*CU Amiga*, **May 1995**, reports the change:

> Team 17 have signed a deal with **Ocean Software to exclusively distribute their games on all formats**… both Ocean and Team 17 retain their own separate identities… will increase the Team's software distribution from their current **18 countries to 64**… In the future, games which fall under the deal will be branded with **both the Ocean and Team 17 logos**.

The magazine then asks the obvious question in its own words — "**Will Team 17 be swallowed up though?**" — and prints the answer from Team17's PR manager Alan Bunker: "Team 17's branding will be maintained and **we are still 100% independent**".

So by the time of the *Alien Breed 3D 2* row, Team17 was a year into exclusive distribution by [Ocean](/vault/companies/ocean-software), dual-branded, with Ocean's cash and a reach three times larger. The Swedish two-person development duos of the [Andreas Tadic](/vault/people/andreas-tadic) era and the company conducting a press dispute through lawyers and Usenet are the same legal entity operating under different commercial conditions.

That is worth stating because it changes what the feud is evidence *of*. A small Wakefield studio falling out with a magazine is a personal row. A studio inside a major publisher's distribution machine falling out with a magazine is **a structural conflict about what reviews are for** — and it is exactly the point at which a publisher acquires both the motive and the leverage to care about a 38%.

⚠⚠ **A related claim this library cannot support.** Ocean is sometimes said to have influenced review scores commercially. Nothing in this corpus evidences that — the searches return only readers writing about buying Amigas. *Amiga Power*'s own insinuation in The Disseminator is aimed at **rival magazines** taking money, and names no publisher. The Ocean distribution deal is documented; any claim beyond it is not, and is recorded here as unverified, not repeated.

## Why it mattered

**A review score is a commercial instrument.** Publishers bought advertising in the magazines that reviewed them, and a 38% on a game with a marketing budget behind it costs somebody real money. Most magazines managed that tension by scoring generously; AP's answer was to score honestly and then publish evidence that its rivals did not.

Whether it was right is a separate question from whether it was unusual, and it was unusual. The same instinct runs through the era's better journalism — [CRASH](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine) publishing from Ludlow instead of London, as the [Roger Kean](/vault/people/roger-kean) entry sets out, kept a similar distance for structural reasons.

It also cost something. A magazine at war with a major publisher is a magazine that struggles for review copies, cover-disk demos and access — and AP was fighting one of the most successful British studios of the period, whose games its readers wanted to read about.
