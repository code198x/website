**Spectrum clones** were unofficial ZX Spectrum variants produced across the Soviet bloc and elsewhere during the 1980s-90s. The Spectrum's spare architecture made it copyable without Western manufacturing capabilities, spreading computing education across regions where official imports were banned or unaffordable.

## Fast facts

- **Era:** 1985-1990s
- **Origin:** USSR, Eastern Europe, South America
- **Reason:** Import bans, cost, educational needs
- **Variants:** Hundreds of designs
- **Notable:** Pentagon, Didaktik, Scorpion

## The clones are in the file format

The strongest evidence that these were treated as real machines rather than
curiosities is that the Spectrum community's own tape-format standard gives them
identifiers. The **TZX** specification's Hardware Type table lists thirty computers,
and nine of them are clones or regional variants:

| ID | Machine | Where |
|---:|---|---|
| 08 | Pentagon 128 | USSR |
| 0A | Didaktik M | Czechoslovakia |
| 0B | Didaktik Gama | Czechoslovakia |
| 0E | ZX Spectrum 128k, Spanish version | Spain |
| 0F | ZX Spectrum, Arabic version | — |
| 10 | TK 90-X | Brazil |
| 11 | TK 95 | Brazil |
| 12 | Byte | Bulgaria |
| 13 | Elwro | Poland |
| 14 | ZS Scorpion | Russia |

A tape file could declare which clone it was written for. That is a level of
recognition the table below does not convey — and it names Brazilian and Bulgarian
machines the table omits, and two Didaktik models where the table has one. (A
third, the Didaktik Kompakt, turns up in software compatibility listings.)

## Not every clone was a copy

The word "clone" flattens a real distinction. Grussu separates machines "either
semi-officially built like the **Unipolbrit Komputer 2086** (Poland), or bypassing
copyright — from the Didaktik series (Czechoslovakia/Slovakia) to the CIP (Romania),
up to the Pentagon, Scorpion and Sprinter (USSR/Russia)". Licensing status varied by
country, and some manufacturers had an arrangement.

**And the originals arrived first.** The design travelled by hand before it
travelled by schematic: "Often smuggled from the West, the Spectrums, due to their
low cost and ease of use, found fertile ground for diffusion in those countries, to
the point that local microelectronics companies made 'their' versions of the
machine" — some of which "would have outlived their 'forefather'".

## Why the Spectrum?

| Factor | Importance |
|--------|------------|
| **Few parts** | ULA + Z80 copyable |
| **Documentation** | Schematics available |
| **Educational need** | USSR wanted computer literacy |
| **Cost** | Cheaper to clone than import |

## Enhanced Clones

Some clones exceeded the original:
- **Pentagon 1024:** Up to 1MB RAM
- **Scorpion:** Better build quality, still made
- **Features:** Turbo modes, extra memory

## Legacy

Spectrum clones created computing cultures across Eastern Europe that persist today. Many programmers in the region learned on these machines. The clone ecosystem shows both the limits of IP enforcement and the power of a design cheap enough to copy and documented enough to reproduce.
