In 1983 Donna Mason walked into the computer lab at Alice Deal Junior High School in Washington, D.C., and found ten Commodore 64s "and little else". There was no software and no guidance, and she had to build a computer literacy course for 1,000 students. "I was just told to do it," she told *COMPUTE!'s Gazette* in 1988. "There wasn't too much software available for the 64, so our program consisted of teaching BASIC programming."

That was how BASIC got into many classrooms. It was rarely chosen after a debate about languages. It was the language built into the machine the school had bought, as the [BASIC Programming](/vault/culture/basic-programming) entry explains, and it was ready to use the moment the computer was switched on. A teacher with a room of new computers and nothing else could start with `PRINT` and go on from there.

## Fast facts

- **Language:** whichever BASIC the school's computers had in ROM: [BBC BASIC](/vault/systems/bbc-micro#bbc-basic-and-the-assembler), Commodore's [BASIC V2](/vault/languages/basic-v2), Applesoft, [Sinclair BASIC](/vault/languages/sinclair-basic) and others.
- **Britain:** the BBC's [Computer Literacy Project](/vault/phenomena/bbc-computer-literacy-project) from 1982, and government subsidies that put a computer, usually a BBC Micro, into primary and secondary schools.
- **North America:** Apple held 58 per cent of American public-school computers in 1987, according to figures *COMPUTE!'s Gazette* quoted; Commodore said it led in Canada.
- **France:** the *Informatique pour tous* plan, announced on 25 January 1985, equipped schools with Thomson and Exelvision computers.
- **The rivals:** [Logo](/vault/languages/logo-language) in primary schools, and [Pascal](/vault/languages/pascal), which the College Board required for its Advanced Placement computer science course in the 1980s.

## Britain: a television course with a computer

The British approach was unusual because it began with broadcasting. The BBC's [Computer Literacy Project](/vault/phenomena/bbc-computer-literacy-project) built *The Computer Programme* (1982) around a computer of its own, the [BBC Micro](/vault/systems/bbc-micro), so that every program shown on screen would run at home and in school. In the first issue of *[Acorn User](/vault/magazines/acorn-user)*, in the summer of 1982, the project's editor David Allen set out the thinking behind the second series, *Making the Most of the Micro*. "Controversy reigns about the value of teaching programming," he wrote, "but there is growing evidence that, providing the nitty-gritty of 'coding' is seen as the last stage of a process of 'problem solving', the route to computer literacy is through 'hands-on' experience."

The machines arrived in schools with government money. In July 1982 the Prime Minister announced a £9 million scheme to put a microcomputer into every primary school by 1984, with the Department of Industry paying half the cost of one of three approved machines. By March 1983 *The Micro User* reported that 87 per cent of the schools taking up the offer had chosen the BBC Micro over the two alternatives. The [Educational Software](/vault/culture/educational-software) entry tells the story of those schemes and of the programs written for them.

Many learners met BASIC through a printed course rather than a teacher. The National Extension College in Cambridge published *30 Hour BASIC* to go with the BBC's series, and sold it for other machines too. Its 1983 advertisement for the ZX81 edition called it "Britain's top selling BASIC course", with "over 100,000 users", 8 chapters, 155 programs, 30 worked examples, 53 self-checks and 35 exercises, at £5.95.

## North America: Apple, Commodore and the school lab

In the United States the school computer was usually an Apple. In October 1987 *[COMPUTE!'s Gazette](/vault/magazines/computes-gazette)* quoted a *USA Today* count, drawn from several sources, that gave Apple "a whopping 58 percent" of the computers in public schools, Tandy 14 per cent, Commodore 12 per cent and IBM 6 per cent. Commodore's machines were cheap enough to buy in quantity. Lilly Meizner Toback, whose firm supplied school systems in the Northeast, had begun selling Commodore's PET to schools ten years earlier and had sold "thousands of 64s and hundreds of 128s" since. "Commodore's affordability," she said, "allows school systems to provide hands-on experience to a large number of students at a reasonable cost." Commodore itself had no educational marketing division by then. A year later the same magazine reported that the company had "dropped out of the education market" after its early success, while in Canada it still claimed the lead: "We probably have around 21 percent of the market share, and Apple has about 19 percent," said Ray Prachun, one of Commodore Canada's regional education managers.

The arc of Donna Mason's course is the arc of many. Five years after she started teaching BASIC on her ten 64s, her lab no longer taught programming as a subject. "We really moved away from pure computer literacy, where we taught its history and how a computer works," she said, "to just using the computer as a tool." Her students used tutorials, drill-and-practice programs and LogoWriter, a Logo package that combined drawing with a word processor, in lessons across the school.

## France: computers for everyone

France went further than any other country in this entry. On 25 January 1985 the Prime Minister, Laurent Fabius, presented the *Informatique pour tous* plan ("Computing for all"). It aimed to introduce every pupil in France to computers, open the equipment to the public, and train 110,000 teachers in a year. According to Jacques Baudé, who led the French association for computers in education, the EPI, at the time, about 12,000 schools and colleges received a *nanoréseau*, a small network of home computers run from a professional machine, and the 33,000 schools without a network got a basic set: a home computer, a television, a program recorder and a printer. The home computers came from two French makers, Thomson (the MO5 and TO7) and Exelvision. The plan had its critics, among them, Baudé recalled, educational psychologists who claimed that making children program in BASIC was "massacring young minds". It was interrupted after the change of government in March 1986.

## The case against BASIC

BASIC's critics were as old as the classroom micro. In 1975 the Dutch computer scientist Edsger Dijkstra wrote, in a memo titled "How do we tell truths that might hurt?": "It is practically impossible to teach good programming to students that have had a prior exposure to BASIC: as potential programmers they are mentally mutilated beyond hope of regeneration." BASIC's own inventors, John Kemeny and Thomas Kurtz, complained that home computers had frozen their language at its 1964 level, and wrote True BASIC to replace it; the [BASIC Programming](/vault/culture/basic-programming) entry tells that story, and the American educator Arthur Luehrmann's warning about "street BASIC".

The commercial alternative was Pascal. In 1986 Kyan Software advertised its Pascal for the Commodore 64 and 128 under the headline "BASIC IS FOR WIMPS!", promising programs that ran "30 times faster" and making a pitch to students: "pascal is required by the College Entrance Exam Board for advanced placement in computer science." Directly below it in *COMPUTE!'s Gazette* that June, a teacher's 13-lesson BASIC course for Commodore machines, over 220 pages for $19.95, advertised that it was "currently used in both High School and Adult Evening Education classes".

In primary schools the alternative was [Logo](/vault/languages/logo-language), [Seymour Papert](/vault/people/seymour-papert)'s language, in which children steered a turtle around the screen or the classroom floor. Both Logo and BASIC ran on the same subsidised BBC Micros, and on Donna Mason's Commodores.
