In the spring of 1984 the letters pages of Britain's computer magazines filled up with a seven-digit number. Type 6031769 while *[Manic Miner](/vault/games/manic-miner)* is running, *[Sinclair User](/vault/magazines/sinclair-user)*'s readers learned in March, and "a boot will appear by the lives at the bottom"; then hold down combinations of the number keys and the game jumps to any of its twenty caverns. By June *[Computer & Video Games](/vault/magazines/computer-and-video-games)* could begin a tip with "Everyone must have heard by now that it's possible to cheat on the Spectrum version of Manic Miner".

A **cheat code** is a secret input, a number, a word or a sequence of buttons, that changes a game's rules: more lives, a level select, invulnerability, all the weapons. Unlike a POKE, which alters the program from outside, a cheat code is something the game itself was written to recognise.

## Put there for testing

Very often the person who wrote it in was the programmer, who needed a way through their own game. *Wireframe* reported in 2022 that *Manic Miner*'s number was based on [Matthew Smith](/vault/people/matthew-smith)'s driving licence. When the game moved to [Software Projects](/vault/companies/software-projects), the code changed with it. In September 1984 *C&VG*'s tips columnist, who admitted to being confused too, set out "the codes for the last time": 6031769 for the Bug-Byte tape, TYPEWRITER for the Software Projects one, and WRITETYPER for *[Jet Set Willy](/vault/games/jet-set-willy)*.

Kazuhisa Hashimoto put what *Wireframe* calls "the most famous cheat code of all" into Konami's Famicom *[Gradius](/vault/games/gradius)* in 1986 because, as he later said, he could not finish the game himself. Andrew Braybrook, author of *[Paradroid](/vault/games/paradroid)*, told *Wireframe* that publishers sometimes pushed developers to add cheat modes "so they could see that the game is all there, or skip past a bit that wasn't working properly", and might then insist the cheat stayed in the final master. Once a code was in a released game, he added, "it's going to get published sooner or later".

*Wireframe* suggests an earlier candidate for the first cheat code: XYZZY, the magic word in *[Colossal Cave Adventure](/vault/games/colossal-cave-adventure)*, which carries the player between two distant locations and saves a long walk back with the treasure.

## How the codes travelled

The magazines ran on readers' discoveries, and they printed the finders. *[CRASH](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine)*'s tips pages in August 1984 signed off each tip with a name and a town, David Fortune of Bramley, Leeds, or Kris Hammond from Whitchurch, Bristol, and *C&VG* told readers they had "Simon Stokes from Erdington, Birmingham to thank" for one of its tips. *[Nintendo Power](/vault/magazines/nintendo-power)*'s Classified Information column did it differently: its tips came "From Agent #312" or "From Agent #010", a numbered secret agent rather than a named reader. Its January 1993 column gave the [Konami Code](/vault/culture/konami-code) a heading of its own and described it as "a long-standing code which makes for special circumstances in several games": Up, Up, Down, Down, Left, Right, Left, Right, B, A, Start.

The telephone was the next channel. By 1991 British magazines carried adverts for "Dial-a-Tip" cheat lines on premium-rate 0898 numbers, "updated weekly". One in *[Amiga Action](/vault/magazines/amiga-action)* in June 1991 charged 33p a minute cheap rate and 44p at other times, and printed the line "PLEASE GET PERMISSION TO RING". [Hint Lines](/vault/culture/hint-lines) follows that business.

The playground needed no adverts. In April 1994 the German magazine *[PC Player](/vault/magazines/pc-player)* opened an editorial by asking what the most frequently asked question in Germany's school playgrounds was, and answered: "Kennst du schon den Blood Code für Mortal Kombat?", do you know the blood code for *[Mortal Kombat](/vault/games/mortal-kombat)* yet? On the Mega Drive version, pressing A, B, A, C, A, B, B at the code screen unlocked the blood and the fatal finishing moves that the conversion otherwise hid. The German *Video Games* had printed it in November 1993 as the "Gore Code"; the American fanzine *Digital Press* listed it the same year as the "Quick Blood Code".

## Codes without translation

A cheat code is a literal string, so it crossed borders unchanged. id Software's *[Doom](/vault/games/doom)* (1993) answered to typed words such as IDDQD, for invulnerability, and IDKFA, for every weapon and key. Germany's *PC Player* explained IDDQD in February 1994 ("Mit »IDDQD« machen Sie sich unverwundbar", with IDDQD you make yourself invulnerable), and *[Computer Gaming World](/vault/magazines/computer-gaming-world)* did the same in April. The Italian *[MC Microcomputer](/vault/magazines/mc-microcomputer)* called them "famosi ormai", famous by now, in February 1995. That January the Russian magazine *Games* printed the *Doom II* codes and, beside them, the codes for *Heretic*, where the same two strings had become traps: IDDQD gave instant death and IDKFA took every weapon away.

Some codes outlived their games. More than a decade after *Manic Miner*, DMA Design's *[Grand Theft Auto](/vault/games/grand-theft-auto)* (1997) accepted 6031769 as a cheat when typed in as the player's name. *C&VG* and the Australian *PC PowerPlay* both listed it in June 1998.

## The Konami Code

The Konami Code, ↑ ↑ ↓ ↓ ← → ← → B A, began as Hashimoto's shortcut in the Famicom *Gradius* in April 1986. The NES *[Contra](/vault/games/contra)* made it famous in America as the way to start with 30 lives, and by 1991 *Nintendo Power* was calling it "the Konami Code". Its history has [its own entry](/vault/culture/konami-code).

## Fewer codes

*Wireframe* describes the 1990s and early 2000s as "a golden era for the popularity of cheat codes and unlockable secrets", with tips magazines, official guides and websites such as [GameFAQs](/vault/culture/gamefaqs). Online play, *Wireframe* argues, made cheating less acceptable, because one player's advantage came at other players' expense. It also suggests that button-press codes became rare because they "can mess up carefully designed achievement systems", and some publishers began to sell unlocks instead. Some games kept them anyway. *Wireframe* names *Fallout: New Vegas*, *The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim* and *Grand Theft Auto V*, which let players cheat but switch off trophies and achievements while they do.
