The [Commodore 64](/vault/systems/commodore-64) was the same machine
everywhere. Same processor, same 64K, same
[SID](/vault/hardware/sid-chip), same [VIC-II](/vault/hardware/vic-ii). Any
account of it built from one country's press will describe that machine
accurately and still be wrong about what it was, because everything that made
owning one different from owning something else was local — and only the local
press wrote it down.

This entry compares the C64 as it appears in six national presses held in this
project's library.

## It did not arrive everywhere at once

Only two magazines here were publishing *before* the C64 existed, so only two can
say when it turned up.

| | Publishing since | First names the C64 |
|---|---|---|
| *[MC-microcomputer](/vault/magazines/mc-microcomputer)* (Italy) | September 1981 | **March 1982** |
| *[Micro Sistemas](/vault/magazines/micro-sistemas)* (Brazil) | October 1981 | **July 1983** |

Italy has it before it shipped — March 1982 is the announcement, not the
product. Brazil takes until July 1983, eleven months after buyers elsewhere
could have one.

And the manner of the Brazilian arrival is the useful part. It is not a review,
a preview or an advertisement. It is a **reader's letter** about a Commodore
users' club, from a correspondent who belongs to the *Toronto PET Users Group*.
The C64 enters Brazil's leading computer magazine as something a reader has heard
about from abroad.

That is what [an import-substitution
market](/vault/culture/brazilian-market-reserve) looks like from the inside. A
machine you cannot legally import is not news.

## Nowhere the leading machine

Counting how often each machine is named across the text of each title, in the
general (not single-platform) press:

| Country | Title | Leading machine | C64 share |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Italy | *MC-microcomputer* | **PC 33%** | 16% |
| Brazil | *Micro Sistemas* | **PC 39%**, MSX 29% | *outside top five* |
| Greece | *[Pixel](/vault/magazines/pixel)* | **Amstrad 40%**, Spectrum 38% | 6% |
| Poland | *[Bajtek](/vault/magazines/bajtek)* | **Spectrum 39%** | 13% |
| Yugoslavia | *[Računari](/vault/magazines/racunari)* | **PC 40%**, Amstrad 25% | 5% |
| Yugoslavia | *Svet Kompjutera* | **Spectrum 29%**, PC 28% | **19%** |

The C64 leads in none of them — but look at the two Yugoslav rows before
trusting any single one.

## One magazine is not one country

*Računari* and *Svet Kompjutera* were both general Yugoslav computer magazines,
both published in Belgrade, both measured over 1984–87. They do not agree.

| | *Računari* (n=540) | *Svet Kompjutera* (n=1,433) |
|---|---:|---:|
| PC | 40% | 28% |
| Spectrum | 17% | **29%** |
| C64 | **5%** | **19%** |
| Amstrad | 25% | 15% |

Nearly four times the C64 share in one than the other, from the same country in
the same years. *Računari* leans technical and business; *Svet Kompjutera* leans
toward games. Both are "the general press" and neither is wrong.

**Poland now shows the same thing.** *Bajtek*, a supplement to the official youth
newspaper, gives the Spectrum 34% and the PC 15%. *Mikroklan*, which began as an
inset in the professional journal *Informatyka*, gives the PC **58%** and the
Spectrum 15% — same country, same years. The divergence tracks the magazine's
parent institution, not its nation; see
[institutional computer press](/vault/culture/institutional-computer-press).

⚠ **This is the most important caveat on this page, and it was found by
accident.** Yugoslavia was the first territory here represented by two titles,
and Poland is now the second.
Every other row — Italy, Brazil, Greece, Poland — rests on **one magazine**, and
the Yugoslav pair shows how far two magazines from one country can diverge. The
rows should be read as *what this title covered*, not *what that country used*.

The comparison survives that, and this has now been tested rather than assumed.

## The two load-bearing results, checked against second titles

Italy, Brazil and Greece each hold further titles in this library. Measured over
a wider 1982–90 window, they say:

| Country | Title | Leading machines |
|---|---|---|
| Italy | *MC-microcomputer* | PC 32%, Amiga 23%, **C64 14%** |
| Italy | *K* | Amiga 46%, **C64 21%**, Spectrum 13% |
| Brazil | *Micro Sistemas* | PC 37%, **MSX 31%**, Spectrum 13%, ZX81 12% |
| Brazil | *CPU* | **MSX 93%** |
| Brazil | *Input* | **ZX81 37%**, Spectrum 36%, MSX 23% |
| Greece | *Pixel* | **Amstrad 41%**, Spectrum 25% |
| Greece | *PC Master* | **Amstrad 65%** |

Both load-bearing results hold. **The C64 appears in no Brazilian top four
across three independent titles**, and MSX is prominent in all three. **Both
Greek titles lead with the Amstrad**, the second more emphatically than the
first.

Italy's two titles differ on ordering — *K* is an Amiga-era magazine — but agree
the C64 sits third or lower.

⚠ These are not all general magazines: *CPU* is MSX-focused and *Input* Sinclair-
focused, which is why their spread is so wide. What makes their agreement useful
is that titles with *opposite* platform loyalties still fail to put the C64 near
the top in Brazil. The window here is also wider than the table above, so these
percentages are not directly comparable with it.

No single percentage on this page should be quoted as a national figure. The
patterns that survive multiple titles can be.

Two of those results are worth sitting with.

**Brazil's second machine is the MSX**, at 29% — ahead of the Spectrum and the
ZX81, and far ahead of the Commodore. MSX was built in Brazil under licence by
Gradiente and Sharp. The ZX81 and Apple II, the next most prominent, were the
machines Brazilian firms cloned. The pattern is not a preference; it is a list
of what could legally be manufactured.

**Greece leads with the Amstrad**, at 40%, ahead of the Spectrum — an ordering
that does not hold in Britain, where the Amstrad ran third behind Spectrum and
Commodore.

## One machine, three prices, one country

The clearest single artefact of the whole comparison is a price column in
*[Bajtek](/vault/magazines/bajtek)*. Its February 1989 table carries three
headings over the same list of machines:

| GIEŁDA „BAJTKA" | PEWEX / BALTONA | RFN |
|---|---|---|
| thousands of złoty | US dollars | Deutschmarks |

The open market, the hard-currency state shops, and West Germany. A British
magazine printed one price because there was one way to buy a computer; a Polish
one printed three because which price applied to you depended on what money you
could get.

A C-64C is listed at 650 on the market that week. The same table puts a
Spectrum 48K at 90–190 DM across the western border.

## Seven routes to the same silicon

Set the territories side by side and what varies is not the machine but the
arrangement by which a person came to have one.

| Territory | How the machine reached people |
|---|---|
| **Britain** | Imported and sold retail. One price, one channel. |
| **Italy, Greece, Spain** | Imported through a commercial market, covered by a commercial press. |
| **Argentina** | **Built locally under licence.** The product is *[la Drean Commodore 64](/vault/magazines/drean-commodore)* — the licensee's name is in the machine's name. |
| **Brazil** | Imports restricted, so no licence and barely a C64. The domestic industry [built MSX and cloned the ZX81 and Apple II](/vault/culture/brazilian-market-reserve) instead. |
| **Poland** | Three parallel prices — open market in złoty, hard-currency shops in dollars, West Germany in Deutschmarks. |
| **Yugoslavia** | A magazine [printed a machine you built yourself](/vault/magazines/racunari). |
| **East Germany** | A [state-licensed journal](/vault/magazines/mikroprozessortechnik) teaching the domestic stack — the Soviet 8086 clone, the GDR's own dBASE. |

The specification is a constant across that table. Everything else — price,
legality, whose name is on the case, whether you bought it or soldered it — is
local, and none of it is visible from the hardware.

⚠ These are seven cases this library can document, not a claim that there were
seven kinds. Countries whose press is not held here will have had arrangements of
their own.

## What this does not show

⚠ **Mention share is press attention, not market share.** A magazine names a
machine when it reviews it, advertises it, or argues about it. Nothing here
counts a single unit sold.

⚠ **Editorial focus dominates the number.** A single-platform magazine reports
its own platform by construction: *Commodore Computer Club* in Italy gives the
C64 67%, and that says nothing about Italy. Only general magazines are compared
above, and the one single-platform British title is shown alongside as a control
— *ZZAP!64*, a C64 magazine, still names the Spectrum exactly as often as the
C64.

⚠ **The windows are not identical.** Italy, Brazil, Greece, Poland and
Yugoslavia are measured 1982–87 or as much of it as each title covers, but the
titles begin in different years and no adjustment is made for that.

⚠ **This library is not the world.** The territories compared are those whose
magazines have been scanned and uploaded, which reflects the interests of
present-day preservation communities rather than the size of past markets.
Countries absent from this table may have had a thriving press; their absence
here is evidence about archives, not about computing.
