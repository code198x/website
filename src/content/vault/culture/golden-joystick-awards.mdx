In July 1983 *[Computer & Video Games](/vault/magazines/computer-and-video-games)* told its readers that it wanted to give "the best of British" a chance "to prove itself". It would award five Golden Joysticks to British software houses that autumn, and the readers would pick the winners. The magazine hoped the prizes would "become the Oscars of our industry". More than forty years later they are still being handed out, which makes them one of the oldest awards in video games, and for most of that time the winners have been chosen by public vote.

## Fast facts

- **Started by:** *Computer & Video Games* (*C&VG*), published by [EMAP](/vault/companies/emap). Announced in the July 1983 issue.
- **Chosen by:** readers' votes, sent in on coupons cut from the magazine; online voting today.
- **First Game of the Year:** *[Jetpac](/vault/games/jetpac)* by [Ultimate Play the Game](/vault/companies/ultimate), for 1983.
- **First ceremony:** a dinner at Mortons in Berkeley Square, London, early in 1984, hosted by the Radio One DJ Dave Lee Travis.
- **Today:** run by Future, with public voting and a ceremony in London each November.

## A coupon and five categories

The first awards had five categories. Best Arcade-style Game would go to the most "addictive and thrilling" game of 1983. Best Strategy Game was for a game "to test the mind rather than the trigger-finger", and Best Original Game for the most original idea. Software House of the Year was for "the best reputation for service, quality game and entertaining adverts". *C&VG* promised to check readers' suggestions with dealers and to "set some devious tests on the service side" for the main contenders. The fifth award, Game of the Year, was "the most coveted Golden Joystick" of all.

Readers voted on a coupon printed in the magazine and posted it to *C&VG* at Durrant House in London. The first announcement planned to present the awards at the Brainwave consumer electronics exhibition in November 1983. In the event, *C&VG* announced the winners in its March 1984 issue and reported the dinner in April. It said that "literally hundreds of games for all the popular micros" had been nominated.

The results for 1983:

- **Game of the Year:** *Jetpac* (Ultimate), ahead of *[The Hobbit](/vault/games/the-hobbit)* ([Melbourne House](/vault/companies/melbourne-house)), *[Manic Miner](/vault/games/manic-miner)* ([Bug-Byte](/vault/companies/bug-byte)) and *Arcadia* ([Imagine](/vault/companies/imagine-software)).
- **Software House of the Year:** Ultimate, ahead of Melbourne House, Imagine and [Llamasoft](/vault/companies/llamasoft).
- **Best Arcade-style Game:** *Manic Miner*.
- **Best Strategy Game:** *The Hobbit*, ahead of *[Football Manager](/vault/games/football-manager-1982)* ([Addictive Games](/vault/companies/addictive-games)).
- **Best Original Game:** Imagine's *Ah Diddums*, ahead of Ultimate's *Pssst* and [Quicksilva](/vault/companies/quicksilva)'s *[Ant Attack](/vault/games/ant-attack)*.

At Mortons, Bug-Byte's John Phillips and [Matthew Smith](/vault/people/matthew-smith) collected the arcade award for *Manic Miner*. Carol Ward accepted Game of the Year for Ultimate, and [Tim Stamper](/vault/people/stamper-brothers) came up for Software House of the Year. Addictive Games put its nomination into its advertisements for *Football Manager*.

Not every reader liked the idea. In June 1984 an Atari owner wrote to complain that the prizes were bound to go to whoever made games for the Spectrum.

## Riverboats, barracks and roof gardens

The awards grew each year. Adventure and Programmer of the Year categories were added for 1984, and readers were told the closing date was 31 December. *C&VG* reported in June 1985 that "several thousand" readers had voted, and that Ultimate had "swept the board". *[Knight Lore](/vault/games/knight-lore)* was Game of the Year, with Activision's *Ghostbusters* runner-up. Ultimate was Software House of the Year, "the Ultimate team" won Programmer of the Year, and Louise Stamper collected the awards at a lunch in London. [Beyond](/vault/companies/beyond-software)'s *[Lords of Midnight](/vault/games/lords-of-midnight)* won Best Strategy Game, *[Elite](/vault/games/elite)* Best Original Game, and [Ocean](/vault/companies/ocean-software)'s *Daley Thompson's Decathlon* Best Arcade-style Game. A special award went to Softaid.

For the 1985 awards *C&VG* "pushed the boat out — literally", hiring a Thames riverboat for an afternoon with champagne and a meal. Jools Holland of *The Tube* presented the awards with the editor, Tim Metcalfe, "resplendent in bright red suede shoes". Melbourne House ended Ultimate's run by taking both top awards: Game of the Year for *[The Way of the Exploding Fist](/vault/games/way-of-the-exploding-fist)* and Software House of the Year. Paula Byrne and Geoff Heath collected them. Steven Crow was Programmer of the Year for *Wizard's Lair* and *Starquake*, ahead of [Jeff Minter](/vault/people/jeff-minter).

In 1987, for the 1986 awards, the magazine "enlisted the help of the army" and took over Cadogan Hall at the Duke of York's barracks on the King's Road, followed by a visit to the Limelight nightclub. Holland presented the awards for the third time. [US Gold](/vault/companies/us-gold)'s *[Gauntlet](/vault/games/gauntlet)* was Game of the Year, [Elite Systems](/vault/companies/elite-systems) Software House of the Year, and [Andrew Braybrook](/vault/people/andrew-braybrook) Programmer of the Year; his *[Uridium](/vault/games/uridium)* won the arcade award. A new soundtrack category went to [Rob Hubbard](/vault/people/rob-hubbard) for *Sanxion*.

On Wednesday 9 March 1988 the awards moved to the Roof Gardens in Kensington, with Chris Tarrant as host. *C&VG* counted "a record six thousand entries", and called the event the "5th birthday of Britain's premier computer games awards". *[Out Run](/vault/games/out-run)* won Game of the Year and US Gold Software House of the Year; [Jon Ritman](/vault/people/jon-ritman) was Programmer of the Year, and [Hewson](/vault/companies/hewson-consultants)'s *[Nebulus](/vault/games/nebulus)* Best Original Game. The strategy award went to *Vulcan* from CCS. "'What?' went the critics. 'Who?' said the software people. Just goes to show how little we know."

## EMAP's awards

In 1989 EMAP opened the vote to readers of its other magazines, *[Sinclair User](/vault/magazines/sinclair-user)*, *Commodore User* and *[The One](/vault/magazines/the-one)*, and added awards sponsored by individual titles. Many prizes were now split between 8-bit and 16-bit games. At the Roof Gardens on 6 April 1989, again with Chris Tarrant, Ocean's *Operation Wolf* was 8-bit Game of the Year and the [Bitmap Brothers](/vault/companies/bitmap-brothers)' *Speedball* 16-bit Game of the Year. Ocean and [Mirrorsoft](/vault/companies/mirrorsoft) were the 8-bit and 16-bit software houses of the year. Sega's *Thunder Blade* won the C&VG Console Award.

By 1991 the magazine called the event "EMAP Image's Golden Joystick Awards", held on 4 April at the Kensington Roof Gardens; [Anco](/vault/companies/anco)'s *Kick Off 2* was 16-bit Game of the Year and there were separate awards for PC and console games. The following year's event was billed as the "Tenth EMAP Golden Joystick Awards", hosted by Jonathan Ross, and added a Hand-Held Game of the Year, won by *R-Type* on the Game Boy.

By 1997 readers of all EMAP's games magazines could vote. That year's awards were sponsored by HMV "in association with Nintendo 64 and Sony PlayStation", and readers voted on magazine categories such as Best-Looking Pages and Scoop of the Year as well as on games. *Sega Saturn Magazine* described the ceremony that November at the Café de Paris in London, and still called the Joysticks "the Oscars of the videogame industry".

## Later years

By October 2001 *C&VG* belonged to Dennis Publishing. The awards did not appear every year around then: public sources date the unbroken run of ceremonies from 2002. *[Edge](/vault/magazines/edge)* printed the 2004 winners, with *Doom 3* as Ultimate Game of the Year, and reported in 2005 that the awards, staged in association with *The Sun*, BBC Radio One, The Carphone Warehouse and others, had collected more than 200,000 votes in 19 categories. Jimmy Carr hosted, and *Grand Theft Auto: San Andreas* won five awards.

Today Future plc runs the Golden Joystick Awards, and votes are cast online through its website GamesRadar+. At the 2025 ceremony in London, in what *The A.V. Club* called the awards' 43rd year, *Clair Obscur: Expedition 33* won Ultimate Game of the Year and six other awards. The 2026 ceremony is scheduled for 11 November at the Troxy in London.
