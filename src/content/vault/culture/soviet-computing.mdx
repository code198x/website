In a letter that *CRASH*, the British Spectrum magazine, printed in February 1989, a 15-year-old in Arkhangelsk explained how Soviet teenagers got hold of a computer. "Because there is a shortage of good computers in our country, almost all Soviets who like computer games and programming must make little computers themselves," he wrote. His own had an 8080 processor, 2K of EPROM and 32K of RAM, built to a design "taken from a Soviet hobby magazine". He was writing the letter on a home-built ZX Spectrum, "in 'Russian style'", made by a friend. Personal computing in the Soviet Union grew up like that: from circuit diagrams in magazines, a few factory machines that were hard to buy, computer clubs that charged by the hour, and a school subject that most schools had no computers to teach.

## Fast facts

- **Hobbyist designs:** the Micro-80 (published in *Radio*, 1982–83) and the Radio-86RK (*Radio*, 1986), both built round the KR580 processor, a Soviet copy of Intel's 8080.
- **Factory machines:** the Agat (shown at a Moscow trade fair in July 1983), modelled on the Apple II; the Elektronika BK-0010 (described in 1985), with the instruction set of the Elektronika 60 minicomputer.
- **Schools:** a course in the "Fundamentals of Informatics and Computing Technology" in every secondary school from the 1985–86 school year.
- **Spectrum clones:** home-built and factory-made, among them the Pentagon 128, the Scorpion and the Byte.
- **Best-known program:** *[Tetris](/vault/games/tetris)*, written by [Alexey Pajitnov](/vault/people/alexey-pajitnov) at the Computer Centre of the USSR Academy of Sciences, 1984 by most accounts.

## Computers from a magazine

The monthly *Radio*, the magazine for the Soviet Union's radio amateurs, started a series on microprocessors and microcomputers in September 1982. Its introduction came from the rector of the Moscow Institute of Electronic Engineering, and the editors wrote that they meant to bring radio amateurs into the field "since the specialists say: the future belongs to microprocessors!" Over 1982 and 1983 the magazine published a complete personal computer, the Micro-80.

Few were built. When *Radio* introduced its successor in April 1986, it explained why: the Micro-80 needed more than 200 chips, the magazine had published no drawings of its circuit boards, and, above all, the chips were not on sale in the shops. The new design, the Radio-86RK, cut the count to 29 chips on a single board, whose layout the editors had tested by building one in the office. It used a KR580 processor, a 2K ROM holding the monitor program and 16K or 32K of RAM, with a cassette recorder for storage and a television for a screen. Even then, the editors admitted, it was "still difficult to obtain these 29 chips", and they hoped industry would sell them as a kit.

The letter to *CRASH* shows what happened next. Its writer's machine, with its 8080, 2K EPROM and 32K of RAM and an 8275 video controller, matches the Radio-86RK, and he found it worked "pretty well, but has poor graphics". So, he wrote, "many of us are looking for a design with better graphics, and containing components available in Russia. A year ago many chose the Sinclair ZX Spectrum model", although "some rare special ICs like the ULA controller are not available here".

## Factory computers

Soviet factories made personal computers too, but they were expensive and hard to find.

**The Agat.** *BYTE* published an American eye surgeon's account of the Agat in November 1984. Leo Bores had used one for a week in August 1983, after a prototype was shown at a Moscow trade fair that July. Its operating system and ROM, he wrote, "seemed to be a direct lift from the Apple computer, with only a few minor differences", and he dubbed it the "yablocka (Russian for apple)". *BYTE*'s standfirst was less polite: "a bad copy of the Apple". It was a big machine with a disk drive built in, a Russian typewriter keyboard and a SECAM colour television for a monitor.

**The Elektronika BK-0010.** The journal *Mikroprotsessornye sredstva i sistemy* described the BK-0010 in its first issue of 1985 as a "household personal microcomputer", meant to be a home information centre for games, calculations, reference files and educational programs, and to prepare schoolchildren to work with computers. It plugged into a household television and cassette recorder, had a 16-bit K1801VM1 processor, 32K of RAM and 32K of ROM, and ran the language FOCAL from a plug-in ROM. Its instruction set and architecture were compatible with the Elektronika 60, the minicomputer on which Pajitnov wrote *Tetris*; *Amiga Format* later called its processor "a PDP-11 clone, based on DEC's minicomputer design". It was made from 1985 into the early 1990s.

**What they cost.** In 1989 the Polish magazine *Bajtek* visited Agat, a computer club in Moscow on Dmitrovskaya Street, founded in 1986 and owned by the Lianozovo works, a factory that assembled about 3,000 Agats a year. An hour in the room of Mikroshas, a factory-made version of the Radio-86RK, cost 50 kopecks; an hour on an Agat cost 1 rouble 20 kopecks. The club also hired computers out by the day. A Mikrosha cost about 500 roubles in the shops, "if it gets there at all", and Agats had recently sold officially for 4,900 roubles. A Soviet survey quoted in the article found that only 14.6 per cent of computer-club members had a computer at home, most often a BK-0010 or a Japanese Yamaha.

## Informatics without computers

From the school year that began in September 1985, a resolution of the Communist Party's Central Committee and the Council of Ministers put a course called "Fundamentals of Informatics and Computing Technology" into every Soviet secondary school. *Radio* reported it alongside a story about schoolboys at a young technicians' station in Kiev, who had adapted the simplest and cheapest pocket calculator, the B3-23, into timers and other gadgets.

The machines did not follow quickly. When the American "Information USA" exhibition toured the Soviet Union from June 1987, *Family Computing* sent a writer, Steve Morgenstern, to Moscow. An expert told him that computers for schools were scarce, "with little prospect for short-term improvement". "Computer science is taught," Morgenstern wrote, "but it is largely an abstract, conceptual topic; the students lack machines to try their hands at programming." A Moscow editor had managed to get his 13-year-old son into an after-school computer class that used half a dozen Yamaha MSX computers. Blank 3.5-inch floppy disks sold on the black market for 20 to 50 roubles each, and visitors argued over whether you could really walk into a shop and buy a computer: some said yes, others quoted waits "from six months to a lifetime".

## The Spectrum takes over

Western home computers came in through tourists and the black market. The letter to *CRASH* reported that Spectrums bought abroad were resold "for more than 2,000 roubles", which the magazine reckoned at about nine months' average wages, and that the authorities had "banned or limited the purchase of computers abroad". Alessandro Grussu's history of the Spectrum describes the same pattern across the Eastern bloc: Spectrums "often smuggled from the West" spread "due to their low cost and ease of use", until "local microelectronics companies made 'their' versions of the machine".

The ULA, the Spectrum's one custom chip, could not be bought in the Soviet Union, so clones such as the Byte did its work with ordinary TTL logic chips instead. Looking back in 2005, *Retro Gamer*'s John Szczepaniak found that the Pentagon 128 had been the most popular of the first Soviet clones, often hand-built and sometimes "little more than a collection of circuit boards and wires", with the Scorpion 256 and the Kay 1024 following. About 80 per cent of the clones, he wrote, had a Beta Disk floppy interface built in and ran the TR-DOS disk system rather than loading from tape. Factories made clones too: the Byte came from the Brest electromechanical works in Byelorussia from about 1990. The TZX format, used to preserve Spectrum tapes, still gives the Pentagon 128 and the Scorpion their own hardware codes.

## Tetris and after

The best-known Soviet program came out of a research institute, not a hobby magazine. Pajitnov wrote *Tetris* on an Elektronika 60 at the Computer Centre of the USSR Academy of Sciences, where the machine could draw the pieces only with square brackets; a 16-year-old, Vadim Gerasimov, ported it to the IBM PC, and it spread from there. The Vault's entries on [Tetris](/vault/games/tetris) and the [Tetris legal battles](/vault/phenomena/tetris-legal-battles) tell how the rights, held by the Soviet state, were sold in the West.

The machines outlived the Soviet Union. In 2005 *Retro Gamer* found the Russian Spectrum scene "still big", alongside the Dendy, a Famicom clone whose name, the magazine wrote, "would become synonymous with gaming". Russian developers kept making puzzle games: in 1995 the Moscow *Games Magazine* listed *Tetris*, *Biprolex* and *Color Lines* among Russia's successes. *Color Lines*, a PC game, travelled as far as Yugoslavia, where two Amiga programmers released *Lineae Coloris*, "based on the PC game: Color Lines". *Amiga Format* described it in 1997: counters of the same colour lined up in fives on a 9×9 board, and three new ones arriving after every move that did not complete a line.
