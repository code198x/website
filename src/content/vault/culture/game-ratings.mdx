The first game to carry an age on its box was not a fighting game or a shooter. It was *Dracula*, a text adventure, which the British Board of Film Classification gave a 15 certificate at the end of 1986 because its author, Rod Pike, had warned his publisher that parts of it were horrific. For the next seven years a rated game was a curiosity. Then, in 1993 and 1994, a run of controversies, led by *[Mortal Kombat](/vault/games/mortal-kombat)* and the [US Senate hearings](/vault/culture/congressional-hearings-1993) that followed it, pushed the industry into rating everything. What came out was not one system but many. Each country built its own, and each was shaped by its own laws: in America the labels were voluntary, in Britain and Germany some ratings carried the force of law, and in Australia games that needed an adult rating could not be sold at all.

## Fast facts

- **First rated game:** CRL's *Dracula*, 15 certificate from the British Board of Film Classification (BBFC), 1986
- **Germany:** games could be put on an "index" of material harmful to young people from the 1980s; the industry's Unterhaltungssoftware Selbstkontrolle (USK) gave advisory age ratings from 1994, legally binding from 2003
- **United States:** Sega's own Videogame Rating Council (1993), then the [ESRB](/vault/culture/esrb) (1994), voluntary
- **Britain:** voluntary ELSPA labels (1994–2003), then PEGI (from 2003), with BBFC certificates required by law for some games; PEGI 12, 16 and 18 legally enforceable from 2012
- **Japan:** the Computer Entertainment Rating Organization (CERO), from 2002
- **Australia:** government classification, with no adult category for games until R18+ arrived on 1 January 2013

## Britain: the law came first

Britain's Video Recordings Act of 1984 was written for videos, but it could reach games too. Most games were exempt, *Commodore User* explained in December 1986, with exceptions "such as games which depict sex, sexual organs, excretory functions or violence towards animals". *Dracula*, written in the first person, had the player die repeatedly and quoted Bram Stoker, so CRL took legal advice and submitted it. The magazine called it "the first case of certification in the entire twelve months the act has been in existence", and asked the Board's director, James Freeman, whether other games should have been submitted too. "Possibly," he said, "but our role is limited. It is not for us to prosecute or even to hunt material down." CRL went on to win the first 18 certificate for a game, for *Jack the Ripper* in 1987 (see [CRL](/vault/companies/crl-group)).

The industry's own labels arrived in April 1994. The European Leisure Software Publishers Association (ELSPA) introduced four age bands, 0 to 10, 11 to 14, 15 to 17 and 18 plus, for its 60 member companies to print on their boxes. Geoff Brown, chairman of US Gold, called them "a major step forward in assisting parents to choose appropriate games for their children". *[ST Format](/vault/magazines/st-format)* noted that the labels were not legally binding, and suspected the scheme was a way to delay a compulsory one.

So for most of the 1990s British games could carry two different marks. A reader who wrote to *PC Zone* in 2001 to correct the magazine's editor put it plainly: "The ELSPA rating is advisory and voluntary. The BBFC is legally binding." A demo of a game that ELSPA had restricted but the BBFC had not rated was therefore, he argued, "legal to distribute to any age".

In April 2003 ELSPA's labels gave way to PEGI, the Pan European Game Information system, used in sixteen countries and run by the Netherlands Institute for the Classification of Audiovisual Media. Its age labels are 3, 7, 12, 16 and 18, with pictograms for the content: *Edge* listed a spider for horror, a syringe for drugs and a clenched fist for violence. PEGI too was voluntary at first. That changed on 30 July 2012, when the Video Standards Council took over rating games in Britain under PEGI and selling a game rated 12, 16 or 18 to a younger child became a criminal offence. The [ESRB](/vault/culture/esrb) entry tells the British story in more detail.

## Germany: the index and the USK

Germany had a law that could restrict games before it had any ratings. *[Computer Gaming World](/vault/magazines/computer-gaming-world)* explained to American readers in November 1987 that under a law first passed in 1953, works "deemed to be 'immoral, brutalizing, inciting violence, crime, racial hatred or which glorify war'" could be put on an index. An indexed game could not be advertised or displayed, and could be sold only where minors were not allowed in. "Only 30 computer titles have been banned," the magazine reported, "among them [Activision](/vault/companies/activision)'s *River Raid*", and several games from the American publisher Microprose had been restricted too.

The publishers' association, the VUD, set up a voluntary rating body, the USK, in 1994. Its trial run went badly. *[PC Player](/vault/magazines/pc-player)* reported in July 1994 that a panel of Berlin psychologists, paid 1,000 marks a game, had passed Microprose's war simulations at 12 but rated a peaceful economic simulation, *Der Planer*, 16, because younger players would not understand its economics. A year later the system was working. *[Amiga Joker](/vault/magazines/amiga-joker)* wrote in April 1995 that the USK had been rating games for almost exactly a year, with yellow-and-white stickers, and that apart from a few early oddities, such as *[Monkey Island](/vault/games/monkey-island) 2* being rated 12, its decisions were sound. Its ratings were still only recommendations, though; unlike the film board's, they had no legal standing. The magazine also suspected that publishers liked the stickers for another reason: they were a weapon against importers who sold unrated games.

To reach a lower rating, or to be sold at all, publishers often changed their German releases. In September 1995 *PC Player*'s editors argued in print over the German *[Command & Conquer](/vault/games/command-and-conquer)*, whose infantry had been turned into cyborgs. In 2003, after a debate about violent games that followed the Erfurt school shooting of 2002, a new youth protection law made USK ratings part of a state-approved system with legal force.

## Japan: CERO

Japan's industry body, the Computer Entertainment Supplier's Association, set up the Computer Entertainment Rating Organization in June 2002. Its first ratings were bands of age, the oldest being 18 and over. In 2006 it replaced them with letters: A for all ages, B for 12 and over, C for 15, D for 17 and Z for 18. Koji Aizawa of *Famitsu PS2* explained to *Edge* readers what Z meant in practice. Shops could sell Z games only from a dedicated corner or from behind the counter, under a sign that had to be visible from 1.5 metres away, and some prefectures required buyers to show identification. Japan's big convenience-store chains said they would not stock Z games at all. Among the first eleven Z-rated games were *[Grand Theft Auto](/vault/games/grand-theft-auto): San Andreas* and *Max Payne 2*.

## Australia: no adult rating

In Australia games were classified by the government, not by the industry, and until 2013 the highest category a game could have was MA15+. A game judged too strong for that was refused classification and could not be sold. A reader complained to the Australian *Official PlayStation 2 Magazine* in January 2003 that *Grand Theft Auto: Vice City* had been cut "because there is no R18+ rating", and that "in the UK and the US, the industry decides the ratings for games, but noooo, in Australia we have to do things differently by having the government decide the ratings." In 2005 *Hyper* reported that Midway's *NARC* had been refused classification, "in news that surprised no one", and called for the push for an adult rating to start again. It took until 1 January 2013. The first game given the new R18+ was *Ninja Gaiden 3: Razor's Edge*.

## North America: the industry rates itself

The American story began with [Sega](/vault/companies/sega), whose Videogame Rating Council rated its games from the summer of 1993. After the Senate hearing of December 1993, where *Mortal Kombat* and *Night Trap* were shown, the industry set up the Entertainment Software Rating Board, whose ratings were in the shops for Christmas 1994. Its labels carry no legal force, but they matter: console makers and big retailers refused to handle Adults Only games, so an AO rating works almost like a ban. When a California law tried to restrict sales of violent games to children, the US Supreme Court struck it down in 2011, ruling that games are protected speech. The [ESRB](/vault/culture/esrb), [Congressional Hearings 1993](/vault/culture/congressional-hearings-1993) and [Mortal Kombat Controversy](/vault/culture/mortal-kombat-controversy) entries tell that story in full.
