In early 2011 the best-selling paid program on Apple's new Mac App Store was a puzzle game called *Braid*, at £2.99. *Edge* found it seventh by revenue as well, between two of Apple's own office programs. Its designer, Jonathan Blow, had first released it on Microsoft's Xbox Live Arcade in 2008. He saw the Mac store as the latest of a growing number of platforms that let developers release their games independently, and told *Edge* that "an independent developer can make plenty of sales without trying to get on to the console platforms like XBLA and PSN. It's a huge pain to go through the approval and certification process for one of the consoles, but Steam, Mac and the iPad are much more lightweight."

Games made by one person or a small team and sold without a publisher are called indie games. The practice is as old as the home computer. What changed in the 2000s was that downloads let such games reach the whole world, and "indie" became the name of a movement.

## Before the word

Home computer programmers sold their own games from the start. [Jeff Minter](/vault/people/jeff-minter) formed [Llamasoft](/vault/companies/llamasoft) in 1982 and sold his games by mail order and at computer shows from his parents' house. In 1991 he gave away *Llamatron* as [shareware](/vault/distribution/shareware) and asked players who kept it to send him £5. [Public domain](/vault/distribution/public-domain) libraries spread free games by post, and one of them, 17-Bit Software in Wakefield, turned into [Team17](/vault/companies/team17). *Amiga Power* called its founders "ex-PD people".

As games grew more expensive to make and to put in the shops, going it alone got harder. At the end of 2002, in a feature on studio closures, *Edge* wrote that life for "small, one-project indie devcos" was "becoming prohibitively difficult".

## Freeware and downloads

The games that revived the idea were free at first. In March 2005 *Edge* picked out *Doukutsu Monogatari*, or *Cave Story*, a Japanese action game made by one programmer and given away as freeware. It had, the magazine wrote, a "level of polish and depth that could easily elevate it from freeware", and a fan group's English translation made it "an essential download".

Download services gave such games a way to be sold. Xbox Live Arcade on the Xbox 360, Valve's [Steam](/vault/tools/steam) on the PC and later on the Mac, and Apple's App Stores all sold small games with no disc, box or shop. [Digital distribution](/vault/distribution/digital-distribution) did not remove the gatekeeper, though. It moved the job from publisher to store. In the same 2011 feature *Edge* noted that Positech's political simulation *Democracy 2* "was turned down by Steam, but is on the App Store". And as Blow said, each store asked a different price in approvals and paperwork.

## Paying for it

Developers tried new ways to charge. In 2010 the first Humble Indie Bundle offered several independent games for whatever buyers chose to pay, with part of the money going to charity. Talking to *Edge* in January 2011, Blow pointed to it as proof that independents did not need a backer: "Look at the Humble Indie Bundle guys – they've made almost $200,000 per game, plus a bunch of money for charity. That was not backed by anybody, that was them putting up a webpage."

Markus Persson's [Minecraft](/vault/games/minecraft) took money before it was finished. Players bought it during what *Edge* called "a two-year beta". When it reached version 1.0.0, Persson told the magazine that "with millions of sold copies, it didn't really make much sense to call the game 'pre-release' any more". He said he enjoyed the limits set by programming contests such as the [Ludum Dare](/vault/communities/ludum-dare) 48-hour competition, and he defended the game's lack of a tutorial by pointing to an older tradition: "Roguelikes have been doing this for decades."

Early in 2012 Tim Schafer's Double Fine asked players on the crowdfunding site Kickstarter to pay for a point-and-click adventure. By the time *Edge* went to press it had raised more than $2.2 million. Obsidian, the developer of *Fallout: New Vegas*, joined what *Edge* called "gaming's latest gold rush", and its designer Chris Avellone said player funding was "proof certain genres aren't dead". *Edge* also found the other side: a small studio, Spark Plug Games, had raised $941 of a $25,000 target with 16 days to go.

## Older genres, new audiences

Many independent hits went back to genres from the home computer years. In 2010 *Edge* grouped *Spelunky* with the [roguelikes](/vault/genres/roguelike) *ADOM* and [NetHack](/vault/games/nethack), games that build a new dungeon every time you play.

Some of the older companies became indie publishers themselves. In 2013 Team17, the studio that had grown out of a PD library, went back to publishing other developers' games. In 2019 *Wireframe* described it as "a British indie publishing powerhouse", "valued in the hundreds of millions range".
