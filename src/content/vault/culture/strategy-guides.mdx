In late 1981 Ken Uston, a card counter who had made his name beating Las Vegas casinos at blackjack, spent four days writing a book about *[Pac-Man](/vault/games/pac-man)*. *Video Games* magazine reported that within three weeks of publication *Mastering Pac-Man* had reached the top of the *New York Times* bestseller list. To find his routes through the maze he drew up the board, photocopied "about 100 copies" and experimented with patterns on them. He knew who his readers were. "I'm helping these 12-year-old kids save a little money," he told the magazine. "Arcade operators don't like it, but that's life."

## Fast facts

- **First wave:** arcade guides in 1981–82, among them Tom Hirschfeld's *How to Master the Video Games* (Bantam, $2.95) and Uston's *Mastering Pac-Man* (Signet, $1.95).
- **Publishers' own guides:** Melbourne House's *A Guide to Playing The Hobbit* (1984); Nintendo's *Official Nintendo Player's Guide* (by 1988).
- **The guide industry:** Prima's *Secrets of the Games* line from 1990; BradyGames from November 1993; Piggyback, in Europe, from 1998.
- **Price:** most Sega-related American guides of the 1990s cost between $9.95 and $14.99; in Britain in 2004 a guide cost about £10, "a quarter (but often more)" of the price of the game.
- **End of an era:** Penguin Random House announced in November 2018 that it would close Prima Games in 2019.

## Arcade guides, 1981–82

The first boom in guides followed the first boom in arcade games. *[Computer Gaming World](/vault/magazines/computer-gaming-world)* reviewed Hirschfeld's *How to Master the Video Games* at the start of 1982: 177 pages billed as "the first complete guide to the 30 most popular coin-operated arcade games", sorted into Space Invaders-type games, Asteroids-type games, maze games, reflex games and the rest. Hirschfeld opened with "Seven Steps to Mastery", from reconnaissance (watch the game played several times before you try) to memorisation, and added a chapter of hand, eye and finger exercises for the player.

By July 1982 *[Electronic Games](/vault/magazines/electronic-games)* had a shelf of them to review, and not all were welcome. "Here come the quickie rip-offs," it began, of a 48-page booklet called *How to Win at Video Games* ($2.50): "Save your money. You'll learn more by changing the $2.50 into quarters and playing the games." It found Hirschfeld's book dry but "certainly one of the best primers on the coin-op scene yet produced". *Mastering Pac-Man* it rated "one of the better books on playing electronic games now available": 128 pages that would serve a novice, and that the "utter fanatic" aiming to become what Uston called a "Pac-Master", consistently scoring 150,000 or more, "will make this his bible". Uston followed it with *Score! Beating the Top 16 Video Games* and a 674-page *Guide to Buying and Beating the Home Video Games*, and *Electronic Games* was already billing him as "best-selling author of 'Mastering Pac-Man'" that spring.

## Guides from the publishers

Home computer adventures produced a different kind of guide, sometimes from the company that sold the game. [Melbourne House](/vault/companies/melbourne-house) published David Elkan's *A Guide to Playing The Hobbit* in 1984, 76 pages of locations, screens and hints for *[The Hobbit](/vault/games/the-hobbit)*. *CRASH* stressed that it was "only a Guide and not The Solution", since the game had "no one perfect solution", and *Computer & Video Games* noted that it took the player through the adventure at three different levels of help.

Nintendo's own guide reached some players with the console itself. In 1988 a reader wrote to the first issue of *[Nintendo Power](/vault/magazines/nintendo-power)* asking for a copy of *The Official Nintendo Player's Guide*, which a friend had "acquired" when he bought the NES basic set, and later that year the magazine told another reader that there were no plans yet for a second one. *Nintendo Power* itself, with its maps and walkthroughs, did the same job in every issue.

## Prima, BradyGames and the guide industry

The modern guide business, *[Edge](/vault/magazines/edge)* wrote in 2004, was, "most agree", "sired in 1990 by Prima Publishing with its 'Secrets of the Games' imprint", which made its debut with *Nintendo Game Secrets*. BradyGames was "established in November 1993 with the *Mortal Kombat Kompanion*". Their books split into two kinds. Some were "official", licensed by the game's publisher; others were openly "unauthorized", like Prima's *Sega Saturn Pocket Power Guide Unauthorized* of 1997. A list of Sega-related books published in the United States shows how stable the price was: of 57 guides and secrets books from 1990 to 1999, 47 cost between $9.95 and $14.99, from *Sega Genesis Secrets* at $9.95 in 1990 to a run of Dreamcast guides at $12.99 in 1999.

By 2004 *Edge* found a big business. Prima said at least eight of its titles had sold more than 100,000 copies in the United States in 2003, among them guides to *Madden NFL 2004* and *Medal of Honor: Rising Sun*, and claimed more than 1,100 books and 90 million copies in print in its 14 years. BradyGames spoke of a *Final Fantasy* guide with more than a million sold. Piggyback, founded in 1998 to make guides for European markets, began with a guide to *[Metal Gear Solid](/vault/games/metal-gear-solid)* and spent more than six months on each book; with Konami's artwork it made official guides that *Edge* called "coffee table pieces".

The work was hard. "We've written some books in seven days," Prima's lead in-house author, Mario DeGovia, told *Edge*. "Our days are long when we're doing a guide - 12 to 14 hours a day, six to seven days a week is common." Leigh Davis, editor-in-chief at BradyGames, thought a guide harder to write than a computer book, because "you have no way of knowing how long it will take you to get through the game".

## The internet, and after

Free help arrived alongside the books. Players posted walkthroughs to sites such as [GameFAQs](/vault/culture/gamefaqs) from 1995, and by the turn of the century, *Edge* wrote, "many were inclined to forecast" that home internet access would damage the guide market. Why pay "£10 — a quarter (but often more) of the RRP of the actual game" when "CJayC's GameFAQs site can offer a bare-bones equivalent for free"? "People have been asking the same question for ten years," Davis replied. "And you know what? We're selling more strategy guides now than we ever have." In Britain, the other paid source of answers, the premium-rate [hint line](/vault/culture/hint-lines), charged by the minute.

The two rivals ended under one owner. Random House bought Prima in 2001, and its merger with Penguin in 2013 put Prima and BradyGames in the same company; in June 2015 BradyGames was folded into the Prima Games name. In November 2018 Penguin Random House announced that it would close Prima Games in 2019. The brand was sold in March 2019 and continued as a website, without the printed guides.
