Some games never left Japan. Import gaming became a dedicated hobby as players sought RPGs, shooters, and quirky titles that publishers deemed uncommercial for Western release. Specialist shops stocked Japanese cartridges. Players modified consoles or bought adapters. Language barriers became challenges to overcome rather than deal-breakers.

## Fast facts

- **Motivation:** Access unreleased games.
- **Barriers:** [Region locking](/vault/culture/region-locking), language.
- **Solutions:** Mods, adapters, persistence.
- **Community:** Dedicated enthusiasts.

## Overcoming region locks

This was a retail market with competing products, prices and reviews. *Super
Play* ran a converter group test in February 1993 — "THE ADAPTOR!" — naming the
**Honey Bee Super-Magic Converter** (Q Systems, £15), a **TDS Trading** converter
at "approx. £15", a **Spellbound Adaptor** and a **Universal Game Converter**, each
with a supplier phone number. Fifteen pounds was the going rate.

The trade behind it was routine enough for *Mean Machines* to reference in passing
in 1990, in a step that assumes "you have a Japanese games adaptor
(available from mail order import companies)".

It was not, however, treated as uncontroversial. *Super Play* discussed the
practice in its first issue as part of "the grey [import] industry", weighing
whether Japanese objections to it had "justification".
