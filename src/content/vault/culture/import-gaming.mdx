In November 1990 *Mean Machines* told a reader that he would be able to buy Nintendo's new [Super Famicom](/vault/systems/super-nintendo) "from grey importers at the end of November", long before any British release: "expect to pay around £190.00 or so". For British players in the late 1980s and early 1990s, that was how the newest consoles arrived. Japan got the machines and the games first, Europe got them late or not at all, and a trade of mail-order importers, adaptors and magazine warnings grew up in between.

## Why import

The reason was time. *Super Play*, the British Super Nintendo magazine, explained it in its first issue in 1992: when a new product was a success in Japan, its maker turned next to the United States, "and by the time it gets round to the rest of the world (ie us) years have often gone by". Europe "suffers from the relatively late introduction of systems and software", the magazine wrote, and an unofficial import trade had grown up to fill the gap.

Some machines never came at all. NEC never launched its [PC Engine](/vault/systems/pc-engine) in Britain. "NEC didn't launch the PC Engine, but people imported them anyway," *[ACE](/vault/magazines/ace-magazine)* wrote, looking back on 1989, and Nintendo's Game Boys were "imported, begged, borrowed and stolen, thanks largely to the inclusion of Tetris with the machine". In December 1989, before the [Game Boy](/vault/systems/game-boy)'s official British launch, *[The Games Machine](/vault/magazines/the-games-machine)*'s Christmas guide listed it on import from PC Engine Services at £79.90, with cartridges at £24.90 each. Grey imports were not only British: in March 1989 Jurgen Goeldner, who ran the West German software distributor Rushware, told *ACE* that the "greyimported PC Engine" was doing well there.

Later, games themselves were the draw. Many Japanese titles, above all role-playing games, were never translated. The [fan translation](/vault/communities/fan-translations) entry tells how players who had imported them began, in the 1990s, to translate them themselves.

## The grey importers

The importers were small businesses, and the magazines named them. In December 1988 *The Games Machine* described Micro Media of Shropshire as "the sole UK outlet for the PC Engine". *Super Play*'s 1992 feature on the trade, "Grey imports: the price of fame", profiled Console Concepts of Newcastle-under-Lyme, which had been "at the forefront of the market since 1989", doing most of its business by mail order. It had begun with the PC Engine, and the market grew again with each new console: the Mega Drive, the Super Famicom, the Neo Geo.

The prices tell how much buyers would pay to be early. Console Concepts sold most of its first batch of 400 Super Famicom games at £90 each before they had even arrived. In Japan they cost 9,800 yen, about £42. The importer paid a wholesale price of about £50, then VAT at 17.5 per cent and shipping on top. *Super Play* reported that the grey import market for consoles might be 15 to 25 per cent of the whole, and concluded: "It looks like grey importers are here to stay".

## Adaptors and lockout chips

Playing an import on a British console usually meant an adaptor. Nintendo's machines checked each cartridge with a security chip: the console contained a chip that looked for its partner chip in the cartridge, and refused to run a game whose chip did not match. American and Japanese Super Nintendos used the same chip, so a cartridge from one ran on the other once it fitted; Japanese slots were too narrow for American cartridges, and American slots had small tabs to block Japanese ones, which owners removed with pliers. The British machine used a different chip. An adaptor for it took a British cartridge in the back, to answer the security check, and the imported game on top.

"Adaptors are a grey importer's lifeline," *Super Play* wrote, and by 1993 they were a market of their own. In February 1993 the magazine tested five of them, among them the Datel Universal Adaptor at £14.99, the Honey Bee Super-Magic Converter from Q Systems at £15 and a converter from TDS Trading at "approx. £15". For the time being, it told readers, "the vast bulk of titles are only available on import". It also warned that newer games such as *Super Mario Kart*, *Desert Strike* and *Axelay* did not work well on older adaptors, so buyers should check that the model they ordered had been updated.

Sega owners met the same problem. When *[Mean Machines](/vault/magazines/mean-machines)* reviewed *Rainbow Islands* for the [Mega Drive](/vault/systems/sega-mega-drive) in 1990, the game was available only from importers, and a "grey import warning" explained that it would not run on an official British machine without "a Japanese games adaptor (available from mail order import companies)" or a modification, which the magazine did not recommend because it invalidated the warranty.

## After the official launches

Official releases did not end the trade. Imports still came first, and some machines never got an official launch at all. In 1993 a *Computer and Video Games* reader asked what a PC Engine cost on import, and was told to ask Console Concepts, or to buy one second-hand. The pull of games that never left Japan lasted longest: the role-playing games that drew importers in the 1990s were the ones fan translators later turned into English.
