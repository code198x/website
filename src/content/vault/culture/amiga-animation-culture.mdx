Before YouTube, before streaming, the **Amiga animation community** created and distributed short animations through BBSes, Aminet, disk magazines, and physical disk swapping. Using tools like Deluxe Paint, Sculpt 3D, and Imagine, individual artists produced cartoon shorts, technical demonstrations, and music videos that circulated globally through informal networks. This was user-generated content decades before the term existed.

## The other half: desktop video

⚠ **This entry describes the hobbyist half and stops.** By 1992 the Amiga had a
professional video-production market substantial enough to support a dedicated
*Amiga World* special issue, whose cover counts the field: **"24-Bit Displays, 11
Amiga Genlocks, 6 Video Titlers, 8 3-D Packages, 4 Edit Controllers."**

The prices place it. A DPS Personal TBC II time base corrector "plugs into your
Commodore Amiga or PC system for only $995 — and since that's thousands less than
conventional TBC" units. That is not a bedroom purchase; it is a cost saving
against broadcast kit.

**And the skills being taught were television skills.** The contents read as a video
production course, not a computing one: A/B roll editing, SMPTE time code ("if you
want to edit videotape accurately, you need to know the basics of 'time code'"),
getting "your video signals in sync" with a TBC, single-frame controllers for
laying animation down to tape, white balance.

Beside that, the same issue teaches the traditional craft — storyboarding, walk
cycles ("how to walk before you run"), sound synchronisation, reflection mapping —
with one writer arguing that "sometimes tried-and-true traditional animation
techniques are exactly what you need."

So the Amiga animation culture ran from a teenager trading an ANIM on a BBS to a
production house cutting to tape with a $995 sync card, and the two halves shared
tools, magazines and vocabulary. This entry has only described the first.
