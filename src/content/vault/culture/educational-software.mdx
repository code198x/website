Beyond games, educational software included typing tutors, encyclopaedias, language courses, and drill-and-practice programs. The 1990s CD-ROM era brought multimedia learning: talking dictionaries, virtual dissections, and interactive encyclopaedias. Some programs genuinely helped learning; others moved worksheets to screens and stopped there. The category evolved into today's learning apps.

## The British case was a state programme

⚠ **This entry describes an American consumer market. In Britain the category was
built by government.** Primary-school software came from the **Microelectronics
Education Programme**, and it arrived attached to a hardware subsidy.

By July 1984 the MEP pack, "developed by the MEP at a cost of about £100,000, has
gone to **27,000 primary schools**."

**It was not a BBC Micro monopoly**, which is the usual assumption. The software
"accompanies any machine which primary schools buy under an assisted purchase
scheme", and "schools can choose between the **BBC**, the **48K Spectrum**, and the
**RML 480-Z**." The subsidy followed the school's choice rather than dictating it —
which is why a Spectrum could turn up in a classroom.

**And the package was not software alone.** It "includes teacher training material,
as well as language and mathematical programs." What was bought institutionally was
a curriculum intervention with a disk in it, not a product.

## Fast facts

- **Types:** tutors, drills, reference, simulation.
- **CD-ROM boom:** multimedia encyclopaedias, courses.
- **School market:** significant institutional purchasing — in Britain, state-funded: MEP software plus an assisted purchase scheme covering the BBC, 48K Spectrum and RML 480-Z.
- **Home market:** parents seeking educational value.
- **Modern evolution:** apps, online learning platforms.

## Software categories

- **Simulations:** learning through experimentation.
- **Reference:** encyclopaedias, dictionaries.
- **Tools:** word processors, spreadsheets for students.
