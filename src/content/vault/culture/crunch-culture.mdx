**Crunch culture** refers to the game industry's practice of mandatory extended overtime periods, typically before release deadlines. While the practice dates to the earliest commercial games, the 2004 "EA Spouse" blog brought widespread attention to the human cost, sparking ongoing debates about labour practices.

## The hours are older than the word

⚠ **The period press recorded the hours, and admired them.**

*Amiga Format*, 1993, profiling Archer MacLean: when he "comes head to head with a
particularly difficult problem he sometimes spends 18 hours a day working on it
and doesn't step outside his front door for days at a [time]." The sentence sits
in a piece that goes on to praise his go-karting and skydiving. It is offered as
evidence of seriousness.

Two things follow. The working pattern this entry describes predates the language
used to criticise it by two decades. But MacLean was an **independent developer on
his own game**, not an employee under mandatory overtime — so the passage
documents the hours, not the coercion this entry defines crunch by. The shift the
2004 disclosure marks is less the discovery of long hours than the reframing of
them as something done *to* workers rather than *by* authors.

⚠ **This entry is thinly served by the reference library.** Its documented
incidents are all post-2004, past the corpus. The period sourcing above establishes
the working pattern; it does not establish the industrial practice.

## Fast facts

- **Definition:** Extended mandatory overtime
- **Typical hours:** 60-100+ per week
- **Duration:** Weeks to months
- **Exposed:** 2004 ([EA Spouse](/vault/culture/ea-spouse))

## Legacy

Despite decades of exposure, crunch remains endemic. Unionisation efforts (Game Workers Unite, 2018+) represent the latest attempt to address working conditions. The issue highlights the tension between creative ambition and sustainable labour practices.
