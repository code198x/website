An arcade conversion is a home version of a coin-op game. It is a provenance, not a genre: the original could be a racing game, a shoot-'em-up or a beat-'em-up. What set conversions apart was how they were judged. A magazine reviewing a conversion asked two questions at once: how close is it to the arcade machine, and is it any good? The answers often disagreed, and in February 1988 one issue of *[CRASH](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine)* showed both on the same page.

## Two verdicts on one page

That issue reviewed two of the biggest arcade licences of Christmas 1987, U.S. Gold's *[Out Run](/vault/games/out-run)* and Ocean's *Gryzor*, the European name for Konami's *[Contra](/vault/games/contra)*. Nick, reviewing *Out Run*, began with a warning: "all those of you that are expecting this to be just like the arcade version had better leave the room right now, because you're going to be so disappointed." Then he gave it 93%. The graphics were "as well drawn as is possible on the Spectrum", the 128K sound "absolutely fantastic", and the game "an essential purchase". His two colleagues gave 63% and 59%, and the magazine summed up the split: views ranged between "the best you could have expected" and "a poor translation of a great arcade game".

*Gryzor*, opposite, got 49% overall, with the verdict "not living up to the arcade original's high standards". "It's not really the month of good conversions, is it?" one reviewer asked. A month earlier *[Sinclair User](/vault/magazines/sinclair-user)* had found a way to say what *CRASH*'s reviewers were arguing about. It gave *Out Run* 8 out of 10 and remarked that if the game had been called *Sunday Driver*, "everyone would have been bowled over". Measured against the cabinet, the Spectrum game disappointed; measured as a Spectrum racing game, it was good.

## How the press judged them

The fidelity question could go either way. When *[Mean Machines](/vault/magazines/mean-machines)* previewed the NES *Gauntlet II* in February 1991, faithfulness was the whole recommendation: "Here's a show-stopping arcade conversion! *Gauntlet II* is one of the most faithful recreations of a coin-op yet seen on the Nintendo... This Nintendo conversion has every feature of the coin-op, and, amazingly, has all the speech." When *CRASH* made Ocean's Spectrum *Chase H.Q.* a Smash at 95% in December 1989, its reviewer put the two tests together: "This is an accurate conversion that is as playable as the real thing — and that sure is a big recommendation!"

Some reviewers told readers to set the arcade aside. In December 1982 the American magazine *JoyStik* opened its guide to Atari's VCS *Pac-Man* by asking readers to "forget you've ever seen or played the original arcade version", and concluded that it was "a very good home-video game". *Video Games* had done the opposite in August 1982, warning that "anyone who buys Pac-Man because they love the arcade game with the same name may wind up disappointed". The [*Pac-Man*](/vault/games/pac-man-atari-2600) entry tells that story.

By the late 1980s readers voted on conversions as a category. *CU Amiga*'s ballot for its 1989 readers' poll, printed in February 1990, listed "Best Coin Op Conversion" and "Best Arcade Conversion" alongside "Best Coin-Op (Arcade)" for the cabinets themselves and "Best Original Game".

The same game could also score very differently on different machines. *Amstrad Action* gave the Amstrad *Out Run* 37% and called it "probably one of the worst" arcade conversions, while *[ACE](/vault/magazines/ace-magazine)* rated the Atari ST version 873 out of 1,000. Tiertex's *Strider* scored 96% on the Amiga and 42% on the Commodore 64 in *[Zzap!64](/vault/magazines/zzap-64)*.

## What the machines gave up

Each conversion cut something, and reviewers noticed what. The Spectrum *Out Run* loaded each stage from tape as the player reached it; a 128K machine kept the stages it had loaded. The Commodore 64 version dropped the forks in the road and made you choose one of five fixed routes before starting, and *ACE* wrote that without the junctions "it's hard to call this Out Run at all". The Amstrad version kept the routes but had no engine noise.

Colour was the Spectrum's problem. *CRASH* noted that the Spectrum *R-Type* used colour "very liberally, but even the resulting clashes don't spoil the detailed, fast-moving sprites"; the [attribute-aware design](/vault/techniques/attribute-aware-design) entry explains why clashes happened at all. On the Atari VCS, which could not show five characters on the same lines of the screen, *Pac-Man*'s four ghosts took turns to appear, one per frame, and flickered.

The 16-bit machines narrowed the gap but did not close it. For U.S. Gold's Amiga *[Street Fighter II](/vault/games/street-fighter-ii)* in 1992, the sprites were about 20 per cent smaller than the arcade's and the Amiga showed 32 colours. U.S. Gold told *[Amiga Power](/vault/magazines/amiga-power)* that it had not tried "to create a perfect conversion", only "a home computer version of Street Fighter II which feels as close to the real thing as possible".

## Working from the cabinet

Fidelity depended on what the programmers had to work from, and it was rarely the arcade game's code. At Tiertex, U.S. Gold's regular conversion house in Manchester, "the programmers had no access to the original arcade source code – the game had to be played over and over again in order to make the conversion accurate", Anthony Ball recalled of converting *Mercs*. The cabinet lived in the company's attic, where the artists worked, and Ball's team grabbed graphics from its RGB signal with a digitiser. "Virtually every one from Tiertex was coded and published within six months," he added. For *Strider*, according to *The One*, Capcom was "understandably wary about revealing its coin-op secrets", so the programmer built the game from long sessions on the machine and a video of it being played through.

Not every team started with a machine. *Sinclair User* reported that the programmers of the Spectrum *R-Type* travelled to London from Swansea to play the coin-op "until they could get the proper feel for the game", until Activision sent them a machine "to save their rail expenses". Ocean could do better for *RoboCop*, because it owned the licence: it had bought the film rights and sublet them to Data East, which made the arcade game. Peter Johnson, converting it to the Atari ST and Amiga from home, was sent "the Data East arcade game supplied by Ocean, built into a handy portable suitcase, alongside a finished build of the Spectrum version".

## The business

Conversions were licensed products, bought from Japanese and American arcade makers, and in Britain two publishers dominated. U.S. Gold and [Ocean](/vault/companies/ocean-software) were partly owned by the same people, and a U.S. Gold executive later described an unwritten division of the arcade makers between them: "They went after Taito, we went after Sega, they went after Nintendo, we went after Capcom." [Elite Systems](/vault/companies/elite-systems) had its own run of Capcom games: in March 1986 *CRASH* announced that "after the massive success of *Commando*, Elite Systems are to continue their series of Capcom arcade conversions with *Ghosts and Goblins*".

The licence fee and the arcade game's fame set the schedule. U.S. Gold's *Out Run* was in the shops for Christmas 1987 before review copies had gone out, *The Games Machine* noted, and U.S. Gold claimed more than 200,000 copies sold in the first two weeks. Having one developer write every format was cheaper. Geoff Brown of U.S. Gold explained that Tiertex's work on all formats cost "a hundred thousand pounds for four formats whereas it might be fifty thousand for one". Mick West, who worked there, put the cost in quality: "We were just to copy the game, and do it as quickly as possible."

## From loose copies to near-identical ports

The first famous licensed conversion was a loose one. Atari bought the home rights to *[Space Invaders](/vault/games/space-invaders)* in 1980, and its VCS cartridge had 36 invaders instead of the arcade's 55, three shields instead of four, and 112 game variations. It still became, *Creative Computing* reported, "the fastest selling of Atari's thirty-six games".

In Britain the conversion became a major business on the 8-bit computers in the mid-1980s, and the Spectrum carried on getting arcade licences into the 1990s. When the 128K-only Spectrum *Street Fighter II* arrived in 1993, with each fighter loading separately from tape, *Sinclair User* guessed it was "likely to be the last big licence coin op conversion ever on the Speccy". By then the comparison that mattered was with the consoles. Capcom's own *Street Fighter II* had come out on Nintendo's Super NES, and *The One* judged the Amiga version against it: "absolutely excellent", but "not as good as the SNES version".
