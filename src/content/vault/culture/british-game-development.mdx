In May 1980 two Liverpool University students were selling programs by mail order, and every tape they sent out was a separate SAVE from their computer, because they had no duplicating machine. By August 1985 one budget publisher, Mastertronic, was receiving around 200 games a month from people who hoped to be published, and *Sinclair User* was telling would-be authors that breaking into software had become as hard as getting a recording contract. In five years Britain had grown a games industry out of almost nothing, and it had done it with cassettes, sold for a few pounds in the same high-street shops as records and stationery.

That industry's companies mostly did not last. Many of the best-known names of the 1980s were bankrupt, bought or renamed by 2000. But their people went on to new studios, and a line runs from those tapes to *Grand Theft Auto*, *Total War* and *LittleBigPlanet*.

## Cheap computers

The machines came first, and Sinclair made them cheap. The [ZX81](/vault/systems/sinclair-zx81) of March 1981 cost £69.95 built or £49.95 as a kit to solder together, against almost $300 for a Commodore VIC-20, and Sinclair advertised "over 50,000 in the first 3 months". The [ZX Spectrum](/vault/systems/sinclair-zx-spectrum) followed in April 1982 at £125 for 16K and £175 for 48K, with colour and sound. Sinclair had them built under contract, much of it at the Timex factory in Dundee, where ZX81 production rose from 10,000 to 80,000 a month in its first year.

Acorn's [BBC Micro](/vault/systems/bbc-micro) was dearer, at £299 and £399, and came with a public mission. It was the machine of the BBC's [Computer Literacy Project](/vault/phenomena/bbc-computer-literacy-project), which began with *The Computer Programme* on BBC2 in January 1982, and in July that year the government announced a £9 million scheme to put a microcomputer into every primary school, paying half the price of an approved machine. The BBC Micro became a common sight in British schools, and it was the computer *[Elite](/vault/games/elite)* was written for.

How many homes had a computer is harder to pin down than it is often made to sound. A *Meal* survey reported in the summer of 1987 found that "18% of homes in the UK own a sub £500 home computer, which equates to 4 million homes". The magazine that reported it warned that surveys like this were "fairly hard to understand", and pointed out that owning a computer and using it were different things: if all four million homes bought one game a week, "software sales would be through the roof".

## Publishers and the post

Anyone with a Spectrum or a BBC Micro owned a development system, and a publisher needed nothing from Sinclair or Acorn to sell software for it. Publishers advertised for programs and read what came through the post. [Ocean](/vault/companies/ocean-software) began in Manchester in 1982 with an advert in *Your Computer*, and one of the programmers who answered, Christian Urquhart, was still at school. The first wave of companies was small and often short-lived: [Bug-Byte](/vault/companies/bug-byte) (1980) and [Hewson Consultants](/vault/companies/hewson-consultants) (1980), then [Imagine](/vault/companies/imagine-software) and [Ultimate Play the Game](/vault/companies/ultimate) (both 1982), [Software Projects](/vault/companies/software-projects) (1983), and [Gremlin Graphics](/vault/companies/gremlin-graphics), [U.S. Gold](/vault/companies/us-gold) and [Mastertronic](/vault/companies/mastertronic) (all 1984). The [bedroom coder](/vault/phenomena/bedroom-coder) entry follows the programmers themselves, and what they were really paid.

By 1985 the door was narrowing. *Sinclair User*'s feature "Programs for Profit" found that Mastertronic looked at about 200 submissions a month and published four to six. The same feature worked out that a £6.95 game paid its author about 40 pence a copy. Publishers were moving to teams, and by May 1986 Ocean had up to sixty programmers under contract at once.

## Cassettes on the high street

Games reached buyers through ordinary shops. An advertisement for [Activision](/vault/companies/activision)'s *Ghostbusters* in January 1985 listed "selected branches of Boots, WH Smith, John Menzies, Lasky's, Spectrum, Rumbelows, Woolworth and good computer software stores everywhere". Mastertronic went further still. It launched in the spring of 1984 with games at £1.99, when most cost between £5 and £10, took more than 100,000 orders in its first week, and sold through newsagents, garage forecourts and motorway service stations as well as W H Smith.

## Magazines

A crowd of specialist magazines grew up alongside the games. *[Computer & Video Games](/vault/magazines/computer-and-video-games)* appeared in November 1981, before the Spectrum or the Commodore 64 was on sale. *[Sinclair User](/vault/magazines/sinclair-user)* followed in April 1982. In Ludlow, a market town in Shropshire, [Newsfield](/vault/companies/newsfield) grew from a Spectrum mail-order business into the publisher of *[CRASH](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine)* (February 1984) and *[ZZAP!64](/vault/magazines/zzap-64)* (May 1985), each reviewing games with several named reviewers. Future Publishing began in Somerset with *[Amstrad Action](/vault/magazines/amstrad-action)* in October 1985, and *[Your Sinclair](/vault/magazines/your-sinclair)* took its name in January 1986.

The magazines made programmers into names. "Software houses are endeavouring to promote the programmers as well as the programs," *Sinclair User* wrote in February 1984, and "those whizz-kids might soon be elevated to the status of superstars." Hewson put its authors' names on its games, among them Andrew Braybrook, Steve Turner and Raffaele Cecco. Ultimate went the other way and said almost nothing about itself, while *CRASH* counted fourteen Spectrum hits in a row from it, with an average review score of 93%.

## Headlines

The industry was news beyond the specialist press. Gremlin's *Wanted: Monty Mole*, a Spectrum game about a mole stealing coal during the miners' strike, with a caricature of the miners' leader Arthur Scargill, was featured on the national television news on 25 July 1984. Sixteen days earlier, on 9 July, Imagine Software had been wound up with a BBC documentary crew still filming. It had grown from a front room to more than a hundred staff in eighteen months, and the programme went out on BBC2 that December.

## Where it happened

The industry grew up in regional towns and cities as much as in London, often because one company's staff founded the next nearby.

Two of these clusters grew from single companies. Guildford's began with Bullfrog, formed there in 1987 by Peter Molyneux and Les Edgar, and by 1999 *Edge* counted about 300 developers in the town. Dundee's began with DMA Design, founded in 1988 by David Jones, who had been an apprentice at the Timex factory.

## Bigger teams, bigger owners

The 16-bit machines raised the cost of a game, and the consoles raised it again. [Psygnosis](/vault/companies/psygnosis), founded in Liverpool in 1984 out of the wreck of Imagine, published only for 16-bit machines and made its name with *Shadow of the Beast* and DMA's *Lemmings*. [Sony](/vault/companies/sony) bought it in 1993, and its *[WipEout](/vault/games/wipeout)* was the best-selling game at the European PlayStation launch.

Over the next few years most of the big independents were sold. [Electronic Arts](/vault/companies/electronic-arts) bought Bullfrog in 1995. [Infogrames](/vault/companies/infogrames) bought Ocean in 1996 and Gremlin in 1999, [Eidos](/vault/companies/eidos) bought U.S. Gold in 1996, and Hewson had gone into receivership in 1991. Gremlin had bought DMA Design in 1997; DMA passed on to Take-Two and became Rockstar North in 2002. Infogrames closed its Sheffield studio, the old Gremlin, in 2003, and many of its staff moved to a new Sheffield developer, Sumo Digital.

## Studios with roots in the period

When *Edge* listed the world's leading studios in February 2013, its British entries included [Codemasters](/vault/companies/codemasters) (founded 1986), The Creative Assembly (1987), Jagex (2000), [Rockstar North](/vault/companies/rockstar-north) (2002), Rocksteady (2004) and [Media Molecule](/vault/companies/media-molecule) (2006). Two of them grew straight out of the 1980s: Codemasters was started by Richard and David Darling, who had written for Mastertronic as teenagers, and Rockstar North was DMA Design renamed. David Braben, co-author of *Elite*, set up Frontier Developments in 1994. Electronic Arts bought Codemasters in 2021.

## State support, and its cultural test

Government money came late. *Edge* reported in February 2013 that the government had confirmed tax relief for British developers, and it took effect on 1 April 2014, as a payable tax credit worth 25% of qualifying production costs. Games qualify only by passing a cultural test, scored on their cultural content, cultural contribution, where they are made and who makes them. *Edge*'s news panel summed up one consequence: "Devs can earn tax relief points by setting their games in the UK." Its gloss on the announcement was "Touché, Canada?", a nod to the incentives that had drawn studios abroad. From January 2024 the relief began to be replaced by the Video Games Expenditure Credit, at 34% of qualifying expenditure, and games must still be certified as British.
