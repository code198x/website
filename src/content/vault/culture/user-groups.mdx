**User groups** were local computer clubs that formed the backbone of computing communities before the internet. They provided technical support, software sharing, social connection, and collective purchasing power that made computing more accessible and enjoyable.

## How you found one

Before the internet there was a page in a magazine. *Amiga Shopper* ran a **User
Groups** directory every month through the early 1990s — "Find your local group" —
with a coupon at the foot of it:

> If you run a user group which isn't listed on this page, fill in the form below
> for your free entry.

That is the whole discovery mechanism: a national magazine acting as the index, a
free listing, and a postal address or phone number for each club.

## What one page contained

March 1992's directory is a good cross-section.

**They met wherever a member could get a room.** Biggin Hill Library. "Ground
floor function suite, The Civic Centre" at Chester-le-Street. The Harlequin Club
in Macclesfield, every Tuesday from 8pm. Holyhead Unemployed Workers Club. A
training centre in Ayr, a youth centre in Ashford, a community centre outside
Maidenhead, and — in a neighbouring issue — Harrogate Leisure Centre and GEC
Aerospace.

**Joining cost between nothing and £30.** Most were free. The Avon Micro Computer
Club asked £3 a year, Amigamania £7, Club Amiga and Champion PD £10, and the Amiga
Musicians' Club £30 — which bought "a disk with 50 IFF samples a month for 12
months". The Amiga Beginners' Club charged "£2 for a single disk, or £20 for every
issue". Kent Youth Computer Group charged by the night: "30p entry/evening".

**Membership bought shared infrastructure.** PD libraries, club disks and disk
magazines, printed newsletters, telephone helplines — Club Amiga advertised "a
24-hr telephone helpline service" — a bulletin board, discounted hardware, a DTP
scanning service, free small ads.

## Federated, and specialised

Two structures the "local computer club" description misses.

**National bodies with local branches.** The Independent Commodore Products Users'
Group appears once as a national entry, meeting at Biggin Hill Library with
"lecture nights and open nights where members can get help", and then again as
Anglesey, Ayr, Bury St Edmunds, Buxton, Macclesfield, Mid-Thames and Solent ICPUG,
each with its own venue and contact.

**Clubs for tasks, not machines.** By 1992 the fragmentation had gone well past
one club per computer. The same page lists a 1520 Plotter Group, an Amiga Artists
Club, an Amiga Musicians' Club, an Amiga Video Producers' Group, an AMOS
Programmers' Exchange *and* a separate AMOS User Group, a Basic Programmers'
Group, a CanDo User Group, a CDTV Users Club, a Public Domain User Group, and an
Imagine Users' Group "to encourage use of Imagine and other ray tracers", with its
own object library. One club per thing you did with the machine.

Groups also policed their own boundary. The Amiga Artists Club's three-line entry
describes itself as a place "for mutual assistance and programming demos" and then
adds: "Pirates not welcome."

## Reach

The directory was not confined to Britain. Alongside entries from Limavady,
Ballymena, Glasgow, Carnoustie and Anglesey sits Comp-U-Pal, an "Australian user
group for users in the outback. Newsletter, phone helpline, PD library. Membership
A$24" — listed in a British magazine, for readers who would join by post.

The American clubs ran on the same three services a decade earlier, visible in
*BYTE*'s club columns: the Chicago CP/M group that "meets regularly and produces a
monthly newsletter entitled PIP", SEMCO's newsletter subscription, groups meeting
on a fixed weeknight of the month. Meeting, newsletter, library.

## Fast facts

- **Era:** 1975-1995 (peak)
- **Format:** Local club meetings
- **Activities:** meetings, newsletters, PD libraries, helplines, group buying
- **Costs:** free to £30/year; some charged per meeting
- **Structure:** independent local clubs, national federations with branches, and single-application groups
- **Platforms:** All home computers
- **Legacy:** Original computing communities
