Up, up, down, down, left, right, left, right, B, A. Kazuhisa Hashimoto put that sequence into Konami's Famicom version of *[Gradius](/vault/games/gradius)* for his own use. "I hadn't played it that much and couldn't beat it myself, so I put in the Konami Code," he said in a 2003 interview. "Because I was the one who was going to be using it, I made sure it was easy to remember." He forgot to take it out, and the cartridge went on sale in Japan on 25 April 1986 with the cheat still inside. Two years later the American *[Contra](/vault/games/contra)* made it famous as the way to start with 30 lives. By 1991 *[Nintendo Power](/vault/magazines/nintendo-power)* was calling it "the Konami Code", and it has since spread far beyond Konami's games.

## Fast facts

- **Sequence:** ↑ ↑ ↓ ↓ ← → ← → B A, often printed with a final Start.
- **Author:** Kazuhisa Hashimoto, programmer of the Famicom *Gradius*.
- **First game:** *Gradius* on the Famicom, 25 April 1986. The 1985 arcade game does not have it.
- **Made famous by:** the NES *Contra* (1988), where it gives 30 lives instead of three.
- **Named in print:** "The Konami Code strikes again", *Nintendo Power*, February 1991.

## A cheat for the programmer

The arcade *Gradius* was hard, and Hashimoto had to make the home version work. "The arcade version of Gradius is really difficult," he told *Retro Gamer*. "I never played it that much, and there was no way I could finish the game, so I inserted the so-called Konami code." By his own account the conversion took a team of four less than half a year. Konami at the time sent its most experienced programmers to arcade games, and in Hashimoto's words "the employees who didn't know a thing about programming or game creation were put into the custody of a single 'teacher' who would teach them all to program as they went along". *Wireframe* suggests that in a small team moving that fast, a cheat left in the code could easily go unnoticed.

In *Gradius* the code works while the game is paused. Nintendo's European *Club Nintendo* told players to press Start, enter the sequence, and release the pause: the ship, which it called the Warp Rattler, would be supercharged "with options, barriers and missiles". It was not unlimited. The code worked "only a limited number of times; up to the total number of times you destroy the Big Core plus one", the Big Core being the game's recurring boss ship. Because the cheat takes effect as soon as B and A are pressed, the final Start in *Gradius* only unpauses the game. That is why printed versions of the code disagree about whether Start is part of it.

## Contra and the 30 lives

*Gradius* hid the code; *Contra* made it famous. The first issue of *Nintendo Power*, in the summer of 1988, gave the sequence a headline of its own: "Start your game with over 30 extra men with this 'extra life' command." "Contra is not an easy game," it explained. "Sometimes it seems like you get wiped out before you even get started." Entered on the title screen, the code gave 30 lives, and *Nintendo Power* later added that "thirty more fighters will appear when you continue". Players knew it as the "Contra code" or the "30 lives code", and Sega Retro calls it "almost necessary for players to have a chance of beating the game". *Wireframe* credits the game's popularity in America for the fact that the code became tied to *Contra* rather than *Gradius*.

The sequel did not reuse it. *Nintendo Power* reported in 1993 that for *[Super C](/vault/games/super-c)* "a new code was created which allows for ten fighters in reserve": right, left, down, up, A, B, Start.

## Konami's house code

By the early 1990s the sequence was turning up across Konami's range. In February 1991 *Nintendo Power* opened a tip for the Game Boy's *Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles: Fall of the Foot Clan* with "The Konami Code strikes again", describing it as "the code that works on a number of Konami games". In that game, pausing and entering it refilled the energy meter once per game. In January 1993 the magazine's Classified Information section treated it as a convention in its own right: "The programmers of Konami games created a long-standing code which makes for special circumstances in several games."

| Game | What the code did | Where to enter it |
|------|-------------------|-------------------|
| *Contra* | 30 lives, and 30 more on continuing | Title screen |
| *[Life Force](/vault/games/life-force)* | "extend your ships in reserve to a total of 29" | Title screen |
| *Gradius* | "Your ship will be loaded with special Power-Up items" | Paused |
| *Nemesis* (Game Boy) | "what it does for Gradius" | Paused |
| *Fall of the Foot Clan* (Game Boy) | Refills the energy meter once | Paused |

It was never universal. *Wireframe* notes that the first three *[Castlevania](/vault/games/castlevania)* games on the NES did not have it, and that the 1990 spin-off *Kid Dracula* recognised the code but only displayed a message meaning "There's nothing here". Sega Retro finds that "for many years, the Konami code was restricted to just Konami games", with "even Konami failing to include it in many of their releases". On Mega Drive it works in Konami's *Castlevania: Bloodlines* and in a few games from other publishers, such as *The Jungle Book* and *Mega Turrican*. On the Saturn it does something useful mainly in Konami's shoot-'em-up compilations, such as the *Gradius Deluxe Pack*.

## Beyond Konami

The code's fame grew through the 1990s, when Konami put it into newer series such as *[Dance Dance Revolution](/vault/games/dance-dance-revolution)* and *[Metal Gear Solid](/vault/games/metal-gear-solid)*. By the 2000s other studios were using it as an Easter egg; *Wireframe* lists *The Bard's Tale*, *LittleBigPlanet 2*, *BioShock Infinite* and *Just Cause 4*. Web developers hid it in pages too. Typed on the *Overwatch* website, it sent the game's D.Va mechs trickling down the screen; on a Bank of Canada page for a commemorative $10 note, it set off a shower of banknotes and a warbling version of the Canadian national anthem.

Hashimoto's death, at 61, was reported in February 2020. The news came first from Yuji Takenouchi, a composer who had worked with him at Konami in the 1980s, and Konami remembered him as "a deeply talented producer who first introduced the world to the 'Konami Code'". Besides *Gradius*, he worked on Konami's *Ganbare Goemon*, *Lethal Enforcers* and *Snatcher*.
