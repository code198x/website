A computer magazine could reach readers in another country in one of three ways. A local publisher could license it and write its own edition. The home publisher could simply send its own magazine abroad under a new cover number. Or a manufacturer could produce a club magazine once and translate it for each market. The arrangement decided how far the editions could drift apart. *The Games Machine* shows the extreme: Britain's edition closed in 1990 after 34 issues, and the Italian edition was still being published in 2026, at issue 419.

## The licensed edition

A local publisher buys the right to make its own version of a magazine, and staffs it.

Milan's [Edizioni Hobby](/vault/companies/edizioni-hobby) licensed [*Zzap!*](/vault/magazines/zzap-64) from [Newsfield](/vault/companies/newsfield) in 1986 and [*The Games Machine*](/vault/magazines/the-games-machine-italy) in 1988. Both colophons carried a licence line; *The Games Machine*'s read **"Su licenza di NEWSFIELD PUBLICATION"** (under licence from Newsfield Publication). The Italian editions translated British articles, sometimes months late, and mixed them with reviews, news and letters written in Milan. They also set their own direction. The Italian *The Games Machine* opened by claiming to be the first magazine in Italy "interamente dedicata al divertimento elettronico ed ai sedici bit" ("entirely devoted to electronic entertainment and to sixteen-bit machines").

Because the licensee was a separate business, the editions could part company, and these did. The British *The Games Machine* closed in September 1990. Newsfield went into liquidation a year later, and the Italian magazines appeared under a new publisher's name, [Xenia Edizioni](/vault/companies/xenia-edizioni), which one of their writers later described as the old publisher renamed. From October 1991 the licence line was gone. The Italian *Zzap!* lasted until December 1993; *The Games Machine* changed hands several times more and is still on sale.

**How to recognise it:** a licence line in the colophon, local staff on the masthead, and a run that stops matching the original's.

## The export

One publisher and one editorial team make the magazine; only the cover number and the small print change.

*[Amiga Computing](/vault/magazines/amiga-computing)* took over American subscribers in 1995, after "the previous Amiga World database" was transferred to it. The first copy they received, dated June 1995, was the British issue 87 with an overseas cover price of £4.25, plus a letter welcoming them to *Amiga Computing US Edition*. From July the American copies were numbered separately, starting at "Issue 2", and were printed in the USA with American subscription rates of $44 or $70 a year. The editor, Paul Austin, and the Macclesfield editorial office were the British magazine's, and the July copy led on the reviews the covering letter had promised.

**How to recognise it:** a run that restarts its numbering while the parent keeps counting, with the same staff on both mastheads.

## The manufacturer's club magazine

Here the magazine is produced once, centrally, and translated.

[*Club Nintendo*](/vault/magazines/club-nintendo) appeared in 1989 in English, German and Dutch. All three first issues run to twenty pages, all put Mark Smith at the head of the masthead and name Catalyst Publishing of Warwick for the design or printing, and all give the same advice on photographing a high score off the television screen. The main differences are the language and the club address: a PO box for British members, a Hamburg Postfach for German ones and a Postbus in Breukelen for Dutch ones. A German special edition of 1990, *Club Nintendo Classic*, named the arrangement: it was published by Nintendo of Europe GmbH "in Zusammenarbeit mit" (in cooperation with) the Japanese publisher Tokuma Shoten.

Not every manufacturer's magazine worked this way, and the three examples differ in how close the publisher stood to the manufacturer:

| | Published by | Made |
|---|---|---|
| *[Club Nintendo](/vault/magazines/club-nintendo)* | Nintendo of Europe, Nintendo's subsidiary (named from 1990) | once, translated for each country |
| *[Atari Club Magazin](/vault/magazines/atari-club-magazin)* | Atari's German company in Hamburg | in Germany |
| *[Club Commodore](/vault/magazines/club-commodore)* | Microelectrónica y Control, Commodore's exclusive Spanish distributor | in Spain |

*Atari Club Magazin*, which began in 1982, reads as a German production: it told members to watch for Atari advertisements in *Stern*, and described games its staff had been shown in America. *Club Commodore*, from Barcelona, was the loosest tie. It spoke for the distributor, with articles signed by the company's publicity department, but it also printed technical material such as a Commodore 64 memory map, and a reader's program that let a VIC-20 receive radio-teletype signals from radio amateurs and news agencies.

## Where France drew the line

France had a state body that judged whether a computer magazine counted as journalism at all, and it drew the line somewhere else. In December 1986 [*ST Magazine*](/vault/magazines/st-magazine) told readers that the **Commission paritaire**, which granted the press registration that brought lower VAT and cheaper postage, had refused it. Magazines devoted to a single computer standard, the commission had decided, were "grosso modo, à des catalogues de publicité pour la marque" ("more or less, advertising catalogues for the brand"). The magazine answered in its editorial, "Non ! Non ! Nous ne sommes pas des journalistes !" ("No! No! We are not journalists!"), and complained that its subscribers would pay the price.

That test was about how narrow a magazine's subject was, not who owned it. On it, an independent magazine about one computer counted as promotion.

In East Germany the state licensed computing journals into existence. *[Mikroprozessortechnik](/vault/magazines/mikroprozessortechnik)*, launched in East Berlin in 1987, printed "Lizenz-Nr. 1710 des Presseamtes beim Vorsitzenden des Ministerrates der Deutschen Demokratischen Republik": licence no. 1710 of the Press Office of the Chairman of the GDR Council of Ministers.

## Crossing a border one program at a time

All three arrangements move a whole magazine. The Italian weekly *[PAPERsoft](/vault/magazines/papersoft)* moved software one listing at a time. From 15 June 1984 it printed type-in programs and nothing else, and its contents page credited each listing twice: to its original author, and to the Italian who translated and adapted it ("trad. e adatt. di"). Readers in Italy could type in foreign programs, with their text in Italian, years before much commercial software was translated for them.

## When the colophon says nothing

Each arrangement above can be identified because the magazine said what it was. The Italian *The Games Machine* printed its licence line. *Club Nintendo Classic* named Nintendo of Europe and Tokuma Shoten. Britain's *Nintendo Magazine System* printed that Nintendo's trademarks were used "under licence to EMAP Images Limited".

Some magazines said nothing. *[Australian Personal Computer](/vault/magazines/australian-personal-computer)* launched in Melbourne in May 1980 with a British "Editor (U.K.)" on its masthead and British contributors such as David Tebbutt and Sheridan Williams, but named no British magazine. The Australian *[Nintendo Magazine System](/vault/magazines/nintendo-magazine-system)*, published in Sydney by an Australian company, Trielle Corporation in 1994, shared its title with EMAP's British magazine and did not mention EMAP, though its masthead credited five of the British magazine's reviewers and its designer, Gary Harrod, and it carried a Nintendo copyright notice. Both look like licensed editions, but neither says so, so neither can be counted as one.

The three arrangements are the ones these magazines document. Others existed, including co-editions and content-sharing deals, and a magazine that crossed a border without saying how cannot be sorted into any of them.
