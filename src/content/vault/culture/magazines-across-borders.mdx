A magazine that sold in a second country did it in one of three ways. The
distinction is not a legal technicality: it decides whether the two editions can
end up being different magazines, and in one case here the difference runs to
nine years and seventy-eight issues.

## The licensed edition

A local publisher buys the right to make its own version, and staffs it.

[*Zzap!*](/vault/magazines/zzap-64) went to
[Edizioni Hobby](/vault/companies/edizioni-hobby) of Milan in 1986 and
[*The Games Machine*](/vault/magazines/the-games-machine-italy) followed in 1988,
both printing **"Su licenza di NEWSFIELD PUBLICATION"** in the colophon. The
Italian editions ran translated [Newsfield](/vault/companies/newsfield) material
alongside Italian staff, an Italian letters page, and an editorial pitch of their
own — the Italian TGM opened by claiming to be the first magazine in Italy
devoted to 16-bit.

Because the licensee is a separate business making separate decisions, the two
editions can diverge, and these did completely. The British TGM closed in
September 1990 after 34 issues. The Italian one dropped the licence line when
Newsfield collapsed, changed publisher, and ran until May 1999 — 112 issues.

**The tell:** a licence line in the colophon, local staff on the masthead, and a
run length that stops matching.

## The re-badged export

One publisher, one editorial operation, a different number on the cover.

*[Amiga Computing](/vault/magazines/amiga-computing)* appeared from June 1995 as
*Amiga Computing US Edition*, renumbered from 1 — the first US issue being
British issue **87**. The renumbering is the only thing that makes it look like a
separate magazine; the British subscription rates are still printed inside.

Nothing here can diverge, because there is nothing separate to do the diverging.

**The tell:** a run that restarts at 1 while the parent keeps counting, and
domestic prices left in the back.

## The manufacturer's house organ

Produced once, centrally, and localised outward.

[*Club Nintendo*](/vault/magazines/club-nintendo) appeared in 1989 in English,
German and Dutch. The three first issues are the same twenty pages in the same
order, down to the same advice on photographing your television and the same
joke on page 20. The only difference is the address readers write to, because a
club has to be joined somewhere.

The German edition names the arrangement: published by **Nintendo of Europe
GmbH** in cooperation with **Tokuma Shoten** of Tokyo, under project leads titled
*national* and *international*. There is no local editorial judgement anywhere in
that chain, so the editions cannot drift.

**The tell:** identical page numbering across languages, and a colophon naming
the manufacturer rather than a publisher.

## Why it matters for reading the record

A Vault written from one country's holdings will describe a licensed edition's
parent as the whole story, and be wrong about how long the magazine existed and
where most of it was published. *The Games Machine* read from British holdings
is a three-year magazine; counted across this library it is an eleven-year one
whose longest phase is Italian.

The other two shapes carry the opposite risk. A re-badged export and a house
organ both *look* like separate national magazines — separate numbering,
separate addresses — and counting them as such would inflate a territory's press
with editions that were never written there.

⚠ Three arrangements are what this library's holdings show, not a claim that
there were only three. Co-editions, joint ventures and reciprocal content deals
all existed in the period, and none is documented here.
