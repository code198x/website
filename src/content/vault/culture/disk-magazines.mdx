**Disk magazines** (diskmags) were publications distributed on floppy disk rather than paper. They combined articles with interactive elements—music, demos, custom interfaces—creating a unique medium that bridged traditional publishing and the demo scene.

## The commercial model, in numbers

*Loadstar* is the best-documented case, because it advertised heavily across the
American Commodore press for years. It was **monthly software** from Softdisk
Publishing, two 5¼″ disks an issue, sold by subscription: "A three-month trial
subscription to Loadstar is only $19.95 (three big issues). That's just $6.65 an
issue."

What made it a publication rather than a compilation was everything around the
disk:

- **Quality control.** "Every issue of Loadstar passes through dozens of quality
  checks, all to ensure you of error-free enjoyment."
- **A support desk.** "You can count on the Loadstar technical staff to answer
  your questions quickly."
- **Publisher relationships.** "Major software companies from around the world
  offer our subscribers advanced previews of what's to come in commercial
  software."
- **Back issues**, a money-back guarantee on the first disk, and a Software Savers
  Club offering members "up to 40% off retail price".

By November 1990 it was listing the contents of issue #72, and it was still buying
full pages in *RUN* in 1991 — across competing titles, not just a house organ.

**Loadstar and Softdisk were the same company.** The entry's table lists them as
separate publications, which is right, but the *Loadstar* coupon is made payable
"to Softdisk Publishing". One Shreveport publisher ran a disk magazine on the
Commodore and another on the PC.

## Sold in newsagents

Subscription and scene distribution were not the only routes. *Zero* carried an
advertisement in June 1990 for a disk-based magazine going into "OUR NEWSAGENTS
FROM APRIL 26th 1990" — a diskmag on the shelf, priced and racked like a paper
one. (The masthead is not legible in the scan held here; the model and the date
are.)

## Where the two ends met

The commercial and scene halves of the taxonomy above were not sealed off from
each other. *ST Format*'s public-domain pages reviewed and scored scene diskmags
alongside utilities and games — Floppyshop's *Diskmag* at 83%, *Dreams Diskmag*
at 54%, another described as "a highly dodgy diskmag that's packed with features"
— and PD libraries sold them by post at PD prices. A scene diskmag could be
bought, reviewed and marked out of a hundred like any other software.
