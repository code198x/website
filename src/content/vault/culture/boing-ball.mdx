The **Boing Ball** is a demo shown at the Amiga's debut at Winter CES in January 1984, a red and white checkered ball bouncing around the screen with smooth animation and a convincing thud. It became the Amiga's unofficial mascot—the image that symbolised what made this machine different. The Boing Ball proved the Amiga could do things no other home computer could match.

## Written at the show, not for it

The demo was not prepared and packed. It was written on the CES floor because what
the team had brought was not good enough:

> Mical and Luck worked furiously at the show itself to improve upon the software
> the team had brought with them. They created the first version of the Boing demo
> in Amiga's little rented back room on the CES show floor in an all-night hacking
> session fueled by "a six-pack of warm beer."

Three people are credited on the original: **Sam Dicker, Dale Luck and RJ Mical**.

**And the machine barely worked.** The Lorraine prototype "was a cobbled collection
of circuit boards and wires that failed constantly", with hardware technicians who
"brought piles of chips and other spare parts with them and learned to repair the
machine on the fly during demonstrations". The programmers had had access to it
"for just a few days before the show".

It had no operating system, no tools, and — the detail that makes the all-nighter
remarkable — no keyboard: "the Lorraine did not even have a keyboard or, indeed,
any user interface at all; it could be operated only by uploading a program from a
remote terminal connection."

**The stakes were the company.** Amiga, Incorporated "was quickly running out of
money and desperately needed to demonstrate something to the potential additional
investors who might keep it afloat", with founders "mortgaging their houses to keep
the lights on".

The demo outlived all of it as a brand. By 1990 Dale Luck was selling "the Boing
Mouse, $125... from Dale Luck's Boing! company".

## Fast facts

- **Created:** overnight at the show, January 1984
- **Creators:** Sam Dicker, [Dale Luck](/vault/people/dale-luck) and RJ Mical
- **Event:** Consumer Electronics Show
- **Purpose:** show what the Amiga could do, to investors who might keep the company alive
- **Status:** Unofficial mascot
- **Legacy:** Still used in Amiga branding

## Technical Cleverness

The rotation was an illusion:

- The ball never rotated
- Colours cycled through palette
- Created appearance of spin
- Minimal CPU overhead
- Demonstrated custom chip power

## CES Impact

At the 1984 Consumer Electronics Show:

- Attendees stopped to watch
- Nothing else looked like this
- IBM PCs showed CGA text
- Macs displayed black and white
- The Amiga bounced a ball

## Cultural significance

The Boing Ball represented:

- **Technical superiority** - "Your computer can't do this"
- **Playfulness** - Not just serious computing
- **Innovation** - Genuinely new capabilities
- **Identity** - Instant Amiga recognition

## Persistence

The Boing Ball endured:

- Amiga company logos
- User group imagery
- Retro computing references
- Emulator icons
- Community branding

## Why it mattered

In 1984, home computers displayed text and flat blocks of colour. The Boing Ball showed something that looked like television—smooth, colourful, alive. It wasn't a game or an application; it was pure demonstration that the Amiga was fundamentally different.

## Legacy

Decades later, the Boing Ball remains the instant visual identifier for the Amiga platform. It represents not just a technical demo but a moment when home computing changed—when machines stopped being calculators with screens and started being multimedia devices.
