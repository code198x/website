**Amiga art** had a distinctive look that set it apart from graphics on other platforms. The combination of hardware capabilities (4096 colours in HAM mode, smooth gradients) and tools (Deluxe Paint) created an aesthetic characterised by rich colour gradients, skilled dithering, airbrushed realism, and fantasy themes. This style defined game graphics for years and influenced digital art well beyond the Amiga era.

## Much of it was photographed

⚠ **The entry credits the look to hardware and Deluxe Paint. A large part of it came
through a lens.** *Amiga Format*'s 1990 digitising guide is, in substance, a
studio-lighting tutorial:

- **Camera.** Shoot monochrome. "Black and white cameras tend to have a higher
  resolution than their colour counterparts and they're a good deal cheaper" — the
  Panasonic WV140 at around £200.
- **Lighting.** Fluorescent lamps, "as these simulate daylight convincingly", "three
  or four shining on the object". Too much light produces "Moire patterns"; too
  little gives "grainy grabs".
- **Stability.** Fasten the camera "to a tripod or copy stand". Movement shows as
  "wavy lines or an out-of-focus look".

**Dithering was partly a camera setting.** "When using 32 colours or less, it is a
good idea to raise the sharpness level as this increases dithering and smooths out
colour bands that sometimes appear." Lowering it instead would "remove the
'confetti' or haze from 4096-colour pictures" — HAM fringing, managed at the lens
rather than in the paint package.

**And the isolated-subject composition was a palette strategy.** "If you are
digitising a picture into 32 colours or less, try to have a plain background behind
the subject. A single-colour background will not detract from the main object, and
it gives the illusion that more colours are available in the area of interest." The
flat backdrop so common in Amiga imagery is colours being spent where the eye goes.

⚠ **On "primary tool".** For 4096-colour work the period advice was not Deluxe
Paint: "DigiPaint 3 (£69.95 from Precision) is a good choice when it comes to HAM
painting". Deluxe Paint did not support HAM until version IV.

## Fast facts

- **Era:** 1985-1995
- **Primary tools:** [Deluxe Paint](/vault/tools/deluxe-paint); DigiPaint or similar for HAM until DPaint IV
- **Also:** video digitisers, a mono camera and studio lighting
- **Colour depth:** 32/64/4096 colours
- **Characteristics:** Gradients, [dithering](/vault/techniques/dithering), chrome
- **Themes:** Fantasy, sci-fi, airbrushed realism
- **Legacy:** Still influences pixel art

## Visual Characteristics

The Amiga look:

| Technique | Effect |
|-----------|--------|
| **Smooth gradients** | Rich colour transitions |
| **Dithering patterns** | Extend limited palettes |
| **Chrome/metallic** | Reflective surfaces |
| **Airbrushed shading** | Soft, realistic lighting |
| **Anti-aliasing** | Smooth edges manually |

## Why It Looked Different

The Amiga enabled this style:

| Capability | Impact |
|------------|--------|
| **HAM mode** | All 4096 colours |
| **32-colour mode** | Rich gaming palettes |
| **Smooth playback** | Hardware support |
| **Deluxe Paint** | Tools for the style |

| Platform | Look |
|----------|------|
| **C64** | Blocky, limited palette |
| **Atari ST** | Similar but fewer colours |
| **PC (CGA/EGA)** | Harsh, limited |
| **Amiga** | Smooth, rich, "painted" |

## Common themes

Amiga artists favoured:

- **Fantasy scenes** - Dragons, warriors, castles
- **Science fiction** - Spaceships, chrome robots
- **Portraits** - Photorealistic faces
- **Landscapes** - Dramatic lighting
- **Metallic surfaces** - Chrome everything

## The Artists

Masters of the style:

- **Jim Sachs** - Photorealistic detail
- **Roger Dean** - Fantasy landscapes
- **Dan Malone** (Bitmap Brothers) - Game art
- **Various demo scene** - Technical showcases

## Game Art Impact

The Amiga look defined game graphics:

- **Cinemaware titles** - Defender of the Crown
- **Bitmap Brothers** - Speedball, Gods
- **Team17** - Alien Breed, Worms
- **Psygnosis** - Shadow of the Beast

## Legacy

The Amiga art style trained a generation of digital artists in:

- Palette management
- Dithering techniques
- Lighting and shading
- Working within constraints

These skills transferred to later careers in game development, illustration, and digital art. The aesthetic itself remains influential in pixel art communities and indie games seeking that "painted" quality.
