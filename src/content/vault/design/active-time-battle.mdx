In the first three *Final Fantasy* games, a battle waited for you. You chose a command for each character, the round played out, and the monsters stood politely still while you thought about the next one. Square's *Final Fantasy IV* put the fight on a clock. Each character has a gauge that fills in real time and may act only when it is full, and the enemies have gauges too: if you dither in the menu, they hit you anyway. Square called it the Active Time Battle, took out patents on it in the United States, and used it in every main *Final Fantasy* for the rest of the 1990s.

## Fast facts

- **Introduced:** *Final Fantasy IV* (Super Famicom, 1991), released in America as *Final Fantasy II*.
- **Patent:** US 5,390,937, naming Hironobu Sakaguchi and Hiroyuki Itou as inventors, with a Japanese priority date of 16 July 1991; granted in 1995, with a divisional patent, 5,649,862, in 1997.
- **The rule:** "a time gauge for each character indicates whether they are busy or can be assigned new orders. The enemy will continue to attack in realtime whether you act or not" (*Edge*, 1996).
- **Variants:** Charge Time Battle in *Final Fantasy Tactics*; *Parasite Eve* and *Xenogears*.
- **Replaced:** by the turn-order Count Time Battle in *Final Fantasy X* (2001); its gauges returned in *Final Fantasy XIII*.

## How it works

*Edge* gave the clearest short account when it previewed [Final Fantasy VII](/vault/games/final-fantasy-vii) in October 1996:

> FFVII retains the 'Active Time Battle' concept. All combat is menu-driven but a time gauge for each character indicates whether they are busy or can be assigned new orders. The enemy will continue to attack in realtime whether you act or not, so quick decision-making is rewarded.

*Computer and Video Games* added the detail that makes the system tick. Both sides "have to wait until they have recovered enough stamina to make a move", and the speed at which each character's time bar recovers "is governed by the experience level of the character, or any number of effects caused by enemy magic". A fast character acts more often than a slow one, and a spell that speeds up or slows down a gauge changes the shape of the whole fight.

Square's patent describes the same machinery as a programmer would build it. A time "stipulated for each character" is counted "from the end of a previous action"; when it runs out, a player character may be given a command, and an enemy may attack "even while the player character is in the process of inputting a command". In code terms each combatant carries a timer that fills at its own rate, the game loop keeps advancing all of them while the menus are open, and a full timer either opens a menu or lets the computer choose.

The clock could be tuned. *Nintendo Power*'s 1991 feature on the American release listed a configuration screen where players could "customize screen colors, battle speed and message speed", a setting that only makes sense when battles run in time. By *Final Fantasy VII* the range was wide: "While it's possible for beginners to restrict fights to a simple taking-turns structure," *Edge* wrote in its review, "at its realtime fastest ... battles can elevate to almost action-game status."

## A patented idea

*Edge* called it "Square's patented Active Time Battle system", and the patent was real. US patent 5,390,937, "Video game apparatus, method and device for controlling same", was filed in March 1992 on the strength of a Japanese application of 16 July 1991, and granted in February 1995. It names Square's [Hironobu Sakaguchi](/vault/people/hironobu-sakaguchi) and Hiroyuki Itou as the inventors. A second patent, 5,649,862, split off from the first and was granted in 1997. Square printed both numbers in the legal line of its 1999 advertisement for *Final Fantasy Anthology*, the American reissue of *Final Fantasy V* and *VI*.

## The 1990s

[Chrono Trigger](/vault/games/chrono-trigger) (1995) took the gauges out of the separate battle screen. *Nintendo Power* explained that its fights were "menu driven like traditional RPGs", but took place "in the world in which you move around, not on separate battle screens", and that "the action can take place in real-time or in turns". *Super Play*'s feature on the game that June ran a boxout on Active Time Battle beside a chronology of Square's games.

On the PlayStation, Square's other role-playing games were measured against it. *Edge* described [Final Fantasy Tactics](/vault/games/final-fantasy-tactics)' Charge Time Battle in April 1998 as giving "characters of both sides a 100-unit Charge Time gauge, restricting movement and action (known as Active Time) until the gauge is full", with fill rates that meant "different characters become active at different times", "minimising the board-game feel of taking turns". *Parasite Eve*'s combat that November was "derived from the usual Final Fantasy-style Active Time Battle, where attacks can only be made once a character's AT gauge has filled", but let both sides "move anywhere within the combat area". [Xenogears](/vault/games/xenogears)' "fighting game-style combat cleverly disguises what is essentially a variation on Square's well-worn Active Time Battle system".

## After the PlayStation

*Final Fantasy X* (2001) dropped it. *IGN Unplugged* reported that the game "replaced the Active Time Battle system that has been in use since FFIV with the Count Time Battle (CTB) system", in which turns come round one at a time in an order set by each character's Speed and shown in a window on screen. It was a turn-based system again, and the player could see who would act next.

The gauges came back. Reviewing *Final Fantasy XIII* in 2010, *Edge* found it "remarkable" that "those Active Time Battle gauges still anchor" a much faster combat system, and in 2013 it described the series' "trademark Active Time Battles" in a preview of *Lightning Returns*. Developers who grew up with the 1990s games kept citing it: in 2021 Edd Parris, the sole developer of *Jack Move*, told *Wireframe* that *Final Fantasy VII* was the first role-playing game he played and that he "fell in love with that turn-based 'Active Time Battle' system".
