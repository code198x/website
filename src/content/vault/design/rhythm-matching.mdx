A rhythm game asks one question, over and over: did you press the button on the beat? The game knows when each note in the music falls; it compares the moment of each press with that moment, grades the gap, and lets the grade change what happens next. *PaRappa the Rapper* (1996) did it with four versions of each song on the CD, swapping to a worse one when the player slipped. Konami's arcade games turned the notes into a scrolling chart and the button into a turntable, a dance mat or a plastic guitar. By 2010 *Edge* could say that "the scrolling note charts, coloured prompts and peripherals that define modern rhythm-gaming are all Konami creations", and that the Japanese games were played for their "fractional hit-windows for notes".

## Fast facts

- **The mechanic:** button presses judged against the timing of a piece of music, with a grade for each one and feedback in the score, the pictures or the music itself.
- **First game the sources treat as the origin:** [PaRappa the Rapper](/vault/games/parappa-the-rapper) (Sony, 1996), by [Masaya Matsuura](/vault/people/masaya-matsuura).
- **Ancestors the period press named:** Milton Bradley's *Simon* (1978), *Quest for Fame* (Virtual Music), Sega's *Digital Dance Mix*.
- **Genre name:** "rhythm action games", reported by *Edge* as a Japanese press label in December 1997.
- **Format-setter:** [Konami](/vault/companies/konami)'s [Bemani](/vault/companies/bemani) games: [beatmania](/vault/games/beatmania) (1997), [Dance Dance Revolution](/vault/games/dance-dance-revolution) (1998), GuitarFreaks, DrumMania, Pop'n Music.
- **Western turn:** [Harmonix](/vault/companies/harmonix)'s [Guitar Hero](/vault/games/guitar-hero) (2005), whose founder credits PaRappa.

## PaRappa the Rapper

*Computer and Video Games* reviewed the UK release in September 1997 and described how it worked. An onion head "moves across the rhythm bar at the top of the screen, passing symbols relating to the lyrics. Now a little PaRappa head passes over the symbols, and you have to hit the corresponding buttons in time." Judgement was a four-step meter: Cool, Good, Bad and Awful. The player had to stay at Good or above to finish a stage; reaching Cool made the tutor "pop-off", leaving the player to freestyle.

Two months later [Matsuura](/vault/people/masaya-matsuura) told the magazine how the judgement reached the player's ears. The soundtrack was streamed from CD, not played by the sound chip: "It has four different tracks of the same type, for cool, good, bad and awful. Play 'cool' and the other tracks aren't heard. If the score system decides you're not good, this cool track disappears and another comes in." He had been composing on computers since 1983, and had already tried the track-switching idea in a prototype on Philips' CD-i.

The reviewers reached for older games to explain it. *C&VG*: "PaRappa's roots go further back to the electronic Simon game by MB, and schoolyard game Follow the Leader." [Jez San](/vault/people/jez-san) of [Argonaut](/vault/companies/argonaut), reviewing it for *Edge* in January 1998, offered "Take 'Simon', the old electronic melody pattern game, add an ounce of Frogger for timing puzzles". His one design complaint was the problem later games fixed: "you can't tap the buttons along with the rapping, because they don't always match up with the visual button sequences".

Whether it was a game at all was a live question. *C&VG*'s answer was "neither. It's an experience!"; Matsuura said he did not care.

## The Japanese wave, 1997–2000

**Bust a Move** ([Enix](/vault/companies/enix), developed by Metro) appeared in *Edge*'s Prescreen pages in December 1997 as "the latest in a new genre to emerge in Japan, currently being labelled by the local press as 'rhythm action games'". Its inputs were strings of commands entered "in a similar fashion to how specials are unleashed in beat 'em ups". Metro's designer Noburo Shirasu told *Edge* the project had started in October 1996 and been beaten to market by PaRappa: "Bust-a-Move is often compared to PaRappa because it is based on a similar rhythm system." It reached the West as *Bust a Groove*.

**[beatmania](/vault/games/beatmania)** (Konami, 1997) put the judgement on a peripheral. *Edge*'s Japan column in September 1998 described "a literally revolutionary 'turntable' controller which players use to scratch and cut along with a dance track", a following among "the chic young gamers of Tokyo's trendy Shibuya district", and a PlayStation conversion with a ¥5,800 turntable made by ASCII. The caption tied it back: "Beatmania's gameplay follows that of Parappa the Rapper to a degree, as players attempt to match an oncoming flow of 'digital-sheetmusic' instructions." That oncoming flow, notes scrolling towards a line where the press is judged, is the form most rhythm games have used since.

**[Dance Dance Revolution](/vault/games/dance-dance-revolution)** (Konami, 1998) moved the buttons under the feet, on a [dance mat](/vault/hardware/dance-mat). *Edge* noted the home floor controller in August 1999 at ¥5,500, adding: "They said it would never take off in Europe, and it certainly hasn't on anything like the scale it did in Japan."

**[Space Channel 5](/vault/games/space-channel-5)** ([Sega](/vault/companies/sega), 1999) came from [Tetsuya Mizuguchi](/vault/people/tetsuya-mizuguchi), who had produced *Sega Rally* in the arcades and had just set up Sega's Software R&D No. 9 to make a rhythm-action game for the Dreamcast. *Edge* described the input: "you beat out the tempo of the music with one finger while executing different dance commands with the other hand - the closer you get to following the instructions in time, the more characters join in the dancing." Mizuguchi's stated audience was "people who don't play games", and *Edge* judged it "a lot easier than Bust A Groove".

**Samba de Amigo** (Sega, 2000) shipped on Dreamcast with motion-sensing maracas, for ¥13,600 in Japan. *Edge*'s review: "six coloured circles inform the player of the height and position at which the maracas must be shaken", to tunes from the "Macarena" to a rock "Take on Me"; it credited [Yuji Naka](/vault/people/yuji-naka) with the structure that kept people playing. *Edge* called it "another Bemani title", using Konami's brand as the name of the genre.

## What Konami built

*Edge*'s 2010 retrospective summarised the division's output. Konami's Games & Music Division, "later renamed the Bemani division in a nod to its breakthrough game, was the original pioneer of peripheral-led music gaming", and before 2000 it had shipped GuitarFreaks, DrumMania, Dance Dance Revolution and Pop'n Music. The appeal of Japanese rhythm-action, *Edge* thought, had "little to do with the actual songs, and everything to do with the primal twitch-gaming rush of matching impossibly fast patterns".

## The Western turn

Harmonix founder Alex Rigopulos told *Edge* in 2010 that his studio's move from music software into games "began with Parappa The Rapper": "it wasn't the first rhythm-action game, but it was the first great one, and it altered Harmonix's trajectory." He had seen Virtual Music's *Quest for Fame* and not found it compelling, and found Konami's games "sorely deficient in a number of key respects: the gameplay was shallow, the visual design was uninspired, the music selection and sound quality was poor". Harmonix's own *Frequency* "sold barely any copies".

*[Guitar Hero](/vault/games/guitar-hero)* (2005) took Konami's arcade idea of a guitar game, with its scrolling chart, to a Western rock soundtrack and a controller with "five multi-coloured fret buttons". *Edge*'s report on the game's stage at the 2006 Download festival noted that Harmonix "has already branded its titles 'superstar simulators'", and that *Guitar Hero* "has brought music games out of the arcades and living rooms and into a more public arena". The 2010 feature gave the peak: almost two million rhythm games sold in one month, with *Guitar Hero 3* in November 2007, and $1.19 billion spent on music games in North America in a single month of 2008. By then, it said, sales were falling faster than the recession could explain, and "there's a feeling that we may have seen the genre's peak".
