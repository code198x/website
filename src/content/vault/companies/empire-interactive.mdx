## Overview

Empire's catalogue is almost entirely **simulation, strategy and wargames** at a time when the Amiga's commercial centre was arcade conversions and platform games. Its prices show the consequence: where most Amiga releases sat at £25.99, Empire routinely asked **£29.99 to £34.99**, and the games it asked it for were tank campaigns and flight simulators.

## Fast facts

- **Press evidence in this library:** reviewed in *Amiga Power* and *Amiga Format* from **1991 to 1995**, at **£24.99–£34.99** — no budget tier at all.
- **Published:** *Campaign* and *Campaign 2*, *War in the Gulf*, *Twilight 2000*, *Dawn Patrol*, *Cyberspace*, *Maelstrom*, *DreamWeb*, *Empire Soccer '94*, *Magic Boy*.
- **Developers:** Jonathan Griffiths (*Campaign*), Rowan Software (*Dawn Patrol*), Creative Reality (*DreamWeb*), and some in-house work.

## Pricing for a different reader

A £34.99 Amiga game in 1992 was near the top of the market, and it makes sense only if the buyer is a different person from the one buying platformers.

That buyer existed. They wanted **the simulation to be accurate more than they wanted it to be quick to learn**, would read a hundred-page manual, and treated a game as a winter's project, not a weekend's. That audience is small, and it does not shop on price — which is precisely why a specialist publisher could charge more, and had to.

*Amiga Power*, a magazine whose sympathies lay firmly with fast and funny games, could be caught grudgingly making room for it. One of its Empire reviews opens by grumbling: "They want a two-page wargame review? Then they've got one." The genre had readers; it just was not the ones the magazine was written for.

## Where the effort went

*Campaign* (1992) let the player command an armoured formation and then climb into any individual vehicle in it, which is an unusually ambitious joining of the strategic and tactical scales for the hardware. *Dawn Patrol*, from Rowan Software, was a First World War flight simulator built on the same firm's flight-model work. *DreamWeb* is the outlier — a bleak top-down cyberpunk adventure by Creative Reality, better known now than it was then, and one of the few games from this catalogue still readily playable through open-source engines.

That range is worth noting: a publisher known for wargames was also the one that shipped the strangest adventure game on the machine.

## Why it mattered

Empire served **the serious end of the market** — the part of eighties and nineties computing that was never about arcades at all, descended instead from board wargames and from flight training, and that quietly sustained a whole publishing niche.

It also shows something about the Amiga's decline. Empire is still publishing in 1995, after the machine's mass audience had gone, because its buyers were never the mass audience. A specialist market shrinks more slowly than a popular one.
