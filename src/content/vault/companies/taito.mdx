For its first twenty years Taito had nothing to do with video games. Michael Kogan, a Ukrainian businessman in Japan, founded the Taito Trading Company in 1953 to import vending machines and jukeboxes into post-war Japan; *Wireframe* adds that it also distilled and sold vodka. It moved into making its own machines and, in the 1970s, into a new kind of coin-op. Its engineer Tomohiro Nishikado designed many of its early video games, and in 1978 he gave it *[Space Invaders](/vault/games/space-invaders)*. The American press barely knew the company. *Electronic Games* introduced it to readers in 1981 as "a then-small Pachinko company".

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1953 by Michael Kogan, as the Taito Trading Company.
- **First business:** importing vending machines and jukeboxes.
- **Breakthrough:** [Space Invaders](/vault/games/space-invaders) (1978), designed by [Tomohiro Nishikado](/vault/people/tomohiro-nishikado).
- **Later arcade games:** *[Elevator Action](/vault/games/elevator-action)* (1983), *[Arkanoid](/vault/games/arkanoid)* (1986), *[Bubble Bobble](/vault/games/bubble-bobble)* (1986), *[Darius](/vault/games/darius)* (1986), *Operation Wolf* (1987), *[Rainbow Islands](/vault/games/rainbow-islands)* (1987), *[Chase H.Q.](/vault/games/chase-hq)* (1988), *[Puzzle Bobble](/vault/games/puzzle-bobble)* (1994).
- **Owner:** Square Enix since 2005.

## Into video games

Taito's first video games followed Atari's. *Wireframe* names *Astro Race*, a copy of Atari's *Space Race*, and *Pong* variations such as *Davis Cup*, *Soccer* and *Elepong*. Many were Nishikado's. He had joined as an engineer, and he built them from logic chips, without a microprocessor. *Speed Race* (1974) was a driving game with a wheel and a road that scrolled down the screen. *Western Gun* put two cowboys on either side of the screen, and Taito licensed it to [Midway](/vault/companies/midway), which sold it in America as *Gun Fight*.

## Space Invaders

*Space Invaders* came out in Japan in July 1978, and Nishikado had built almost all of it himself: design, graphics, program and hardware. Its invaders fired back and marched towards the player, and it set off what Japan called the "invader game" boom. *Electronic Games* reported that within a year Taito had installed over 100,000 machines and taken over $600 million. Midway, Bally's coin-operated game division, bought the American rights, and in 1980 Atari bought the home rights.

*Electronic Games* also said the Bank of Japan had had to triple its production of 100-yen coins. That story is less solid than the sales. Nishikado himself said, "I don't know whether the coins actually became less common because of the game," and *Retro Gamer* files the shortage among the game's urban myths. The [Space Invaders](/vault/games/space-invaders) entry sets out the evidence.

## Lean years and a revival

*Retro Gamer* describes what the money did. Taito invested heavily in video games, set up Taito America in 1979 to handle North American distribution, and licensed American games such as Amstar's *Phoenix* and Williams' *Defender* for Japan. To keep up with demand it sub-contracted smaller Japanese companies, among them [Data East](/vault/companies/data-east), SNK and [Nintendo](/vault/companies/nintendo). *Wireframe* counts dozens of Taito arcade releases in 1980 alone, including forgotten oddities such as *Safari Rally*.

After the American market collapsed in 1983 Taito concentrated on Japan, and its own designers struggled. *Retro Gamer* judged much of their work of the period "very basic and generic", and said that even minor hits such as *Elevator Action* and *Arkanoid* had done little to dent the popularity of rival hits such as Capcom's *Ghosts 'n Goblins*. It turned to outside developers again, sub-contracting [Toaplan](/vault/companies/toaplan) to make vertical shoot-'em-ups such as *Flying Shark*, and distributed [Technos](/vault/companies/technos)'s *[Renegade](/vault/games/renegade)* and *[Double Dragon](/vault/games/double-dragon)* outside Japan.

The change came from Fukio Mitsuji, whose *Bubble Bobble* (1986) was, in *Retro Gamer*'s words, Taito's in-house team's "first worldwide success since *Space Invaders*". Two players, as the dinosaurs Bub and Bob, trapped monsters in bubbles through 100 screens, and Mitsuji said they had to play together to reach the true ending. He followed it with *Rainbow Islands* (1987), which scrolls up the screen and gives its hero rainbows for weapons and platforms. Other Taito games of the period each had a hook of their own: *Darius* spread one shoot-'em-up across three screens set side by side; *Operation Wolf* fixed a replica machine gun to the cabinet; *Chase H.Q.* put the player in a Porsche with orders to ram criminals' cars off the road. In 1987 Taito formed Zuntata, its sound team and in-house band, and in 1989 it moved most of its games onto a single 68000-based board, the F2 system, to cut the cost of designing hardware for each game.

## On British home computers

Taito's games reached Spectrum and Commodore 64 owners under British labels. Imagine published *Arkanoid*. Firebird published *Bubble Bobble*, and *ZZAP!64* gave the Commodore 64 version 97% in October 1987, calling it "a rare and beautiful occasion when a great arcade machine is perfectly converted". [Graftgold](/vault/companies/graftgold) converted *Rainbow Islands* for Firebird's owner, [Telecomsoft](/vault/companies/telecomsoft), but after MicroProse bought Telecomsoft the deal with Taito fell through and the conversions could not be released. In March 1990 *ACE* reported that [Ocean](/vault/companies/ocean-software) had signed an agreement with Taito for the home computer rights and bought Graftgold's code for a sum "rumoured to be in the region of £50,000". Ocean was already converting Taito's coin-ops: *Operation Wolf*, and *Chase H.Q.*, which *CRASH* gave 95% in December 1989.

## After the golden age

Taito carried on into the 1990s with *Puzzle Bobble* (1994), sold in the West as *Bust-a-Move*, and tried and abandoned a satellite-fed games console, the WoWoW, shown at the 1992 Tokyo Toy Show. Wikipedia records that Michael Kogan died in 1984, when his son Abraham became chairman, and that Taito joined the Kyocera group in 1986. In 2004 *Edge* described its Type X arcade board as "essentially a modified PC running an embedded version of Windows XP", and noted that Taito was building an arcade version of *Half-Life 2* on it.

In 2005 Square Enix announced that it would buy Taito, and it owned all of the company by March 2006. In 2010 Square Enix reorganised it into a new Taito Corporation, which took over the group's arcade business. Taito remains part of Square Enix Holdings. In March 2026 it reported 846 employees; it runs amusement centres, builds and maintains arcade machines, runs online crane games, and makes games and toys. It also looks after its back catalogue: in 2022 it launched the Egret II Mini, a small arcade cabinet preloaded with its older games.
