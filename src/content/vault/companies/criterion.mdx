Criterion began as a Japanese camera company's 3D research lab in a Surrey commuter town, and became best known for teaching racing games to crash. Canon set it up in Guildford in December 1993 to sell [RenderWare](/vault/techniques/renderware), a 3D library that ran on an ordinary PC. Two years later it opened a game studio of its own, partly to show what its engine could do. That studio made *[Burnout](/vault/games/burnout)* (2001), a PlayStation 2 racer in which the other cars on the road were the whole point: you drove into oncoming traffic to fill a boost meter, and when you hit something, the game slowed down to show you the wreck. In 2004 [Electronic Arts](/vault/companies/electronic-arts) bought Criterion, engine and studio together.

## Fast facts

- **Formed:** December 1993, in Guildford, as Criterion Software, a wholly owned subsidiary of Canon.
- **Founder:** David Lau-Kee, from Canon Research, the company's managing director and later chief executive.
- **Game studio:** Criterion Studios, opened January 1996; later Criterion Games.
- **Best known for:** RenderWare; *Burnout* (2001) and its sequels; *Black* (2006); *Need for Speed: Hot Pursuit* (2010).
- **Owner since 2004:** Electronic Arts, which completed the purchase on 19 October 2004.
- **Since 2025:** "Criterion – A Battlefield Studio".

## A tools company in a Canon lab

*[Edge](/vault/magazines/edge)* told the origin story in 1996. Asked to set up a European research and development centre, David Lau-Kee, then working at Canon Research, founded Criterion Software instead. His own work had moved from interactive 2D image processing to 3D image processing and then to 3D graphics, and Canon's long-term plan was to build up multimedia software. Games were not in it. "The games thing is more of a Criterion than a Canon thing," Lau-Kee said. "If you want to see the best use of 2D, 3D, image processing, sound, then it's all in games."

RenderWare was announced in early 1994 and sold to other developers for a royalty; the [RenderWare](/vault/techniques/renderware) entry follows it from a software renderer on a 486 to the engine under *[Grand Theft Auto III](/vault/games/gta-iii)*. By 1996 *Edge* said it was used by over 800 developers.

## Criterion Studios

Selling an engine was not enough. *Edge* noted that engine makers "watch developers and can't help but feel they could do a better job", and in January 1996 "the Guildford-based tools house launched Criterion Studios", already employing about 35 people. Its first three games were *Scorched Planet*, a 3D shooter that Lau-Kee said owed "a great deal to *Defender*", with a jet that turned into a tank when it ran out of fuel; *Sub Culture*, an underwater adventure in which the player was a quarter of an inch tall; and a power-boat racer, *Aqua'Tak*. *Edge* called the studio "an organisation to watch".

*Scorched Planet* came out through [Virgin](/vault/companies/virgin-games) in 1996. *Sub Culture* ended up with [Ubi Soft](/vault/companies/ubisoft), which published it in 1997 on an upgraded RenderWare engine; *Edge* gave it seven out of ten. The studio's next game went to [Acclaim](/vault/companies/acclaim): *TrickStyle* (1999), a hoverboard racer for the Sega Dreamcast. Ubi Soft also published *Deep Fighter*, another submarine game, reviewed on the PC in 2000.

By 2001 the studio had changed. "Two games. That's all it's taken Criterion to alter its image," *Edge* wrote when it visited the "Canon-owned codeshop" in Guildford that September. "The right people have been employed, a number have left." The two games were *AirBlade*, a hoverboard game for Sony, and a racer that had begun as a prototype called "Shiny Red Car".

## Burnout

Alex Ward started "Shiny Red Car" with a small team: a stretch of city streets, some lorries and cars, and a small red coupé to drive between them. Paul Glancey later recalled that it did not even have collisions at first. Publishers were not interested. "The game that publishers were really looking for was something like *Gran Turismo*," Glancey said, and Criterion's reputation as a maker of big games "hadn't really kicked in". Acclaim, which had published *TrickStyle*, agreed to take it.

The traffic was the idea. Hamish Young, who wrote the game's AI, told *Edge* that *Burnout* updated "about 300 cars a frame", and that a lap of a larger track passed a thousand to 1,500 of them. Driving close to that traffic, and on the wrong side of the road, filled the boost meter. *Edge*'s review in December 2001 gave the game eight out of ten and called it "a valiant, much-needed adrenaline-filled syringe passionately thrust into the exhausted heart of the videogame racing community". Ward, looking back in 2019, remembered it differently: "Burnout was spectacularly misunderstood all through its development, promotion, and release… I remember being truly heartbroken taking the game to the annual E3 show and no one being interested in seeing it whatsoever."

*Burnout 2: Point of Impact* (2002), again published by Acclaim, added Crash mode: 15 junctions taken from the game's courses, in which the aim was to cause the biggest and most expensive pile-up possible. *Edge* gave it eight out of ten and called the game "a true digital toy box".

## Electronic Arts

By the time *Burnout 3: Takedown* came out in September 2004, Electronic Arts was publishing it. The new game rewarded attacking other racers: shunting a rival into a wall was a "Takedown", worth a chunk of boost. EA had announced in July 2004 that it would buy the Criterion group from Canon Europe, with its studio, *Burnout*, *Black* and RenderWare, and completed the purchase on 19 October; its quarterly accounts put the purchase price at $67.7 million. *Edge*'s news pages argued that the deal "boils down to RenderWare", which rival publishers' biggest games depended on, and Lau-Kee insisted that "we always have, and we always will, provide precisely the same technology internally as we provide externally". The [RenderWare](/vault/techniques/renderware) entry tells how that turned out.

*Edge* found *Burnout Revenge* (2005) throwing "the complete RenderWare manual" at the screen, and called Criterion's first-person shooter *Black* (2006) "a last hurrah for last-gen RenderWare". At the end of 2006 *Edge*'s studio profile of Electronic Arts UK, which covered EA's Chertsey and Guildford teams, listed *Black* and the last two *Burnout* games among its work, with Fiona Sperry, "former general manager of Criterion Games", as general manager and Alex Ward as creative director.

## Paradise and Need for Speed

*Burnout Paradise* (2008), the last game in the series, turned the series into an open world, a city to drive around between events instead of a menu of tracks. Ward said he took the idea from open-world games such as *Mercenaries* and *Crackdown*; his "mantra for the game became 'not playing the game is the game'". Criterion kept adding to it for a year after launch, and Matt Webster later said "the first year of Paradise's launch" taught the studio "a huge amount about how players interact with an open world" — "a precursor to what we call 'live service' in games today".

Then EA gave Criterion its long-running *Need for Speed* series. Criterion's first, *Need for Speed: Hot Pursuit* (2010), was built around a chase: "this guy runs, this guy chases," creative director Craig Sullivan explained to *Edge*, putting a Lamborghini and a police car on the table. It was the first Criterion game not directed by Ward. *Need for Speed: Most Wanted* followed in October 2012.

## After Burnout

On 3 January 2014 Ward and Sperry left Criterion, and later that year announced their own studio, Three Fields Entertainment, which went on to make the crash-mode game *Danger Zone* and the *Burnout*-style racer *Dangerous Driving* (2019). Criterion, much smaller by then, worked for several years on other EA games, including *Star Wars Battlefront* and *Battlefield*. "We never say never," Webster said in 2019 when *Wireframe* asked whether the studio might go back to *Need for Speed*.

It did. In 2020 EA made Criterion the lead studio on *Need for Speed* again, and in 2022 merged Codemasters' Cheshire studio into it, the team that had been Sony's Evolution Studios. Criterion's *Need for Speed Unbound* came out in December 2022. After that the studio moved to *Battlefield 6*, which EA released on 10 October 2025, and in August 2025 its name changed to "Criterion – A Battlefield Studio". The [Guildford games cluster](/vault/culture/guildford-games-cluster) entry follows the studios that Criterion's former staff founded, among them Hello Games and Fireproof.
