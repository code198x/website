In the spring of 1998 the German magazine *PC Player* put Creative Labs' new Voodoo 2 Blaster through its tests, and one of them was a program called *Final Reality*. The card scored 3.68 "Realitymarks", a little ahead of Diamond's Monster 2 at 3.65. *Final Reality* came from [Remedy Entertainment](/vault/companies/remedy-entertainment), the studio a group of [Future Crew](/vault/groups/future-crew) members had founded in 1995, and a new company had just been set up to carry the work on. As Futuremark, and for three years as MadOnion.com, it made *3DMark*, whose scores in "3DMarks" became the numbers magazines and manufacturers used to rank PC graphics cards.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1997, in Espoo, Finland, out of Remedy Entertainment.
- **Names:** Futuremark; MadOnion.com from late 1999 to December 2002; Futuremark again until 2018, when it took its owner's name, UL.
- **Best known for:** *3DMark* (from *3DMark99* in 1998) and *PCMark* (from *PCMark2002*).
- **Also made:** *Shattered Horizon* (2009), a zero-gravity shooter from its games studio, which Rovio bought in 2012.
- **Owner:** UL, from 2014.

## From Final Reality to 3DMark

Remedy premiered *Final Reality* at the Assembly demo party in Finland in 1997, according to Future Crew's own history. *Edge* wrote in 2011 that Futuremark was "born out of Remedy in 1997 to develop the studio's Final Reality 3D benchmarking tool", and listed it, with Remedy and Bugbear Entertainment, among the companies "all founded by members of a famous demo group, Future Crew". Futuremark's own history says its founders started work on PC benchmarks in November 1997, and "On New Year's Eve we receive our official company papers."

*Final Reality* was on magazine cover discs by the summer of 1998; *PC Player*, in its tests, called it the benchmark of VNU Labs. The company's first product under its own name was *3DMark99*, released in October 1998, which its makers call "the world's first benchmark for gamers". Like a demo, it ran animated 3D scenes; unlike a demo, it turned the results into one score. Magazines used its separate tests too. *Maximum PC* recommended its AGP test in February 1999 to a reader who wanted to know whether his graphics card was running in AGP 2x mode: it "takes 16MB worth of 256x256 pixel textures and makes your AGP card suck it down", and single-digit frame rates meant trouble.

The early versions shared an engine with a game. *3DMark2001 Second Edition*, *Computer Gaming World* noted in 2002, "uses Remedy Entertainment's MAX-FX technology", the engine of *Max Payne*, and Futuremark's history records it as the last 3DMark to do so.

## MadOnion.com

In January 2000 *PC Player* reported that "the benchmark specialists at Futuremark", known for *3D Mark 99 (MAX)*, were now called MadOnion.com, "which means something like 'crazy onion'". The company announced *3DMark 2000* and *Video 2000*, and would also distribute *SYSmark 2000*, made with the industry group BAPCo. What all the programs had in common, the magazine explained, was that they put their results on the internet, where users could compare their scores with other people's machines.

The scores travelled well. A *PC Zone* reviewer that January reported a graphics card's "rating of 4489 3DMarks", and through 2001 and 2002 *Computer Gaming World*'s hardware pages tested PCs with "MadOnion's 3DMark2001". *PCMark2002*, a benchmark for the whole PC, followed in March 2002.

On 11 December 2002 the company changed its name back to Futuremark Corporation, to match the names of its products. Its announcement claimed "over 100 large corporate customers and 30 million end-users".

## 3DMark03 and Nvidia

*3DMark03* came out in February 2003. Soon afterwards, members of Futuremark's paying beta programme, who had a developer version with a free-moving camera, noticed that parts of its scenes were drawn strangely on Nvidia hardware, and the websites ExtremeTech and Beyond3D reported similar findings. On 23 May 2003 Futuremark published an audit report. It said it had found eight mechanisms in certain Nvidia drivers that recognised *3DMark03* and replaced its rendering with less work. In a new build of the benchmark that the drivers could not recognise, a GeForce FX 5900 Ultra fell from 5,806 3DMarks to 4,679, a drop of 24.1 per cent; an ATI Radeon 9800 Pro dropped 1.9 per cent. Futuremark called the mechanisms "driver cheats", and argued that a synthetic benchmark was harder to cheat on without being caught than a game's built-in test.

That September *Computer Gaming World*'s hardware columnist wrote that Nvidia had "been in some seriously hot water lately" over its "3DMark2003 driver issues", with accusations flying about whether it was "consciously cheating". By then the two companies had settled their public argument. In a joint statement in June 2003, Futuremark said that it now had a deeper understanding of Nvidia's approach and that "NVIDIA's driver design is an application specific optimization and not a cheat".

## Later benchmarks and a games studio

The benchmarks kept coming, each built to strain the newest cards. *Maximum PC* in November 2004 previewed *3DMark05*: three scenes, among them an airship flying through a flooded valley whose flight path the processor recalculated on every run, "as if it would intelligently steer", although it flew the same route each time. A free version was due, and a professional one for $19.95.

In January 2008 Futuremark set up a games studio. "The benchmark team had been asking us to create games since the first 3DMark," the chief executive, Jukka Mäkinen, told *Edge* in 2011. The studio self-published *Shattered Horizon* in 2009, an online shooter fought in zero gravity among the debris of a huge explosion on the Moon. In 2011 the whole company had 30 staff. In March 2012 Rovio, the maker of *Angry Birds*, bought the games studio, and Mäkinen said Futuremark would concentrate on *3DMark*. The studio's former director, Jaakko Haapasalo, went on to run one of Rovio's own studios.

## UL

The American safety-testing company UL bought Futuremark in October 2014, taking on its 39 employees. By then, UL said, Futuremark received a new benchmark result "every four seconds on average", and made benchmarks for desktop PCs, notebooks, tablets and phones. In April 2018 Futuremark took its parent's name, and the benchmarks, *3DMark* among them, have carried the UL name since. They are still developed in Finland, just outside Helsinki.
