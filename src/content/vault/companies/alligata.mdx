Alligata Software Ltd, **1 Orange Street, Sheffield S1 4DW** — the address is printed on
every advertisement, and it is a fair summary of the company. A small northern publisher
in the first wave of British [Commodore 64](/vault/systems/commodore-64) software, selling
arcade-style games and sports titles from an ordinary office in an ordinary city.

The Vault includes it deliberately, because the eighties are usually told through the
exceptions. Alligata is the layer underneath — and the evidence for what that layer was
like is less flattering, and more interesting, than the shorthand suggests.

- **Press evidence in this library:** *[CRASH](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine)*, *[ZZAP!64](/vault/magazines/zzap-64)*, *Amtix*, *Amstrad Action*,
  *Computer Gamer*, *Commodore Computing International* and *8000 Plus*, **1984 to 1988**,

- **Also published:** *Guardian*, *Cyrus II Chess*, *Here Comes The Sun*.

## The programmer was the company

Alligata's catalogue is unremarkable. Its hiring was not.

**Tony Crowther** wrote *Blagger*, *Son of Blagger*, *Loco* and *Trap* for Alligata, and in
May 1985 *Commodore Computing International* named him in a short list of British
programmers the public could name:

> we don't have the cult of the programmer, though some names (Jeff Minter, Scott Adams,
> **Tony Crowther**, Matthew Smith) are quite well-known.

His name is also why the catalogue is hard to pin down. ***Ghettoblaster*** is routinely
listed as an Alligata game and was published by **Virgin** (*ZZAP!64*, July 1985, £8.95) —
an understandable slip, since Crowther wrote it and wrote four others here. A publisher
whose identity rests on one programmer inherits everything that programmer touched.

***Hollywood*** is attributed to Alligata too, and no game of that name can be traced to
the company from this library at all; the likeliest source of the confusion is Infocom's
*Hollywood Hijinx*.

Four names. Two of them — Minter and Smith — carry whole chapters of the period on their
own. The third was Alligata's.

He left, and *Amtix* reported his return in May 1986 under the headline "**TONY RETURNS TO
ALLIGATA**": Crowther "has signed an **exclusive contract** with Alligata Software Limited"
as a full-time games writer, with a company director quoted as confident he would start
writing straight away.

A publisher of Alligata's size signing an exclusive full-time contract with a named
programmer, in 1986, is the whole strategy stated out loud. It did not have a licence
business, a conversion factory or a marketing department. It had a person.

## What the scores say

The *CRASH* index of January 1988 lists Alligata's Spectrum releases with their ratings,
and the spread is worth reading honestly: **44% to 74%**, clustered in the fifties and
sixties.

That is lower than the entry for "the respectable middle of the market" would suggest, and
it is not the whole picture either — because the same games did better on the machine they
were written for. *Loco* took **44%** on the Spectrum, but *Commodore Computing
International* reviewed Crowther's C64 original in September 1984 and gave its graphics
five stars out of five. *Trap*, per *Computer Gamer*, "was a success for Crowther on the
C64" before the Amstrad and Spectrum versions arrived; *CRASH* gave the Spectrum one
**59%**.

The pattern is the same one visible in [Elite](/vault/companies/elite-systems)'s
*Commando* — **a review score in this period is a verdict on a specific conversion, not on
a game.** For a company whose asset was a C64 programmer, that arithmetic was expensive.

## Jack Charlton, and what an endorsement bought

*Jack Charlton's Match Fishing* is Alligata's best-scoring Spectrum title at **74%**, and
*CRASH*'s own retrospective in January 1988 explains why the number should not be taken at
face value. Listing the era's celebrity endorsements, the magazine notes the rating was
given "from a fisherperson's viewpoint" — and then does not hold back:

> The great Charlton is apparently a mad-keen angler, but **the game has nothing to do with
> him other than the endorsement and not a lot of fishing either.**

That is the cheap end of the celebrity licence, described by the magazine that scored it.
A name on a box, £6.95, no personal involvement — the same manoeuvre
[Martech](/vault/companies/martech) built a company on, done without the appearances.

## Twice too close

Alligata's two appearances in the trade gossip columns are both about Elite's lawyers.

**The game that never shipped.** *Commodore User*, June 1986, in passing: "**Elite stopped
Alligata putting out the original *Who Dares Wins*.**" That is why the game British
readers know is *Who Dares Wins II* — reviewed by *Amstrad Action* in March 1986 at £8.95,
described as "*Rambo* crossed with" something, and by *Amiga Computing* later as "an *Ikari
Warriors* style game". *CRASH* gave it **73%**, and by July 1986 it was **number four in
*Amstrad Action*'s games chart**. Alligata's biggest hit was the sequel to a game a
competitor had blocked.

**The one that nearly did not.** The same column, same month: Alligata released **Bombo**,
and "**Elite are currently seeking legal advice**". *Commodore Computing International*
picked up the resolution in September: "do we hear that Elite is now bosom pals with
Alligata after they threatened legal action over Alligata's alleged '**Bombo**' rip-off of
Elite's '**Bombjack**'."

Neither episode reached a court in this library's record, and both were settled or dropped.
What they establish is a working method. Copying an arcade game's *structure* was normal in
1983 and legally untested; by 1986 the arcade games had licensed home publishers with
budgets, and the same habit had become someone else's cause of action. Alligata is on the
wrong side of that line twice inside six months.

## Selling arcade games to a word processor

One genuinely odd corner of the catalogue, and a good illustration of how small publishers
found revenue.

In 1987 Alligata converted *Guardian* and *Blagger* for the **Amstrad PCW** — a
green-screen machine sold to people who wanted to write letters, with no colour, no sprites
and no joystick port as standard. *8000 Plus* announced it in March 1987 and reviewed the
two-game disc at **£14.95** in May: "*Blagger* is a platform game in which you take on the
role of Rodger the Dodger… *Guardian* is a fast action shoot 'em up."

Fourteen ninety-five for two three-year-old C64 games, sold to an audience with nothing
else to play. A back catalogue is an asset if you can find a machine that has not seen it.

## Why it mattered

Alligata sat in **the middle of the market**: publishers who were neither the
biggest names nor budget labels, whose games scored in the fifties and sixties, and who are
remembered now mainly by the people who owned them.

Any honest picture of the era needs that layer, because a history assembled from the
classics describes an industry that never existed — one where every game was excellent and
every company famous. But the layer should be described accurately. Alligata did not fail
because it was unlucky. It ran on **one programmer's output and other people's ideas**, in
a market that was, year by year, professionalising around it: buying licences, hiring
lawyers, and turning "that's a bit like *Bomb Jack*" from a compliment into a letter.
