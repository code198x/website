## Overview

Alligata Software Ltd, 1 Orange Street, Sheffield — the address is printed on every advertisement, and it is a fair summary of the company. A small northern publisher, in the first wave of British [Commodore 64](/vault/systems/commodore-64) software, selling arcade-style games and sports titles from an ordinary office in an ordinary city.

## Fast facts

- **Based:** 1 Orange Street, Sheffield.
- **Press evidence in this library:** reviewed in *CRASH*, *ZZAP!64* and *Amstrad Action* from **1985 to 1987**, at **£6.95–£9.95**.
- **Published:** *Blagger*, *Guardian*, *Who Dares Wins II*, *Jack Charlton's Match Fishing* (74%), *Knockout* (52%), *Ghettoblaster*, *Cyrus II Chess*, *Hollywood*, *Loco*.

## An ordinary catalogue, which is the point

Nothing in that list is a landmark, and the Vault includes it deliberately.

The eighties British software industry is usually told through its exceptions — [Ultimate](/vault/companies/ultimate), [Ocean](/vault/companies/ocean-software), the games that got 96%. Underneath sat dozens of companies like Alligata: **a few staff, a local address, half a dozen releases a year, most of them competent and none of them famous.** That layer is what made the industry an industry. It filled the shelves, it employed the programmers who later wrote the famous games, and it is nearly invisible in retrospect because nothing it made is being re-released.

The scoring bears it out. *Jack Charlton's Match Fishing* took 74% in *CRASH*, *Knockout* 52%. Those are the numbers most games got, from most publishers, most of the time.

## What the catalogue shows

Three habits of the period are legible here.

**Sport as a licence at the cheap end.** *Jack Charlton's Match Fishing* attaches a footballer-turned-angler's name to a fishing game, at £6.95 — the same manoeuvre [Martech](/vault/companies/martech) built a company on, done casually and without the personal appearances.

**Arcade-alikes without the licence.** *Blagger* is a *Miner 2049er*-shaped platform game; *Guardian* is a *Defender*-shaped shooter. Copying an arcade game's structure was normal, legal in practice, and how most programmers learnt to make games at all.

**Utilities alongside games.** *Cyrus II Chess* is a chess engine, not an arcade game, and it sold through the same channel to the same customer. The boundary between "software" and "games" had not yet closed — a pattern [Quicksilva](/vault/companies/quicksilva)'s early lists show too.

## Why it mattered

Alligata is the entry for **the middle of the market**: the publishers who were neither the biggest names nor budget labels, whose games got seventies percentages, and who are now remembered mainly by the people who owned them.

Any honest picture of the era needs this layer in it. A history assembled only from the classics describes an industry that never existed — one where every game was excellent and every company famous.
