System 3 built its reputation on technical excellence, and the reputation is
deserved — [*The Last Ninja*](/vault/games/the-last-ninja)'s isometric martial
arts, *International Karate+* with three fighters on screen at once, *Myth*'s
rotoscoped animation. It proved the C64 could do things competitors thought
impossible.

Two things get left out of that account. The company's first hit was on the
**Spectrum**, not the C64. And its most-copied game spent 1987 at the centre of an
American copyright case that it was not a party to and could not defend.

## Fast facts
- **Founded:** 1982 by Mark Cale.
- **Location:** London, England.
- **Signature series:** [The Last Ninja](/vault/games/the-last-ninja) (1987, 1988, 1991), *International Karate* (1985-86) and *[International Karate+](/vault/games/international-karate-plus)*.
- **First hit:** *Death Star Interceptor* (1985), on the Spectrum, at £7.95.
- ***CRASH*:** *International Karate* took **68%** in issue 24.
- **Technical focus:** pushed hardware beyond expected limits.
- **Longevity:** still operating, now primarily as a retro publisher.

## Death Star Interceptor

System 3's breakthrough was ***Death Star Interceptor*** (1985, Spectrum, £7.95) —
which is a *Star Wars* game without a *Star Wars* licence.

*Computer Gamer* reviewed it in May 1985 and its first impression was "rather
bad, but it sort of grows on you"; the second screen is "fairly impressive", and
the **Star Wars theme** plays over it, which the magazine notes went down with
"great approval". By August 1985 it was in the Gallup Spectrum top thirty, and in
1986 it turned up on a charity compilation for the Prince's Trust alongside
Ultimate and Melbourne House titles.

Worth setting beside [Domark](/vault/companies/domark), which later paid for the
actual *Star Wars* licence. In 1985 you could still make the game and play the
tune without asking, and a small London publisher did.

## International Karate, and a court in California

***International Karate*** (1985-86) is the game System 3 is most imitated for,
and its American history is a mess that had nothing to do with System 3's conduct.

In the United States the game reached shelves as **Epyx's *World Karate
Championship***, with Activision separately due to publish it under its own name.
*ANALOG Computing* was unimpressed in December 1986, and noted the delay: "it was
promised for August 1985, and a lot of people feel let down."

Then **Data East sued Epyx** — not over System 3's game as such, but over
*World Karate Championship*'s resemblance to Data East's own arcade game
*Karate Champ*. On **10 June 1987**, US District Court Judge **William Ingram**
ruled that Epyx must stop reproducing *World Karate Championship*, refrain from
derivative works, cease marketing it in all formats, and

> **recall all copies of both *World Karate Championship* and *International
> Karate* from all distributors, jobbers, representatives, and retailers.**

The judge held that the total concept and feel of *Karate Champ* and *World Karate
Championship* were "substantially similar", and that the games were
"**qualitatively identical in every respect**".

So a British game was recalled from the American market by a judgment in a dispute
between a Japanese-owned publisher and an American one. System 3 appears in the
order only as the name on the other box.

⚠ This library holds the report of the June 1987 ruling but nothing on what
happened to it afterwards, and copyright judgments of this kind were not always
final. Treat the injunction as the documented event, not as the end of the story.

## The Last Ninja trilogy
Their defining work:
- **[The Last Ninja](/vault/games/the-last-ninja) (1987):** isometric action-adventure with [Ben Daglish's](/vault/people/ben-daglish) acclaimed soundtrack.
- **Last Ninja 2 (1988):** modern New York setting, refined gameplay.
- **Last Ninja 3 (1991):** time-travelling conclusion to the trilogy.

Each game sold well and reviewed brilliantly, establishing System 3 as a prestige developer.

## International Karate
The fighting game series showcased technical prowess:
- **International Karate (1986):** smooth animation, authentic martial arts feel.
- **International Karate+ (1987):** three fighters simultaneously—unprecedented on C64.
- **IK+ extras:** the infamous bonus rounds (bouncing balls) became legendary.

The series competed with *Way of the Exploding Fist* and often won on technical merit.

## Technical excellence
System 3 games were known for:
- **Smooth animation:** fluid sprites when competitors showed jerky movement.
- **Large sprites:** character graphics that felt substantial.
- **Isometric engines:** making 3D-feeling worlds on 2D hardware.
- **Audio quality:** partnering with top composers like [Ben Daglish](/vault/people/ben-daglish).

## Myth and beyond
*Myth: History in the Making* (1989) demonstrated rotoscoped animation on C64—smooth, realistic movement that seemed impossible on 8-bit hardware. The 16-bit versions expanded the visual ambition further.

## Modern System 3
The company survived the transitions:
- Developed and published through the 16-bit and 32-bit eras
- Now focuses on retro re-releases and compilations
- *The Last Ninja* remains their calling card

## Legacy
System 3 proved that small teams with technical ambition could produce AAA-quality games. Their focus on polish over quantity—delivering fewer games at higher quality—influenced how developers thought about production values.
