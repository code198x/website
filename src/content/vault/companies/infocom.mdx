Founded by MIT researchers, Infocom created the gold standard for interactive fiction. Their Z-machine virtual machine ran on virtually every home computer, their parser understood natural language, and their games—from *Zork* to *A Mind Forever Voyaging*—explored genres and themes no one else attempted.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1979 by MIT staff and students.
- **Based:** Cambridge, Massachusetts.
- **Speciality:** text adventures with sophisticated parser.
- **Acquisition:** [Activision](/vault/companies/activision) bought Infocom in 1986.

## Decline and fall

| Year | Event |
|------|-------|
| **1985** | ***Cornerstone*** ships at **\$495** and fails |
| 1986 | Activision acquisition |
| 1988 | Layoffs begin |
| 1989–90 | Final games released; studio closed ⚠ *(see below)* |

*Cornerstone* shipped in **early 1985**, a full year before Activision bought the company —
so the database drained an independent Infocom, not a subsidiary. *80 Micro* was reviewing it
that February and March:

> The latest product from Infocom isn't a game — it's a **data base manager for computer
> novices**… Infocom, maker of popular adventure games such as *Zork* and *Deadline*, is
> entering the business software market with **Cornerstone (\$495)**, a relational data base
> system for non-programmers.

Cornerstone was Infocom's **first business package** and it shipped a full year before
Activision bought the company. The database did not drain a subsidiary's resources after the
takeover; it drained an independent company's, and helped put it in a position where being
bought was the available option.

*80 Micro* also captures why the industry wanted it to work, in a passage that is the best
short account of Infocom's standing in 1985:

> It's no coincidence that the most successful software manufacturers generally show a touch
> of class. Take Infocom. By their own admission, few announcements could be more boring than
> that of a new data base manager. Yet **Cornerstone, their first business package, received
> much favorable attention. The primary reason was not so much the product itself, but
> because people feel good about Infocom.** This is an outfit run by a helpful, intelligent,
> knowledgeable, genuinely likable staff.

Goodwill sold the first copies. It could not sell the second ones, and at \$495 against a
games catalogue priced in tens of dollars, the miss was expensive.

## Activision was the British distributor first

Before it was the owner, Activision was how Infocom reached Britain. *8000 Plus*, November
1986:

> Infocom are the top publisher of adventure games, or "**interactive fiction**" as the
> company prefer to classify its titles. **22 adventures are available from Infocom now,
> through its UK distributors Activision** — including such classics as *Zork I, II and III*,
> *Suspended* and *Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy*.

The arrangement persisted after the takeover and is visible in the small print of the British
press: *Hollywood Hijinx* "is being distributed by Activision" in April 1987, and by August
1987 "the current Infocom main distributors over here are **Activision**", who would also
"supply **clue books** for all their games at **£6.99**".

That is the same [Activision](/vault/companies/activision) UK operation this Vault records
handling System 3, Sierra On-Line and Lucasfilm — a British distribution arm carrying most of
the American catalogue that mattered, including, eventually, one it owned.

## The deathwatch, in print

*ACE* covered the last years as they happened, and the tone is of a magazine watching a
patient. May 1989:

> **Infocom spotting is a worthwhile pastime** because it gives us some idea of the state of
> the fantasy market in the US. Right now, **the signs are not encouraging**. The company
> released several new games (*Nord 'n' Bert*, *Plundered Hearts* etc.) in 1987 and **have
> published almost nothing since. Now owned by Mediagenic, they are not having an easy
> time** — while companies like Origin (*Ultima*) and EA (*Bard's Tale*) continue to reap
> large rewards.

The magazine pinned the company's survival on two releases: "As the release of Infocom's
*Shogun* draws nearer, we're all holding our breath", and "**if *Journey* makes the grade,
then Infocom can heave a sigh of relief. If it doesn't…**"

⚠ **The closing date needs care.** 1989 is widely given. *ACE*, reporting on Activision's
own collapse in **October 1990**, says Mediagenic "spent millions of dollars buying
prestigious adventure developer Infocom, **only to close it down earlier this year**" —
which places the shutdown in 1990. Both dates circulate, and they may be measuring different
things: the end of Cambridge operations and the end of the label. This library supports 1990
for the closure being reported.

The context is worth holding onto. Infocom was not shut by a hostile conglomerate in good
health. [Activision](/vault/companies/activision) had renamed itself **Mediagenic**, lost
nearly \$20 million in a year, and was facing a \$6 million judgment. The company that closed
Infocom was months from being sold itself.

## Legacy

Infocom's influence persists:
- Z-machine specification published, still used
- Games remain playable via interpreters
- Established interactive fiction as literature
- Inform language continues the Z-machine tradition
