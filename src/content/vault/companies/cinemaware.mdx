## Overview

They wanted to make movies you could play. Cinemaware combined lavish visuals, orchestral music, and multiple gameplay styles into "interactive movies." Defender of the Crown's medieval epic and It Came from the Desert's B-movie homage showcased what the Amiga could achieve—games that looked and felt like cinema.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1985.
- **Founders:** **Bob and Phyllis Jacob** — *Ahoy!* credits both as "the executive producers of CinemaWare games".
- **Platform focus:** Amiga (primarily).
- **Closure:** 1991.

## What Cinemaware said it was for

The overview above describes a company that wanted to make playable movies. That is not how
its president described it at the time. *ACE*, June 1989, interviewing **Bob Jacob**:

> **Cinemaware was set up in the first place as a rehearsal for new interactive
> technologies.** … "We're using **computers as a training ground**, developing the
> methodology for interactive [storytelling] — so we'll be ready and waiting whichever
> technology wins out."

The context is the piece's subject: **CD-I** (the Philips/Sony standard) and **DVI** (Intel's
Digital Video Interactive chipsets, sold as add-on boards for an IBM AT). Cinemaware's Amiga
games were, on this account, deliberately a **dress rehearsal** for interactive video formats
that had not yet shipped.

That reframes almost everything else about the company. The lavish production values were not an
end in themselves and not just a demonstration of what the Amiga could do — they were
practice for a medium the company expected to arrive. Jacob is explicit that the point was
not to imitate film: "we want to **create hits — let's create something without creating a
movie**."

The working methods follow from it. The company was hiring **scriptwriters** — "people whose
trade is writing stories" — and "teaching them what it means to write an interactive
script". And it had built **rotoscoping** tooling that "allows us to **film real motion
video, capture it in a machine and let the artist manipulate the images** easily". There was
a **Cinemaware Interactive Group**, headed by **David Riordan**, separate from the games.

⚠ Read the 1991 closure in this light. A company positioned as a rehearsal for CD-I and DVI
was making a bet on which interactive-video format would win. The answer turned out to be
none of the ones it was rehearsing for.

## Who was buying it

Jacob gave *ACE* a figure in December 1988 that explains the design of these games better
than any account of their graphics:

> **The average person who buys one of our products in the US is probably about 32 years
> old.**

The surrounding article makes the general point: American computer users were "a mature
bunch by UK standards, typically in their late 20s or 30s", inclined to "brainy games with a
lot of long-term appeal", and they did not "mind spending a few hours wading through a
manual". Cinemaware was not making games for teenagers, and its production values were aimed
at adults who had bought a computer partly to take work home on.

⚠ Cinemaware is filed as an Amiga house, which undersells how hard it pushed elsewhere, at least in Britain.
A full-page *ACE* advertisement in April 1989 is headed "**Cinemaware present a special
Double Bill for ST Users**" — *Rocket Ranger* and *King of Chicago*, "**BOTH OUT NOW for the
Atari ST**" at **£24.99**, with *Defender of the Crown* and *Sinbad* noted as already
available on ST.

The PC push was strategic rather than an afterthought. *ACE*, December 1988, on the arrival
of **EGA**: Cinemaware, "a software house you'd normally associate with the Amiga, are about
to release their glossy Norman-basher **Defender of the Crown for EGA PCs**" — one of the
first heavyweight 16-bit titles to chase the PC's "huge pool of potential buyers" once the
hardware could show them properly.

## The criticism at the time

Cinemaware is remembered admiringly, and the period reviews were not. *Ahoy!*, May 1988, on
*King of Chicago* — after praising Eric Rosser's score as fit for "any Cagney or Edward G.
Robinson film" and the illustrations as "terrific… full of detail and personality":

> Visually, King of Chicago suffers from what in television and comic books is called
> "**talking heads**". Often, the only thing on the screen is a head-and-shoulders drawing of
> one or more characters… **the overall effect is unexciting.**

That is the standing risk of the whole Cinemaware approach, identified while the company was
at its peak: presentation carried the games, and when the presentation was static the game
had little else to offer.
