Electronic Arts began as a rebellion against how publishers treated developers. Founder Trip Hawkins, an Apple veteran, believed game creators deserved credit, royalties, and respect—like musicians or authors. EA's early packaging featured developer photos and credits. The idealism faded as EA grew into gaming's largest publisher, but they helped establish that developers mattered.

## Fast facts
- **Founded:** May 1982 by [Trip Hawkins](/vault/people/trip-hawkins) in San Mateo, California.
- **Philosophy:** developers as artists, deserving credit and royalties.
- **Early packaging:** album-style, with developer photos and bios.
- **Major early titles:** *Pinball Construction Set*, *Archon*, *M.U.L.E.*
- **[Rob Hubbard](/vault/people/rob-hubbard):** joined EA in late 80s, composed for numerous titles.
- **Modern form:** sports games (*[FIFA](/vault/games/fifa)*, *Madden*), acquisitions, massive scale.

## The early vision
Hawkins' mission statement differed from competitors:
- **Developer credit:** names on boxes, in advertising, in the public eye.
- **Royalty structure:** profit sharing with creators.
- **Quality focus:** fewer games, higher standards.
- **Album packaging:** games presented like music, creators like artists.

The early EA was genuinely different.

## The Amiga years
EA embraced the [Commodore Amiga](/vault/systems/commodore-amiga):
- **Deluxe Paint:** the defining Amiga creative tool, published by EA.
- **Quality ports:** serious investment in Amiga versions.
- **Music investment:** hired composers like [Rob Hubbard](/vault/people/rob-hubbard).
- **Technical ambition:** pushed hardware capabilities.

## "Unless you are looking at a program from Electronic Arts"

The claim that EA credited its developers is simple to assert and hard to show. *Amiga
World*, September 1985, shows it — not by describing EA's policy but by using it as
the exception that proves the rule:

> How many times have you wondered **who programmed** your favorite software package? Perhaps
> you've wondered how long it took to develop your favorite program or how it evolved. In
> most cases, **those questions will remain unanswered — unless you are looking at a program
> from Electronic Arts.**

The same piece states the philosophy in the company's own terms: EA was "dedicated not only
to being a highly successful business, but also **a place where programmers are thought of as
artists**". The word *artists* is used throughout as the ordinary internal term for
developers, not as marketing copy.

## The producer

The mechanism behind the album packaging was organisational, and *Amiga Computing* explained
it in July 1988:

> EA assigns each of its projects a **producer** whose role, **just like his namesake in the
> film industry**, is to act as **a buffer between the commercial interests of the company
> and the freedom of the programming team**.

That is a structural answer to a structural problem, and it is why the artist-friendly
reputation lasted as long as it did. The same article gives the founder's full name —
**William M Hawkins**, "better known as Trip" — and his premise: "Trip realised that it was
**software, not hardware, that made people want to buy computers**", at a time when he was
working for Apple. Among the founding team it names **Tim Mott**, previously of Xerox.

⚠ One date to keep straight. *Amiga Computing* says EA "was the 136th firm to enter the home
computing field when its first products shipped in **May 1983**". The founding is usually
dated to May **1982**. Both can stand — founded in 1982, shipping in 1983 — but
the 1983 date is the one the period press attaches to EA's arrival.

## Developing for a machine nobody had

The most interesting thing EA did on the Amiga was solve a logistics problem. In 1985 the
hardware barely existed: prototypes were "black boxes that do not resemble the off-white
units found in retail stores", and they were scarce. EA's answer was the **artist's
workstation** — "a combination of hardware, software and peripherals that allows the
programmer to develop Amiga software on an IBM PC or compatible", with a real Amiga handed
over only for the final phases.

*Amiga World* could not resist the irony:

> The screen displays of a **\$9,000 IBM PC AT** (used in the artist's workstation) have
> **less graphical resolution than the weakest mode of the Amiga.**

The second half of the answer is more startling. EA's developers were not in the building:

> Trip Hawkins pushed his company to install a **large-scale electronic messaging network**
> that artists now access **from their homes and offices using modems** and the workstations.
> "**We communicate electronically with our artists every day**," says Hawkins.

Distributed development over dial-up, with a shared database of "working code for programs
that are already running on the Amiga", in 1985. The developers-as-artists philosophy had a
practical corollary that is rarely noticed: if your authors are independent creators rather
than employees, you have to build the infrastructure to work with them at a distance.

## The author who goes unnamed

*Deluxe Paint* is remembered as the defining Amiga creative tool, published by EA, and
credited to nobody — for a company whose distinguishing practice was putting authors' names
on boxes. The period press had no such difficulty. He is **Dan Silva**, named
routinely and without explanation as "the author of DP3" in *ACE*, as "Deluxe Paint's
programmer" in *Ahoy!*, and as the man who "demonstrated Deluxe Paint II at SIGGRAPH" in
*Amazing Computing*.

*Ahoy!* also records why the program mattered so quickly: it was among the first packages
available after the Amiga 1000's introduction and, unusually for software issued that early
in a machine's life, "**a full-featured and effective graphics package**" — good enough that
"in spite of several competing graphic products **it became a best-seller among Amiga
users**".

## Electronic Arts in Britain

For the first two and a half years, a British buyer did not buy from Electronic Arts at all. They bought from a record company.

**Ariolasoft** launched in February 1985 — at the Hippodrome, in London's West End — and *Your Commodore* filed the story under the headline "Off the record": "two big record companies are entering the software market. They are CBS and **Ariolasoft, which is connected to Arista**." *Personal Computer Games* described the arrival more plainly: "Ariolasoft are another new company marketing US games in the UK. Their first 12 games are all for the Commodore 64 and were **originally written by Broderbund or Electronic Arts** in the States," at £9.95.

So the company that packaged its games like record albums, and called its programmers artists, was introduced to Britain by the software arm of a record label. The analogy EA had reached for as a metaphor turned out to be its actual distribution channel. Through 1985 and 1986 *M.U.L.E.*, *Pinball Construction Set*, *One-on-One* and *Realm of Impossibility* reached British shelves with Ariolasoft's name on them — one *M.U.L.E.* tournament was held in Ariolasoft's own London offices — and EA's name appeared in the British press just **31 times a year**.

That ended in October 1987. *Your Computer* reported it as news:

> **Electronic Arts launches game in UK.** The top U.S. games software publishing house Electronic Arts has started business in the U.K. Its European headquarters have been established in **Langley, Berks** and at present are being headed by two American EA executives, **Mark Lewis**, Director of European Publishing, and **David Gardener**, a producer — "EA likes to draw an analogy between itself and film companies".

The press counts follow the decision exactly: 31 mentions in 1986, **106 in 1987**, **445 in 1988**. The address on the order coupons in *ACE* and in the trade directories of *Amazing Computing* was:

> **Electronic Arts, Langley Business Centre, 11–49 Station Road, Langley, near Slough, Berkshire SL3 8YN.**

The San Mateo company that told American readers its programmers were artists sold to British readers out of a business centre off the Slough road. (By the nineties the listings give a different Langley address, 90 Heron Drive — the Station Road one is the period address, not a permanent one.)

⚠ The film analogy in the *Your Computer* report is worth noticing for its date. It is October 1987, nine months before *Amiga Computing* explained the producer role in the same terms — so the comparison was how EA introduced itself to Britain, not a gloss applied afterwards.

## Growth and change
Success changed EA:
- **Sports dominance:** *Madden*, *FIFA*, *NHL* became annual franchises.
- **Acquisitions:** absorbed studios (Origin, Westwood, BioWare, many others).
- **Scale:** grew into gaming's largest third-party publisher.
- **Reputation shift:** early artist-friendly image gave way to corporate behemoth.

## The C64 library
EA published notable C64 titles:
- **Archon:** chess meets combat.
- **M.U.L.E.:** multiplayer economic strategy.
- **Bard's Tale series:** influential RPGs.
- **Rob Hubbard scores:** EA invested in audio quality.

## Legacy
EA proved game publishing could be big business. Their early developer-friendly approach helped legitimise the medium. That idealism didn't survive corporate growth, but the industry they helped build remains. When developers fight for credit and royalties today, they're continuing arguments EA once championed.
