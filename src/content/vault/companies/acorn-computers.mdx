## Overview

Acorn Computers defined British computing education. Their BBC Micro, developed for the BBC Computer Literacy Project, became the standard machine in UK schools throughout the 1980s. More significantly, Acorn's need for a better processor led to the creation of ARM—originally Acorn RISC Machine—whose descendants now power most smartphones on Earth.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1978 in Cambridge by Hermann Hauser and Chris Curry.
- **Key products:** BBC Micro (1981), Electron (1983), Archimedes (1987).
- **ARM origins:** designed in-house for the Archimedes, later spun off.
- **BBC contract:** won the Computer Literacy Project tender over Sinclair.
- **Demise:** acquired by Olivetti (1985), eventually wound down (1998).

## The BBC Micro era

The BBC contract transformed Acorn:
- **Educational dominance:** nearly every UK school had BBC Micros.
- **Quality hardware:** robust 6502-based design with expansion ports.
- **Software ecosystem:** educational software, games, programming tools.
- **Elite debut:** the landmark space game launched on BBC Micro first.

## The machine its owners kept using for games

Acorn's identity was education, and this entry follows that framing. *Acorn User*'s own pages
show the readership pulling in a different direction throughout.

The magazine ran a **software chart**, and through 1985–86 the top of it is games. *Elite* —
Acornsoft's own — sat at **#1** for months at **£14.95** cassette and **£17.95** on disc.
Around it: *Sabre Wulf* and *Jet Pac* from [Ultimate](/vault/companies/ultimate) at £9.95,
*Repton* from Superior, *Chuckie Egg*, *Beach-Head* from
[US Gold](/vault/companies/us-gold), and — at **#2 in the Electron Top 10** —
[Beau Jolly](/vault/companies/beau-jolly)'s *Computer Hits (10)* compilation.

Two things follow from that list.

**BBC games were expensive.** £14.95, against £9.95 for a full-price Spectrum cassette and
£1.99 for budget. The education machine's software was priced like education software even
when it was a space trading game.

**The Spectrum's biggest publisher came to Acorn's machine.** Ultimate advertised in *Acorn
User* in terms that acknowledge the awkwardness directly: "some poor souls have been missing
out on the Ultimate experience… **so to prove that we are not prejudiced in any way we
present *Alien 8*, *Jet-Pac* and *Lunar Jet-Man* especially for the BBC Micro**."

And the readers wanted more of it. A letter in the same period asks the magazine to **review
more games** — naming *Citadel*, *Dr Who and the Mines of Terror*, *The Way of the Exploding
Fist*, *Nightshade*, *Gyroscope* and *Twister* — and to introduce "**a ratings system of some
sort**", so that "at least then we might get an idea of whether a game is worth spending
money on".

That is a computer-literacy machine's official magazine being asked by its own readers to
start scoring games like *CRASH* did.

## Elite, and what Acorn lost

*Acorn User* was still calling ***Elite*** "**the greatest Acorn game of all time**" at the
end of **1993** — nine years after release, and the highest praise the magazine had.

By then the sequel was going elsewhere. Reviewing what was coming, the magazine noted that
David Braben had "almost completed *Frontier*… **Unfortunately this time it will appear first
on other platforms**, but expect to see it pop up on Acorn machines some time soon."

The regret in that sentence is the Acorn story in miniature. The machine that produced the
defining British game of the decade could no longer command its sequel — and the company's
lasting contribution turned out to be the processor, not the computer.

## ARM: the lasting legacy

Acorn's processor design outlived the company:
- **RISC philosophy:** simple instructions, fast execution.
- **Low power:** critical for portable devices.
- **ARM Ltd:** joint venture with Apple and VLSI (1990).
- **Modern dominance:** ARM powers most mobile devices worldwide.
