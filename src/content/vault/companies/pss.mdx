Personal Software Services, of Coventry, spent the eighties doing something most British publishers did not: **serious wargames**, on machines mostly bought to play arcade games on.

## Fast facts

- **Based:** Coventry. **Founded:** 1981.
- **Named in the press as:** **[Gary Mays](/vault/people/gary-mays)**, co-founder and partner, and
  **[Richard Cockayne](/vault/people/richard-cockayne)**, director.
- **Series:** the **Wargamers** line — *Theatre Europe* was the second,
  *Battle of Britain* the third, both at **£9.95**.
- **Also:** *Tobruk*, *Bismarck*, *Sorcerer Lord*, *The Covenant*,
  *Swords 'n' Sorcery*.
- **Afterwards:** absorbed into **Mirrorsoft**, which reworked *Theatre Europe*
  for 16-bit machines as *Conflict Europe*.

## "Our first and biggest mistake"

Ask most companies about their name and you get a founding myth. *Amstrad Action*
went to Coventry in March 1986 and got the opposite: **Gary Mays** "looks rather
pained", gives a sigh, and explains that the name **"was our first and biggest
mistake"** — pleading in mitigation that they had handled their own early
publicity.

Personal Software Services is a name from the era when it was not yet obvious
that games would be the business. It says software house, in the way a firm
selling accounts packages to small companies might. What PSS sold was the
Warsaw Pact rolling into West Germany.

## The hex grid comes home — and does not stay there

Board wargaming — hexagonal maps, cardboard counters, combat-results tables —
was a substantial hobby with its own publishers and magazines long before home
computers. PSS's first insight was the obvious one: **the computer keeps the map,
tracks supply, and works out the odds**, leaving the player the decisions.

Its second insight is the one worth recording, because it is a design decision
rather than a port.

**PSS put arcade sequences inside its wargames, and let them change the result.**
*Computer Gamer*, previewing *Theatre Europe* in 1985: you plan strategy through
map and radar screens, "however, you will also need a keen fire button as
individual battles are fought out with a joystick on action screens". In
*Battle of Britain* the same year, the action screens put you in a Spitfire
cockpit and behind anti-aircraft guns — and the magazine spells out the
consequence: "**your skill in this stage will affect the outcome of the battles,
as in *Theatre Europe***".

That is a deliberate bridge between two audiences who did not otherwise buy the
same games. The strategist gets a campaign; the fourteen-year-old who bought the
machine for *Jet Set Willy* gets something to shoot at; and the two are wired
together so that neither is decoration. Whether it satisfied either group
completely is a fair question, but as an answer to "how do you sell a hex-grid
wargame to a Spectrum owner" it is a real one.

It worked well enough. By August 1985 *Theatre Europe* was in the Gallup
Commodore 64 top ten and on the Amstrad chart at the same time.

## Theatre Europe

*Theatre Europe* (1985) simulates the first thirty days of a conventional war
between NATO and the Warsaw Pact for control of West Germany. You take either
supreme commander. The Warsaw Pact wins by taking West Germany; NATO wins by
stopping it.

There are three levels: a **non-nuclear** setting for beginners, the accepted
NATO and Warsaw Pact doctrines for more advanced players, and — in *Computer
Gamer*'s description — "a highly unpredictable strategy that should only be
tackled by experts".

**Chemical and nuclear release are available to the player.** Using them ends the
game in the way that ending is usually depicted.

⚠ *Theatre Europe* is widely said to have carried a **CND telephone number** on
its packaging. Period coverage does discuss a CND association — the March 1986
*Amstrad Action* interview turns to it directly — but that passage of the scan is
too damaged to quote or to confirm the specific claim from. What is legible is
Mays's own retreat from the subject: asked about it, he says "**I'm not sure any
more**", and the magazine records that the next releases in the series "will not
pose such weighty" questions.

That is worth more than the packaging detail. A publisher took a position, found
it harder to hold than expected, and stepped back from it within a year.

The context is not abstract. *Theatre Europe* was published at the height of the
Euromissile crisis, when cruise and Pershing deployments were live news, *Threads*
had recently been broadcast, and a substantial part of its audience genuinely
expected this to happen. A game asking a teenager whether to authorise a first
strike was a different proposition in 1985 than it reads as now.

## "There's no need for such devices"

The other thing PSS is on the record for is a stance on piracy that cost it money.

*Acorn User*, October 1984: "**PSS, a Coventry software house, has adopted a policy
of not advertising in any magazine which carries adverts for tape or disc
copiers.**" Director **Richard Cockayne** said he was "fairly appalled" at
companies selling copier programs, and gave both the commercial argument and the
alternative:

> In the longer term they are cutting their own throat. There's no need for such
> devices. **We will replace any tape which doesn't load.**

Set that beside the [Guild of Software Houses](/vault/phenomena/guild-of-software-houses)
charter of the year before, which committed members to replacing defective stock.
PSS's position is the same promise made unilaterally, with a boycott attached —
and a small publisher declining to advertise in the magazines that reviewed it is
not a cheap gesture.

## Why it mattered

PSS is evidence that the British 8-bit market was **broader than its reputation**. Alongside the arcade conversions and platform games there was a real, sustained business in simulation and strategy.

But the more interesting lesson is the compromise. PSS did not simply port a hobby onto a games machine and wait for an audience to find it; it re-engineered the form so that a wargame could be sold to somebody who had never seen a hex grid, and accepted the hybrid that produced. That is a commercial answer to a design problem, and the sort of thing that gets edited out when the period is remembered as either "arcade games" or "serious software".

And *Theatre Europe* is one of the earliest examples of a computer game treated as **comment, not entertainment** — reviewed, and occasionally objected to, on the strength of what it was about, not how it played.
