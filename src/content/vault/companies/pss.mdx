## Overview

Personal Software Services, of Coventry, spent the eighties doing something most British publishers did not: **serious wargames**, on machines mostly bought to play arcade games on.

## Fast facts

- **Based:** Coventry.
- **Seen in the press:** reviewed in *CRASH*, *ZZAP!64* and *Amstrad Action* from **1985 to 1988**, at **£6.95–£14.99**.
- **Known for:** *Theatre Europe*, *Battle of Britain*, *Tobruk*, *Sorcerer Lord*, *The Covenant*, and the *Wargamers* series.

## The hex grid comes home

Board wargaming — hexagonal maps, cardboard counters, combat-results tables — was a substantial hobby with its own publishers and magazines long before home computers. PSS's insight was that the machine could do the tedious part: **the computer keeps the map, tracks supply, and works out the odds**, leaving the player the decisions.

That produced games with a very different rhythm from everything around them on the shelf. A PSS title expected patience, a manual, and an interest in the actual battle it depicted. Reviews in the games press of the period sometimes struggle visibly with this, applying criteria built for arcade conversions to something that had no intention of being one.

## Theatre Europe

The title PSS is most remembered for is *Theatre Europe* (1985), a simulation of a conventional war between NATO and the Warsaw Pact in central Europe — with **nuclear release available to the player**.

Using it ended the game, in the way that ending is usually depicted: a sequence of explosions and a screen telling you what you had done. The game shipped with a **CND telephone number** on the packaging.

Whether that was conscience, provocation or marketing was argued at the time and can be argued now. What is not arguable is the context: *Theatre Europe* was published at the height of the Euromissile crisis, when cruise and Pershing deployments were live news, *Threads* had recently been broadcast, and a substantial part of its audience genuinely expected this to happen. A game asking a teenager whether to authorise a first strike was not an abstract proposition in 1985.

## Why it mattered

PSS is evidence that the British 8-bit market was **broader than its reputation**. Alongside the arcade conversions and platform games there was a real, sustained business in simulation and strategy, aimed at people who wanted to think slowly.

And *Theatre Europe* is one of the earliest examples of a computer game treated as **comment rather than entertainment** — reviewed, and occasionally objected to, on the strength of what it was about rather than how it played.
