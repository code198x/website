Irem's name described its first trade, supplying game machines. In 1985 the American magazine *Electronic Games* spelled it out as "International Rental Electronic Machines of Osaka, Japan". Two years later the same company made *R-Type*, a shoot-'em-up that *CRASH*'s arcade reviewer called "undoubtedly the most impressive coin-op of the lot, and one of the hardest to get to play – because there's almost always a crowd round the cabinet!" Before that it had already produced *Moon Patrol* and *Kung-Fu Master*. In America and Britain, though, those games often reached players under someone else's name.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** As IPM; public sources give 1974. Renamed Irem by the early 1980s.
- **Base:** Osaka, Japan, in the 1980s; its parent company was the monitor maker Nanao.
- **Founder:** Kenzo Tsujimoto, who also founded [Capcom](/vault/companies/capcom).
- **Best known for:** [Moon Patrol](/vault/games/moon-patrol) (1982), [Kung-Fu Master](/vault/games/kung-fu-master) (1984), [R-Type](/vault/games/r-type) (1987).
- **Games business:** Stopped in 1994. Irem Software Engineering, set up by Nanao in 1997, took over the development team.

## IPM, Irem and I.R.M.

The company's early history is patchy in English-language sources, and they do not all agree.

*Wireframe*'s 2019 history says it was first called IPM Corporation, that it entered the video game business with a copy of Taito's *Space Invaders* called *IPM Invader*, and that the name Irem at first stood for International Rental Electronics Machines, later changed to Innovations in Recreational Electronic Media. It names Kenzo Tsujimoto as Irem's founder, as *ACE* did in 1991 when it called him "a veteran of the Japanese video game industry and founder of Irem (producers of R-Type)".

Tsujimoto also founded Capcom, and here the sources diverge. Capcom's own chronology starts in May 1979 with I.R.M. Corporation, a company in Matsubara, Osaka, set up to develop and sell game machines. Wikipedia, citing Japanese press profiles, gives IPM's founding as 1974, has IPM renamed Irem Corporation in early 1979, and treats I.R.M. as a separate company that Tsujimoto set up the same year while still running Irem. In that account the monitor maker Nanao, which built screens for IPM's cabinets, became Irem's majority shareholder in 1980, Tsujimoto was replaced in 1982, and he left in 1983 to form Capcom.

So Irem and I.R.M. share a founder, an Osaka base and nearly the same name, and came into being in the same year. No public source found here explains how the two were related, and Capcom's chronology does not mention Irem at all. The 1974 date and the 1979 renaming rest on Wikipedia and the Japanese articles it cites, not on period magazines.

What is not in doubt is the ownership by the late 1980s. *Wireframe* describes Nanao as Irem's parent company when *R-Type* was made.

## Arcade games in other companies' cabinets

In its early years Irem's games often went out under another name. *Electronic Games* introduced *Red Alert* in March 1982 as a game "from Japan's Irem", in which the player defends six cities from air attack. In America, [Williams](/vault/companies/williams-electronics) sold *Moon Patrol*: 1983 magazine charts list it as "Moon Patrol (Williams)", and the home versions from Atarisoft carried Williams's copyright. *Video Games* explained in May 1984 that Irem was "a company probably best known for its creation of Moon Patrol and Motorace USA", the second also sold by Williams under a licence from Irem.

*Vidiot* noted in 1983 that Williams had "acquired this Japanese game which is quite unlike their others": a "landbound rover" that "shoots in two directions against attacking spaceships and roadblock rocks" and can bounce over potholes. *Wireframe* says it is "widely credited" as the first mainstream game to use [parallax scrolling](/vault/techniques/parallax-scrolling), with layers of scenery moving at different speeds to suggest depth.

Irem also made arcade versions of home computer games. In 1984 it released a coin-op [Lode Runner](/vault/games/lode-runner), licensed from Broderbund. *Video Games* noted the reversal: usually an arcade game came home, and this time a home game was going to the arcades.

*Kung-Fu Master* followed in 1984. *Computer and Video Games*' arcade column described it in April 1985 as one of the "hard-hitting, breath-taking fast games for Kung-Fu adepts". In America Data East sold it under licence. In Britain U.S. Gold released the home versions in 1986, and *CRASH* gave the Spectrum version 56%. According to *Wireframe*, Takashi Nishiyama designed both *Moon Patrol* and *Kung-Fu Master*, and *Kung-Fu Master* helped start the beat-'em-up genre. Nishiyama then moved to Capcom, where he worked on the first *Street Fighter*, and in 1988 to SNK.

## R-Type

*R-Type* appeared in 1987. The player flies a single R-9 fighter through eight levels against the alien Bydo Empire, each level ending with a guardian that fills much of the screen. *CRASH*'s October 1987 arcade column explained its two new ideas. Holding down fire charged the ship's gun, and releasing it let loose "a more deadly beam". A pod could be collected and attached to the front or back of the ship, or fired off across the screen "useful for clearing out infested areas".

It ran on new 16-bit hardware. *Wireframe* says Nanao designed it; the MAME emulator's driver identifies the board as the Irem M72, with an NEC V30 processor running at 8 MHz, a separate Z80 for sound and a Yamaha YM2151 sound chip. An Irem developer, quoted in a Japanese interview later translated into English, described the team's aim: "the huge battleship in stage three, the erotic-yet-grotesque enemy designs, the wave cannon… our goal at Irem is to impress you!"

It had a long life in arcades. *ZZAP!64*'s coin-op top ten still listed it, credited to Irem, in September and November 1988, a year after *CRASH* first saw it.

## R-Type at home

In Britain the home computer licence went to Electric Dreams, a label of Activision (renamed Mediagenic in 1988). Its advertisements promised versions for the Commodore 64, Spectrum, Amstrad, Atari ST and Amiga, at £9.99 on cassette and £24.99 for the 16-bit machines, under the slogan "It's behind you".

Activision guarded the licence closely. In autumn 1988 U.S. Gold was about to publish *Katakis*, a German shoot-'em-up that *ZZAP!64* described as "very much in the R-Type mould". Activision, "who hold the license from Irem to produce the official R-Type conversion", thought it close enough for legal action, and U.S. Gold withdrew the game. *ZZAP!64* had already duplicated *Katakis* as its cover tape and had to replace it at the last minute. *Katakis* came back, changed, as *Denaris*. *Amiga Computing* later reported that its programmers at Factor 5 were then hired to write the official Amiga *R-Type*: "Katakis was such a good R-Type ripoff that the Germans were chosen to produce the official version".

The conversions varied widely.

- **ZX Spectrum:** [Bob Pape](/vault/people/bob-pape)'s version was a *CRASH* Smash at 92% in the 1988 Christmas Special. "This has got to be the arcade conversion of the decade (well at least the past three months)," wrote one reviewer. *CRASH* added a joke about the *Katakis* affair: "According to Mediagenic's lawyers, R-Type is a highly original arcade action shoot-'em-up." *Sinclair User* called it "possibly the best space shoot-'em-up conversion ever".
- **Commodore 64:** *ZZAP!64* gave 72% in March 1989, praising the scrolling but calling the multiload "disastrous" and the tune "annoying".
- **Amiga:** *Amiga Computing* gave Factor 5's version 86% in August 1989: "R-type is the benchmark by which other games of this ilk must be judged."

Consoles got their own versions. Hudson Soft's PC Engine conversion impressed *The Games Machine* in July 1988, which found its scrolling, explosions and sprites "astonishing" on a console that loaded games from a small card. Sega's Master System version scored 87% from Julian Rignall in *Computer and Video Games* in March 1989. In the magazine's awards that year, the PC Engine *R-Type* was runner-up for the console award.

## After R-Type

According to *Wireframe*, Irem followed *R-Type* with other striking action games, among them *Image Fight*, *Mr. Heli*, *Dragon Breed* and *X-Multiply*. *R-Type II* reached arcades in 1989, and *Image Fight* came to the NES in 1990 under Irem's own name. In 1993 Irem released *In the Hunt*, an underwater shoot-'em-up in which the player commands a submarine.

Then Irem stopped. *Retro Gamer* reported in 2008 that the company's management "ordered all game production to be abandoned in 1994", which left the Super Nintendo game *R-Type III* looking like the end of the series. In 1997 Nanao set up a new company, Irem Software Engineering, which took over Irem's development department that July. Its first big project was *R-Type Delta* for the PlayStation. Series producer Kazuma Kujo told *Retro Gamer*: "For three years out of the five between R-Type III and Delta, Irem had completely stopped game production."

Staff who had worked on *In the Hunt* left to form [Nazca](/vault/companies/nazca), later known for *Metal Slug*. Around 2011, *Wireframe* reports, Irem decided to pull out of the games industry and concentrate on slot machines. Irem Software Engineering now describes its business as software for pachinko and pachislot machines, plus console and smartphone games.
