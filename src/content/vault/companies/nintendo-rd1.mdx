R&D1 built machines and wrote games for them. Under [Gunpei Yokoi](/vault/people/gunpei-yokoi) the same department that designed the [Game Boy](/vault/systems/game-boy) also wrote *[Metroid](/vault/games/metroid)*, *[Kid Icarus](/vault/games/kid-icarus)* and *Super Mario Land*, and it spent the mid-1990s building the Virtual Boy, the failure that ended Yokoi's time at the company. In 1994 *Super Play* described it as "a band of samurai", in the words of a colleague outside Japan.

## Fast facts

- **Led by:** Gunpei Yokoi, until he left Nintendo in 1996.
- **Hardware:** [Game & Watch](/vault/systems/nintendo-game-and-watch) (from 1980), the Game Boy (1989), the Virtual Boy (1995).
- **Famicom and NES games:** *[Balloon Fight](/vault/games/balloon-fight)*, *[Wrecking Crew](/vault/games/wrecking-crew)*, *Metroid* and *Kid Icarus*.
- **Game Boy games:** *[Super Mario Land](/vault/games/super-mario-land)* and its sequels, *[Dr. Mario](/vault/games/dr-mario)*.
- **Super NES:** *[Super Metroid](/vault/games/super-metroid)* (1994), with [Intelligent Systems](/vault/companies/intelligent-systems).
- **End:** merged into Nintendo's Software Planning & Development division in 2004, according to public sources.

## From toys to R&D1

The department began with Yokoi. He joined Nintendo to maintain its card-making machinery, and when [Hiroshi Yamauchi](/vault/people/hiroshi-yamauchi) set up a department to make games and toys, Yokoi's Ultra Hand became its first hit. Magazine histories of the 1990s date his move into games to 1969; later histories date his hiring to 1965. Public sources do not agree on when the name "Research and Development 1" came into use, so no founding year is given here. *Super Play* explained in 1994 that Yamauchi had set up three development teams, labelled R&D 1 to 3, and that each worked on both hardware and software.

The [Game & Watch](/vault/systems/nintendo-game-and-watch) handhelds came from Yokoi's team from 1980. So did the idea behind them, which Yokoi called "lateral thinking of withered technology": cheap, well-understood parts put to new uses. "The Nintendo way of adapting technology," he told David Sheff, "is not to look for the state of the art but to utilize mature technology that can be mass-produced cheaply." The multi-screen *[Donkey Kong](/vault/games/donkey-kong)* Game & Watch of 1982 introduced the cross-shaped [D-pad](/vault/hardware/d-pad) that Nintendo put on the NES controller and the Game Boy.

## Famicom games

R&D1's artists and designers drew for the Famicom by hand. Yoshio Sakamoto described in 2004 how, before digitisers, a picture was drawn on graph paper, laid over a grid of 16 by 16 LEDs and filled in square by square, with each colour chosen on a Famicom controller. He named *Balloon Fight* (1985) as the department's first Famicom game, and worked on it and on *Wrecking Crew* the same year, though in the same interview Toru Osawa recalled that R&D1's Hiroji Kiyotake had started on [*Duck Hunt*](/vault/games/duck-hunt) (1984).

In 1986 the department made two games for the [Famicom Disk System](/vault/hardware/famicom-disk-system). *Metroid* came out on 6 August, with Yokoi as producer; its on-screen credits name Satoru Okada as chief director, while *Retro Gamer* names [Yoshio Sakamoto](/vault/people/yoshio-sakamoto) as director, and the *Metroid* entry sets out the evidence. *Retro Gamer* describes Sakamoto setting out to make something unlike the games of [Shigeru Miyamoto](/vault/people/shigeru-miyamoto)'s team. *Kid Icarus* followed in December, directed by Okada and designed by Toru Osawa, who told *Nintendo Dream* in 2004 that he had worked on it alone for most of the year, with an outside company writing the program, until the department finished *Metroid* and Sakamoto joined him. In 1995 Yokoi himself summed up the department's software as "software for the Famicom and Super Famicom such as the Metroid series".

## The Game Boy

The Game Boy of 1989 was R&D1's biggest success. In Sheff's account, Yokoi's 45-strong team "married the NES and Game & Watch": a cartridge console small enough to carry, with a monochrome liquid-crystal screen. Nintendo's own figures put the Game Boy family at 118.69 million units.

The department also wrote games for it. *Wireframe* describes *Super Mario Land* (1989) as the work of "Nintendo's R&D1 squad, headed up by Game Boy designer Gunpei Yokoi", with its aliens, Moai heads and a new princess, Daisy, and notes that *Dr. Mario* and both *Super Mario Land* sequels were also R&D1 games produced by Yokoi.

## Super Metroid

Reviewing *Super Metroid* in 1994, *Super Play* said the game was "the work of none other than R&D 1, headed up by Gunpei Yokoi", a team of more than 30 developers. The game was made with the developer Intelligent Systems. Sakamoto, who directed it, told *Retro Gamer* in 2011 that only three of the original *Metroid* team worked on it and that the rest of the Nintendo side were young trainees. Yokoi oversaw the project without working on it day to day, grew angrier as its deadline slipped, and, once it was finished, told other developers to go away and play it.

## The Virtual Boy and Yokoi's departure

The Virtual Boy was R&D1's last machine under Yokoi. He told *Edge* in 1995 that about 60 people in R&D1 were working on it. It went on sale in Japan on 21 July 1995 and failed; *Edge* later reported shops selling its cartridges for ¥98.

In October 1996 *Computer and Video Games* reported that Yokoi had left Nintendo, without the reasons being revealed. *Edge* passed on a rumour that he had been "removed from active work within R&D1" after the Virtual Boy and had left because of it. He started his own company, Koto, and was killed in a road accident in October 1997.

## After Yokoi

Sakamoto, who had joined Nintendo in 1982, stayed on and directed *Metroid Fusion* (2002) and *Metroid: Zero Mission* (2004), and by 2005 *Edge* described him as the manager of the software development department behind *WarioWare*. Public sources say that in 2004, under Nintendo's president [Satoru Iwata](/vault/people/satoru-iwata), R&D1 and R&D2 were absorbed into a new Software Planning & Development division.
