## Overview

**Novagen Software** was a small Birmingham house built around one programmer,
**Paul Woakes**, and one idea: a solid, explorable three-dimensional world you inhabited
rather than shot your way through.

*Mercenary* (1985) put a whole city on an 8-bit machine in fast wireframe vector graphics —
overground and underground, with buildings you could enter, factions you could trade with,
and a single objective that was economic rather than martial. A Hungarian magazine
introducing the sequel put the house style plainly: Novagen's games are "**extremely
complex, extending to every detail**", and anyone wanting a short afternoon's diversion had
better not start.

## Fast facts

- **Programmer:** Paul Woakes — described in the period press as **Novagen's "genius programmer"**.
- **Based:** Birmingham.
- **The series:** *Mercenary: Escape from Targ* (1985) → *The Second City* (an expansion — new territory, same engine) → *Damocles* (*Mercenary II*, 1990) → *Mercenary III*.
- **In development, 1991:** ***Encounter***, described as a "very fast 3D arcade action combat simulator".
- **Prices at launch:** Spectrum **£7.95**, C64 **£9.95** cassette / £12.95 disk, Amstrad £9.95 / £19.95, Amiga **£24.95**.

## What Mercenary was

*ACE*'s buyer's guide of October 1987 describes it in two sentences that explain why it is
still discussed:

> Fly around a city of strange underground passages, all presented to you in **fast, smooth
> vector graphics**. Your goal is to **escape from war-scarred Targ by buying, stealing or
> earning an Interstellar Ship. Join forces with either of the warring Palyars or Mechanoids
> — or play them off against each other.**

The verbs are the point: **buying, stealing or earning**. The win condition is capital
accumulation. Two factions want your services, and neutrality is a playable strategy —
*ACE*'s Amiga review notes that "should you choose to remain neutral, you could even try
playing the races off against each other".

## It divided people, on purpose

*ACE*, May 1988, reviewing the Amiga release, is unusually direct about the reception:

> When *Mercenary* first appeared on 8-bit formats it immediately achieved **cult status**
> and created quite a stir amongst gameplayers. **You either loved the game and played it for
> months on end, or you hated it and couldn't play it for more than 10 minutes.**

The magazine's own verdict came down on the first side — "the game area is huge", "the game
has real depth", and for anyone "looking for a game with a huge quest to complete then look
no further".

## The wireframe was a decision

The most interesting thing about Novagen is what it declined to do. By 1988 the Amiga could
draw filled polygons, and *Carrier Command* and *Starglider 2* were doing exactly that.
*Mercenary* on the Amiga stayed in outline, and *ACE* recognised it as a choice worth
arguing about:

> Despite the power of the Amiga, the game is **still in its vector format**. This is
> controversial — **some will doubtless have wished the buildings and objects to be filled
> in, while purists would argue that this would remove most of the game's aesthetic.**

That is a genuine aesthetic position, and it is the opposite of the one held by every other
studio in this Vault's vector cohort. [Realtime](/vault/companies/realtime-games),
[Argonaut](/vault/companies/argonaut) and [David Braben](/vault/people/david-braben) all
spent the decade racing toward solid surfaces.
[Firebird](/vault/companies/firebird)/[Rainbird](/vault/companies/rainbird) bought most of
them. Woakes kept the lines, because an empty outline is what makes a city feel large.

*Amiga Format*'s December 1989 survey of the vector generation lists them side by side —
Braben with *Virus*, Realtime with *Carrier Command*, Argonaut with the *Starglider* sequel,
Mike Singleton with *Whirligig*, and "**Paul Woakes with *Mercenary***". One of those five
was not trying to fill the shapes in.

## Benson, and the office in the game

Two details that are pure Novagen.

The player's helmet computer in *Damocles* is called **Benson**, and it narrates to you
through a text strip along the top of the control panel — a companion rather than a HUD,
years before that was a convention.

And somewhere in *Damocles*'s enormous map, in a location the walkthroughs place in
**Birmingham**, there is a building labelled the **Novagen office**. The publisher wrote
itself into its own game world as a place you could walk into.

⚠ The Birmingham detail comes from a printed solution rather than a company statement, and
the walkthrough is in Hungarian. Treat the office-in-the-game as well attested and the
in-fiction geography as what it is — a joke, not an address.

## Why it mattered

Novagen is this Vault's clearest case of a **one-programmer studio holding a position against
the direction of the whole industry**. The technology moved to filled polygons; Woakes stayed
with wireframe because the game he was making was about scale and freedom rather than
spectacle, and outlines cost less to draw and therefore let the world be bigger.

It is also a reminder that the vector-graphics story of the British eighties is not only the
one that ends in Nintendo silicon. One of its five leading names spent the decade building a
city you could get lost in, sold at £7.95 on a Spectrum, and never filled in a single wall.
