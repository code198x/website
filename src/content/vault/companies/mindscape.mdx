Mindscape started out selling educational software on behalf of a school-textbook publisher, and within five years it was the American home of *Deja Vu*, *Balance of Power* and *Paperboy*. Its founder, Roger Buoy, was an Australian running a company in the Chicago suburbs. It changed hands again and again: a management buyout, a merger with The Software Toolworks, the British media group Pearson, The Learning Company, and finally a French management that kept the name going until 2011. British Amiga owners knew it best as the name on the box, from its office in Sussex, of games by [Tony Crowther](/vault/people/tony-crowther), New Zealand's [Acid Software](/vault/companies/acid-software) and the French studio that became Kalisto.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1983, in Northbrook, Illinois, by Roger Buoy, as a subsidiary of SFN Companies.
- **Owners:** SFN (1983–1987); a buyout by John Purcell and Roger Buoy (1987); The Software Toolworks (1990); Pearson (1994); The Learning Company (1998); then Mattel and Gores; from 2001, Mindscape SA in France.
- **In Britain:** Mindscape International, in Lewes by 1989 and at Burgess Hill, West Sussex, by 1992.
- **Best-known games:** *Deja Vu*, *Uninvited* and *Shadowgate* (ICOM Simulations), Chris Crawford's *Balance of Power*, *[Paperboy](/vault/games/paperboy)* and *[Gauntlet](/vault/games/gauntlet)* in America; *Captive*, *[Skidmarks](/vault/games/skidmarks)* and *Liberation* on the Amiga.
- **Bought:** Strategic Simulations Inc. (SSI) and the Bordeaux studio Atreid Concept, both in 1994.
- **End:** left the games business in August 2011 and was liquidated.

## Software for a textbook publisher

Mindscape's first owner was SFN, which its advertisements called "the country's #1 text book publisher for elementary and high schools". In 1984 Mindscape sold programs for children aged four to eight under the Sprout label, starring two robots called Tink and Tonk, and leaned on the parent company's name: "we've got a hundred years of experience to lean on." The magazine *Ahoy!* noted that October that it was "easy to be bold when you're a subsidiary of SFN", and that Mindscape's programs would come from independent developers.

That was the model from the start. Mindscape published other people's work, and it looked for them hard. At the Consumer Electronics Show in January 1985, a bad time in the American software trade, Buoy told *Compute!'s Gazette* that he had been offered four software companies by the third morning. "People make all this noise about the shakeout, but it's really a lot of nonsense," he said.

## Deja Vu and Balance of Power

Some of Mindscape's best-remembered games began on the Macintosh. *Deja Vu* (1985), by ICOM Simulations, was a mystery adventure played almost entirely with the mouse. Its objects appeared on screen as icons to pick up and use. Writing about Macintosh games in February 1987, the British magazine *Computer Gamer* called it "one of Mindscape's most inventive games", which "works because it does away with the keyboard almost entirely". ICOM followed it with *Uninvited* and *Shadowgate*, and when Mindscape brought all three to the Amiga its advertisements listed the prizes: *Deja Vu* was Family Computing's Graphic Game of the Year for 1986, and MacUser chose *Uninvited* as the year's entertainment product. The [point-and-click](/vault/techniques/point-and-click) entry follows where the idea went.

The other big name was [Chris Crawford](/vault/people/chris-crawford)'s *Balance of Power*, which Mindscape's advertisements sold as a "global geo-political simulation of the cold war" with the player as President of the United States. In November 1996 *Computer Gaming World* placed it 78th in its "Top 150 Games of All Time", calling it "a wonderful game of political intrigue that felt just like the Cold War".

## The buyout and the arcade licences

In January 1987 Mindscape's chairman, John Purcell, and Buoy bought the company from SFN. *Compute!* reported the buyout alongside a string of purchases: the educational publishers Scarborough Systems and Learning Well, and CBS Interactive Learning. *Computer Gaming World* later described the buyout as the moment Mindscape turned into an entertainment software company. Among its games of the next few years were conversions of [Atari Games](/vault/companies/atari-games) arcade machines: in America Mindscape published *[Gauntlet](/vault/games/gauntlet)* and *Paperboy* for the home computers, and advertised the two together at the end of 1987. It also published for Nintendo's NES, where its *Paperboy* was still in a German magazine's NES charts in 1991.

Buoy spoke for the industry as well as for his company. When *RUN* asked publishers in January 1987 why software cost so much, he broke down where a dollar spent on entertainment software went: 40 cents to the shop, 15 to the distributor, 20 on making the product, and 25 on everything else, from advertising and staff to the publisher's profit. By 1988 Mindscape was a public company with about 130 employees.

## The Software Toolworks

In December 1989 The Software Toolworks, the Californian publisher of the *Chessmaster* series, agreed to take Mindscape over by exchanging shares. The merger was due to complete in March 1990. Buoy stayed on to run the combined company's entertainment and international divisions and to serve as its creative director. Buoy and the Toolworks' Les Crane told *Computer Gaming World* that Nintendo products would be a main part of the combined company's range.

## Mindscape in Britain

In Britain the company traded as Mindscape International, from a PO box in Lewes, East Sussex, and later from Priority House in Burgess Hill. Its European director was Geoff Heath. By 1990 Mindscape was selling the games of [Origin Systems](/vault/companies/origin-systems) in Britain, in advertisements that carried Origin's logo and Mindscape's address. In 1991 Heath signed a deal to distribute the games of the new label [Renegade](/vault/companies/renegade), home of the [Bitmap Brothers](/vault/companies/bitmap-brothers), so the Amiga charts listed *[The Chaos Engine](/vault/games/chaos-engine)* as "Renegade/Mindscape".

*Amiga Power*'s reviews and previews named each game's publisher and its authors, and Mindscape's entries show a catalogue gathered from many studios, from Britain to France and New Zealand:

## Pearson

In 1994 the British group Pearson, owner of the *Financial Times* and Penguin Books, bought The Software Toolworks for $462 million, or $14.75 a share. The Toolworks then renamed itself after the company it had bought four years earlier, and ran as Mindscape. The same year Mindscape bought Strategic Simulations Inc., the wargame and *Advanced Dungeons & Dragons* publisher, which carried on in California as a subsidiary.

It also bought a studio. In 1994 Nicolas Gaume sold his Bordeaux company, Atreid Concept, to Mindscape, which renamed it Mindscape Bordeaux. Three years later the studio broke away as an independent company and became Kalisto. Gaume told *Edge* in 1997 that there had been "a huge amount of pressure from the parent company, Pearson, who had paid an extortionate amount for Mindscape and were looking to cut costs."

## The Learning Company and after

Pearson sold Mindscape to [The Learning Company](/vault/companies/the-learning-company) in 1998, for $150 million according to public sources. This was the company formed when SoftKey took over the original Learning Company in 1995 and adopted its name. The toy company Mattel bought the enlarged Learning Company in 1999, and in 2000 passed it, Mindscape and SSI included, to Gores Technology Group. According to public sources, Gores dropped the Mindscape name for its games business early in 2001 and sold that business to Ubi Soft.

The name survived in France. In October 2001 Jean-Pierre Nordman, a former executive of the Learning Company group, bought what remained of its international business and reorganised it as Mindscape SA, based in Boulogne-Billancourt near Paris. It published games for another decade. In August 2011 it closed Punchers Impact, its last in-house studio, with the loss of 40 jobs, after the studio's multiplayer PC game *Crasher* "was not a success", in a spokesman's words. Mindscape, he said, would no longer work in games software. The company was liquidated later that month.
