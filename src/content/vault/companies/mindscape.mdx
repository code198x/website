## Overview

*Amiga Power* printed a header on every review with two separate lines: **Publisher** and **Authors**. Across the magazine's run, Mindscape appears in the publisher slot **sixty-three times and in the author slot not once**. That is the whole entry in one fact, and it is worth an entry because the role it describes has almost vanished from how the era gets remembered.

## Fast facts

- **Press evidence in this library:** reviewed in *Amiga Power* and *Amiga Format* from **1991 to 1995**, at **£14.99–£35.99**. ⚠ This library's run begins in 1991; the company was trading from 1983, so its first 8 years are not represented here and nothing in this line should be read as a start date.
- **Published:** *Liberation*, *Fury of the Furries*, *Skidmarks*, *Alfred Chicken*, *Battletoads*, *Genesia*, *Worlds of Legend*, *Wing Commander*, *Captain Planet*, and *The Chaos Engine* on A1200.
- **Developers behind those:** [Acid Software](/vault/companies/acid-software), Kalisto, Byte Engineers, the [Bitmap Brothers](/vault/companies/bitmap-brothers), [Origin Systems](/vault/companies/origin-systems).

## The catalogue, priced

*Amazing Computing*'s Amiga product guide of spring 1989 lists Mindscape's line together,
and the list makes the point better than prose can.

| Title | Description as listed | Price |
|---|---|---|
| *Balance of Power* | "superpower leader in this nuclear-age strategic simulation pitting the U.S. against the U.S.S.R." | **$49.95** |
| *Defender of the Crown* | "Lead the Saxon knights into battle against the Normans and win the Crown of England." | **$49.95** |
| *King of Chicago* | "Battle the legendary Capone mob for control of 1930's Chicago." | **$49.95** |
| *Deja Vu: A Nightmare Comes True* | "Solve the murder, clear yourself of suspicion, and regain your memory." | **$49.95** |
| *Kill or Be Killed* | "Lead your troops into enemy territory to rescue soldiers." | **$9.95** |

Two observations, and both support the subtitle above.

**Not one of the famous ones is Mindscape's.** *Defender of the Crown* and *King of Chicago*
are **Cinemaware**'s. *Deja Vu* is **ICOM Simulations**' — the MacVenture engine that also
produced *Shadowgate* and *Uninvited*. *Balance of Power* is **Chris Crawford**'s. Mindscape's
best-remembered catalogue was assembled entirely from other people's studios, which is what a
publisher is for and exactly why nobody remembers the publisher.

**The price is flat.** Four unrelated games at **$49.95** each — a strategy simulation, two
Cinemaware productions and a graphic mystery — with a single title at $9.95. That uniformity
is the publisher's contribution made visible: a house price, a house slot on the shelf, and a
catalogue assembled to fill it.

The developers named on those games have their own entries in this Vault. Mindscape, which
paid for them and put them in shops, does not appear in most accounts of any of them.

## What a publisher did

Nothing in that list was made by Mindscape. *Skidmarks* is Acid Software's, from New Zealand. *Fury of the Furries* is Kalisto's, from France. *The Chaos Engine* on A1200 is the Bitmap Brothers', originally published by [Renegade](/vault/companies/renegade) and appearing under Mindscape for the AGA release two years later.

What a publisher supplied was everything around the code: **manufacturing, boxes, manuals, advertising, shelf space, and the cash to survive the eighteen months between starting a game and being paid for it**. A four-person studio in Auckland could write an excellent racing game and had no way at all to get it into a branch of Boots in Bristol. Mindscape did.

The Chaos Engine case shows how the arrangement moved. A game can be Renegade's in 1993 and Mindscape's in 1994 without changing a line of its code — different territory, different format, different deal. From the outside, all you see is a different name on the box, which is exactly why publisher credits are such an unreliable guide to who made anything.

## The other Mindscape catalogue

The fast facts above date this library's Mindscape evidence to 1991–1995 and flag that the
company's first eight years are not represented. One strand of those missing years is worth
recovering, because it is the same argument in a different decade.

By 1990 Mindscape International was the British distributor for
**[Origin Systems](/vault/companies/origin-systems)** — the *Ultima* series and its
stablemates. Full-page advertisements for *Space Rogue* and *Windwalker* run under Origin's
logo and motto, and then close with:

> For further information on **Mindscape** products and your local Dealer, contact:
> **Mindscape International Limited, PO Box 1019, Lewes, East Sussex BN8 4DW.**

Origin's games, Origin's branding, Origin's slogan — and Mindscape's address and dealer
network at the foot of the page. It is the publisher case exactly: the invisible half of the
advertisement is the half that got the disk into a shop.

⚠ Note that this arrangement was inherited rather than permanent. Through the late eighties
the same Origin catalogue was advertised in Britain by
**[MicroProse](/vault/companies/microprose)** from Tetbury. Distribution rights moved; the
games did not change. This is the same point the *Chaos Engine* case makes below, five years
earlier and in the other direction.

## The mid-price tier

Mindscape's prices come in two clear bands. New releases sat at **£24.99–£35.99**; a second line ran at **£14.99**, and the 1993 issues are thick with it. *TV Sports Boxing* appears at £14.99 in 1992 — a [Cinemaware](/vault/companies/cinemaware) game, priced like a reissue, the year after Cinemaware itself had gone.

That is the shape of a maturing market. Once a catalogue exists, the back of it can be sold again more cheaply to people who would never have paid full price, and it costs almost nothing to do. Every publisher that lasted long enough worked this out.

## Why it mattered

Mindscape is here as **the publisher case**: the part of the industry that made no games and without which most games did not reach anyone. It is a genuinely unglamorous role, and the reason the Vault records it is that its absence distorts the picture. Games did not go from a bedroom to a shop by themselves.

It is also a corrective to reading a box as a credit. When you find a name on a cassette or a floppy, the honest question is not "who made this" but "which of the several companies involved has its name on this particular edition".
