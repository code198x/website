Konami began in 1969 as a jukebox rental and repair business in Osaka, Japan. By the early 1980s, they had pivoted to arcade games and never looked back. Their output—*Frogger*, *Scramble*, *Gradius*, *Castlevania*, *Contra*, *Metal Gear*—defined genres and spawned gaming's most recognisable secret code.

## Fast facts
- **Founded:** 1969 in Osaka by Kagemasa Kozuki.
- **Name origin:** disputed, but possibly derived from the founders' names.
- **Arcade breakthrough:** *[Scramble](/vault/games/scramble)* (1981) pioneered the side-scrolling shooter; *[Frogger](/vault/games/frogger)* (1981) became a cultural phenomenon.
- **Console dominance:** NES-era classics like *[Castlevania](/vault/games/castlevania)* (1986) and *[Contra](/vault/games/contra)* (1987) cemented their reputation.
- **The Code:** ↑↑↓↓←→←→BA—first used in *[Gradius](/vault/games/gradius)* (1986), immortalised in *Contra* for granting 30 lives.

## The Konami Code
Programmer Kazuhisa Hashimoto added the code to the NES port of *Gradius* because he found the game too difficult to test. It was never removed. When *Contra* shipped with the same code granting 30 extra lives, players spread the secret through playgrounds and magazines. The sequence has since appeared in hundreds of games, websites, and even Netflix.

## Home computer ports
Konami's arcade hits were ported extensively to home platforms:
- **Frogger:** Parker Brothers handled the C64 and Atari versions; other publishers covered the Spectrum and Amstrad.
- **Nemesis/Gradius:** Spectrum and Amstrad ports captured the side-scrolling shooter formula.
- **Licensed vs. in-house:** quality varied wildly depending on who handled the conversion.

## Why it mattered
- **Genre definition:** *Scramble* and *Gradius* established the scrolling shooter template. *Castlevania* refined action-platformers. *Metal Gear* pioneered stealth.
- **Cross-platform reach:** Konami's games appeared on nearly every system of the 80s and 90s, from arcade cabinets to home computers to consoles.
- **Cultural impact:** the Konami Code transcended gaming, becoming a symbol of hidden secrets and insider knowledge.

## How Konami reached Britain

For most of the eighties a British player did not buy a Konami game from Konami. They bought
it from [Ocean](/vault/companies/ocean-software), under the
[Imagine](/vault/companies/imagine-software) name Ocean had acquired.

That arrangement produced the highest-scoring games ever to carry the Imagine label —
*Mikie* **93%**, *Movie* **93%**, *Hypersports* **92%**, *Green Beret* **88%** in *CRASH*'s
issue 48 index — all of them Konami coin-ops converted in Manchester. See the
[Imagine](/vault/companies/imagine-software) entry for what that says about the label.

*Amtix* got David Ward and Colin Stokes of Ocean to discuss it in April 1986, and the candid
part is about how little the licence guaranteed. On *Hypersports*, which Ocean had picked as
"the real blockbuster" of the deal, a freelance programmer "nearly gave up programming the
game" — so that "by the time it came in **we were so relieved to get the flipping thing out
on the streets**".

The relationship outlived the 8-bit machines. *Amiga User International*, July 1988:

> *Nemesis*, the classic Konami coin-op, will soon be appearing on the Amiga, courtesy of a
> **new agreement between Konami U.S. and Ocean** … the deal will mean that Amiga
> conversions of [other] Konami games such as *Gradius*, *Russian Attack* [follow].

Konami did begin publishing under its own name in Britain — *CRASH*'s index carries
*Nemesis* as a Konami release at **47%** (issue 38) and *Konami's Golf* under Imagine at
**58%** — and the comparison is unkind. **The Konami games that reviewed best in Britain
were the ones a British publisher converted; the ones Konami shipped itself did not.**
