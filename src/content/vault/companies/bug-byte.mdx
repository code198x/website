## Overview

Bug-Byte was founded in **May 1980** by **Tony Baden** and **Tony Milner**, two
students reading chemistry at Liverpool University. *Big K* — which ran a company
history in July 1984, when the principals were still speaking to journalists —
records that in the first year the pair had no duplicating equipment at all, so
that "each mail order requirement meant a SAVE": every tape sold was recorded by
hand, one at a time.

The company's earliest advertisements give a Coventry address, 251 Henley Road;
by October 1981 it was trading from 98-100 The Albany, Old Hall Street,
Liverpool. That move is the point at which Bug-Byte becomes the Liverpool company
of the histories, and it arrived early enough that both of the Merseyside
companies that followed — [Imagine](/vault/companies/imagine-software) and
[Software Projects](/vault/companies/software-projects) — were founded by people
walking out of its offices.

Bug-Byte's biggest commercial moment was [*Manic Miner*](/vault/games/manic-miner)
(November 1983), a Matthew Smith original that topped the Spectrum charts. It was
also the moment the company began coming apart.

## Two machines at once, not one

The usual summary — a ZX81 house that moved to the Spectrum — is half the story.
Through 1981 and 1982 Bug-Byte published for the **Acorn Atom** and the **ZX81**
side by side, and the Atom line was the more visible of the two in the general
press. *Your Computer* carried full-page Bug-Byte Atom advertisements from mid-1981:
*Fruit Machine* (£5.00), *Chess*, *Invaders*, *Golf* (£5.00), an Atom
disassembler (£4.00), and a *747* flight simulation "written for Bug-Byte by a
747 pilot". *Your Computer*'s November 1981 review round-up rated the Atom
*Fruit Machine* and *Invaders* alongside Acornsoft's own output.

The ZX81 catalogue ran in parallel: *The Damsel and the Beast* (£6.50),
*Dictator* — a small-state political simulation in which the player manages the
secret police and the economy while avoiding assassination — *House of Gnomes*,
*Star Trek*, and a numbered series of Program Packs at £3.50-£5.00.

The Spectrum catalogue that followed is the one usually listed:

- ***Manic Miner*** (1983) — Matthew Smith's platformer, and the defining release.
- ***Styx*** (1983) — Smith's *first* game for Bug-Byte, which by the company's own
  account "bombed out".
- ***Aquaplane*** (1983), ***Birds and the Bees*** (1983), ***Hunchback*** (1983).
- ***Twin Kingdom Valley*** (1983) — text adventure, still charting in *CRASH*'s
  adventure top 30 in March 1985.
- ***Alchemist*** (1983) — Ian Weatherburn.
- ***Antics*** (1984) — written by the 17-year-old Adrian Sherwin.

*Manic Miner* was big enough to change how Bug-Byte reached shops.
*Personal Computer Games* reported in 1983 that "Bug-Byte Software, the company
that brought us *Manic Miner*... has signed a major distribution deal with CBS,
the record and video company", giving CBS exclusive rights to market all Bug-Byte
software in the UK — a record label distributing computer games, which in 1983
was novel enough to be news.

## The machine-code tools

The part of the catalogue that matters most here is the part nobody remembers.
In 1981 Bug-Byte shipped **ZXAS**, a machine-code assembler for the 16K ZX81, and
**ZXDB**, a companion disassembler and debugger. ZXAS occupied 5K, reset RAMTOP
and loaded itself above it so that source could be saved separately, and took its
source as Z80 mnemonics typed into `REM` statements — semicolons separating
multiple instructions on a line, up to 256 labels in the form `:L0` to `:L255`,
comments after a `*`, and full stops instead of commas, so `LD A,H` was typed
`LD A.H`.

*Sinclair User* ran a survey of ZX81 assemblers by **Phil Garrett** in July 1982
that put it plainly: ZXAS "is a remarkable program and is excellent value at £5",
and using either it or the rival ACS assembler was "the single biggest step to
proficiency in machine code programming". The same review names a bug — `SUB A,n`
did not work, and had to be replaced with `AND A; SBC A,n` — the sort of detail
that surfaces only when a reviewer has written real code with the thing.

This is the connection worth holding on to. The Liverpool company that published
*Manic Miner* had, two years earlier, published one of the tools that made games
like *Manic Miner* possible to write on a Sinclair machine at all. Bug-Byte was
selling the ladder before it sold anything that had climbed it.

## Two walkouts

Bug-Byte lost its programming staff twice inside fourteen months, and both
departures founded a company larger than the one they left.

**January 1983 — Imagine.** *ACE*, looking back at 1983 from January 1990,
records that "its head programmer Eugene Evans, and senior staff David Lawson and
Mark Butler broke away from the company to form Imagine Software". *Big K*'s
account has the departures staggered, with Butler following roughly two months
after Lawson. Imagine's first game, *Arcadia*, went on to be one of the
best-selling Spectrum titles of the year.

**Early 1984 — Software Projects.** Matthew Smith left with *Manic Miner*,
together with **Alan and Soo Maton**. *CRASH*'s first issue reports both Matons
leaving; *Personal Computer Games* describes Alan's rise at Bug-Byte as running
"from despatch rider to marketing manager in a matter of months", which is a
correction worth making because the managing-director version of the story is
widely repeated.

*CRASH*'s own retrospective put the consequence bluntly: Bug-Byte was "founding
father of the home-computer game and many well-known individuals and several
software houses had sprung from the Liverpool company."

## The Manic Miner split, from Bug-Byte's side

Smith's account — that he was under-paid relative to what *Manic Miner* earned —
is the one that survived. Bug-Byte's is on the record too, and it is worth
reading beside it.

Interviewed by *Big K* in July 1984, Tony Baden gave a figure: "they all know
what Matthew's been saying, that he didn't get enough from *Manic Miner*... But I
can tell you he got £50,000 cash from that one game while we sold it."

On why the game left with its author rather than staying with its publisher,
Baden described a drafting failure — a comma in the wrong place, or a missing one,
he could not recall which — that left the contract ambiguous about who owned what.
The company chose not to litigate, and the reason is the detail that makes the
episode make sense: **Smith was still a minor when he signed.** Baden's summary
was that against a minor in open court nobody has a chance. Bug-Byte let the game
go rather than fight for it.

Software Projects released *Jet Set Willy* in April 1984 and re-issued *Manic
Miner* under its own label — so that, as *Big K* noted, the same game had by then
been in the charts under two labels, old and new. Bug-Byte was left with the back
catalogue.

The split is a frequently-cited example of the early Spectrum industry's
contractual learning curve. What the Bug-Byte side adds is that the learning was
not only about money. It was about drafting, and about the legal position of an
industry whose best programmers were school-age.

## Receivership, and the Argus afterlife

Bug-Byte went into **receivership in June 1985**. *CRASH*'s history of the period
calls it the biggest failure of that month and concedes the obvious — "the quality
of Bug-Byte's products had dropped" — while recording that it still came as a sad
surprise, "causing almost as many fears that the end was nigh as had Imagine's
disintegration". Quicksilva had already taken the rights to seven Bug-Byte games
by May 1985.

**Argus Press Software** had been negotiating a merger and could not close it
before the receivership; it bought the company from the receiver afterwards and
ran Bug-Byte as a **budget label**, at £2.95 and below. Games appeared under the
revived name into 1987 — *Dogsbody*, *Cloak of Death*, a set of sports titles sold
by mail order through *Computer Gamer* in May 1987 — which is why the Bug-Byte
name outlives the Bug-Byte company by two years in the review columns.
