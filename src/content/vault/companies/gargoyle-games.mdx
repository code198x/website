Gargoyle Games did not look like the rest of the Spectrum industry. Its two programmers, [Greg Follis](/vault/people/greg-follis) and [Roy Carter](/vault/people/roy-carter), were not teenagers: when *[CRASH](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine)* visited at the end of 1985 they had worked together in the computer department of a large business for around fifteen years, and both had mortgages. They started writing games in their spare time because, as Follis put it, "it was a method to make money that we could afford." Their office was on the third floor of a brick-and-concrete block in King Street, Dudley, above an insurance company. Out of it came *Tir Na Nog*, *[Dun Darach](/vault/games/dun-darach)* and *Marsport*, which *CRASH* scored 92%, 97% and 95%, and *[Heavy on the Magick](/vault/games/heavy-on-the-magick)*, which its readers voted the best adventure of 1986.

## Fast facts

- **Founders:** Greg Follis and Roy Carter, who wrote the games. Ted Heathcote, a long-standing friend of Follis, sold them and booked the advertising.
- **Base:** 74 King Street, Dudley, in the West Midlands. *CRASH* placed the new company in Birmingham in May 1984; Gargoyle's own adverts gave the Dudley address from late 1984.
- **First game:** *Ad Astra* (1984), a 3D space shoot 'em up, £5.95.
- **Second label:** Faster Than Light (FTL), from autumn 1986.
- **Work for others:** *Scooby Doo* (1986) and *Thundercats* (1987), both published by [Elite](/vault/companies/elite-systems).

## From business software to *Ad Astra*

The *CRASH* profile, in its Christmas 1985 issue, told the story in the founders' own words, with the two of them interrupting each other "like two stand-up comedians". Follis started as a programmer at a firm called Hewitts, moved into systems analysis because the money was better, and met Carter there. "When we started, we didn't like each other very much," Follis said. They found they worked well together, and invented a department, "New Products Research and Development", to share an office. Before Gargoyle, the firm had sent them on artificial intelligence courses.

Stories of teenage programmers earning fortunes pushed them to try games. *Ad Astra* took nine months of evenings and weekends. *CRASH* reviewed it in May 1984: a 48K machine-code game at £5.95 with "very large graphics, all depicted in a very solid three dimensions with perspective", in which planetoids hurtle out of the screen towards the player's ship. "It's quite a debut too," the reviewer wrote, and the magazine gave it 80%.

## The Cuchulainn games

*Tir Na Nog* began with a routine. "One day after releasing *Ad Astra* I wrote this routine which showed a character walking in a scrolling background," Carter recalled. Follis thought it "looked nice", and they went looking for a story that fitted the walker. They found the Irish hero Cuchulainn. *Sinclair User* later explained the figure's realism: Follis and Carter based the frames of animation on sequential pictures of real walking. Gargoyle's own advert for *Tir Na Nog* promised "state-of-the-art film animation" and "a true computer movie".

The large, smoothly animated hero walked through a scrolling landscape, which the player could view from any of four directions by turning the "camera". *CRASH* gave *Tir Na Nog* 92% in December 1984 and noted that "strategical thinking and forward planning" mattered as much as fighting. *Dun Darach* (1985) put Cuchulainn in a walled city of shops, banks and pickpockets, and took 97%, the highest score *CRASH* gave any Gargoyle game. *Marsport* (1985) moved the system to science fiction and took 95%. In *CRASH*'s readers' awards for 1985 it came third in a category won by The Edge's *[Fairlight](/vault/games/fairlight)*, two votes ahead of Hewson's *Dragontorc*.

Follis described what they were aiming for. "We make stories that seem to be the tip of the iceberg, so you always have the impression that there was a lot more going on that you didn't know about," he said. "That is what we're aiming for, a true computer movie."

## *Sweevo's World* and *Heavy on the Magick*

*Sweevo's World* (1985) was a deliberate change of pace, a comic isometric game about a clumsy robot. Follis wanted it to be "a bit of fun": "The industry is far too serious now." *CRASH* gave it 95%, and later wrote that it proved Gargoyle "had more in them than complex graphic adventures of the *Dun Darach* type".

*Heavy on the Magick* (1986) combined a large animated sorcerer, Axil, with typed commands. *CRASH*'s Adventure Trail gave it 9 out of 10 and *Sinclair User* five stars. *CRASH*'s readers voted it to number 1 in their Adventure chart in September 1986, and it held that place for ten of the next twelve months; in the Hotline, which took votes for every kind of game, it peaked at 13. Both charts counted readers' votes, not sales (see [Reading the charts](/vault/culture/reading-the-charts)). The same readers voted it the best adventure of 1986.

## "We've got to go with the market"

Behind the scores, the founders were frank about the business. Carter reckoned they lost half their potential sales "not through piracy but by not having adequate PR". Follis explained why they could not simply keep making games like *Dun Darach*: "I would be quite happy to turn out *Dun Darachs* but there's a market out there and say 50% of it is buying *Daley Thompson's Decathlon* so you've got to go with the market trend." They had to release a game every few months to live, and talked of taking on staff or becoming a development house like [Denton Designs](/vault/companies/denton-designs). Heathcote told *[Amstrad Action](/vault/magazines/amstrad-action)*: "We can't be as big as they are, but we do have to compete."

Their plans were ambitious. *Marsport* was announced as the first part of a "Siege of Earth" trilogy, and the second part, *Fornax*, was to take commands through icons or typed sentences, with characters Gargoyle hoped to make "genuinely intelligent". *Amstrad Action* expected it in January 1986. It was never released.

## Faster Than Light

In autumn 1986 Gargoyle opened a second label. *CRASH* reviewed its first game, *Lightforce*, in November:

> *Lightforce* is also the first game from the Faster Than Light label. This is an off-shoot of Gargoyle Games, the people responsible for the acclaimed scrolling graphic adventure quests *Tir Na Nog*, *Dun Darach* and *Heavy on the Magick*. The new label allows programmers Greg Follis and Roy Carter to depart from a game style which they have made their own, and to take on mainstream arcade entertainment.

*Lightforce* was a vertically scrolling shoot 'em up with large, brightly coloured sprites. "The graphics are THE BEST that I have seen on a Spectrum shoot 'em up, if not in any game," one reviewer wrote; another, who had waited for it since the PCW Show, said "usually any game I wait for with such anticipation is bound to disappoint me, but this game really is the exception to the rule!" It took 91% and a Smash. Looking back a year later, *CRASH* pointed out that this was not new territory: Gargoyle "had started out three years earlier, with the 3-D shoot-'em-up *Ad Astra*", and *Lightforce*'s graphics "strongly resembled" it.

Readers agreed with the reviewers. *[Amtix!](/vault/magazines/amtix)*'s readers voted the Amstrad version the best shoot 'em up of 1986, with 24% of the vote, ahead of Elite's *Ikari Warriors* and Electric Dreams' *Tempest*. *CRASH*'s readers put it second in their State of the Art award, behind *Starglider* and ahead of *Heavy on the Magick*.

FTL followed it with *Shockway Rider* (1987), which *CRASH* gave 67%, and *Hydrofool* (1987), a sequel to *Sweevo's World* set under water.

## Work for Elite, and after

Gargoyle also programmed licensed games for Elite. *Scooby Doo* reached the Spectrum in 1986 after, as *CRASH* put it, "a long wait and several scrapped versions", and scored 91%. *Sinclair User* reported that *Thundercats* (1987) was designed by Follis and programmed by Carter, and that new staff had joined to draw graphics and help Carter with the programming. Elite's adverts quoted *CRASH*'s 91% and Smash.

In 1988 Hewson's budget label, Rebound, reissued Gargoyle's three scrolling adventures, and *Sinclair User* said their animation had "not been bettered to this day". Public sources say little about Gargoyle or its founders after the late 1980s.
