## Overview
Gargoyle Games were **Roy Carter** and **Greg Follis**, working out of
Birmingham. *CRASH* introduced them in May 1984 as a new company whose first
game, *Ad Astra*, was a £5.95 space combat game written in machine code — and
called it "quite a debut".

What followed was a run of arcade adventures with a common temperament: large,
slow, literate, and built around a rotoscoped walking animation that gave their
characters a distinctive gait. They reviewed at the top of the market and sold
to a narrower audience than the scores deserved — and in 1986 they did something
about it.

## Fast facts
- **Founders:** Roy Carter and Greg Follis, credited together in *CRASH* review boxes from 1984 onwards.
- **Base:** Birmingham.
- **First game:** *Ad Astra* (1984), £5.95, 48K, machine code — **80%** (*CRASH* issue 4).
- **Second label:** **Faster Than Light (FTL)**, launched 1986.
- **Typical price:** £9.95 — full price, not budget.

**Confirmed *CRASH* scores, from the issue 48 index:**

separate ten-point scale for adventures. The two systems are not comparable and the
9/10 should not be read as 90%.

## The catalogue
*Tir Na Nog* (1984) established the house style: an Irish-mythological
landscape explored by a large, smoothly animated Cuchulainn, with an inventory
system that expected forward planning across some 150 objects. *CRASH*'s review
credits Follis and Carter jointly and dwells on the strategic thinking the
object limit forces. It took **92%**.

*Dun Darach* (1985) refined it into a city, and took **97%** — the highest score
attached to any company in this Vault's working set. *Marsport* (1985) moved the
same engine to science fiction at **95%** and, by *CRASH*'s account, beat Hewson's
*Dragontorc* in that year's reader voting. *Sweevo's World* (1985) took **95%** for a
comic isometric game unlike anything else they made. *Heavy on the Magick* (1986) gave the
formula a sorcerer, Axil, and demons who bargain rather than attack.

## Where they charted
Gargoyle's games did well, and did well in a particular place. [*Heavy on the
Magick*](/vault/games/heavy-on-the-magick) held **#1** in *CRASH*'s Adventure
chart three separate times while sitting at **#16** in the general Hotline
chart. [*Dun Darach*](/vault/games/dun-darach) peaked at #5 in the general chart
in 1985 and was still in the Adventure Top 30 in early 1987.

That is the signature of a house with a devoted audience rather than a broad
one. The reviews were not the constraint — *Heavy on the Magick* took 5/5 from
*Sinclair User* and *Your Computer* and 9/10 from *CRASH* and *Your Sinclair* —
the shelf was.

*CRASH* thought them interesting enough to profile the crew in its 1985/6
Christmas Special, which is not something that happened to every two-person
company.

## Faster Than Light

In the autumn of 1986 Follis and Carter launched a second label, and the reasoning is
legible from the outside: **they made the other kind of game.**

*CRASH*, reviewing its first release in November 1986:

> *Lightforce* is also the first game from the **Faster Than Light** label. This is an
> off-shoot of **GARGOYLE GAMES**, the people responsible for the acclaimed scrolling
> graphic adventure quests *Tir Na Nog*, *Dun Darach* and *Heavy on the Magick*. **The new
> label allows programmers Greg Follis and Roy Carter** [to work in a different form].

*Lightforce* is a vertically scrolling shoot 'em up — no inventory, no mythology, no
planning, nothing to read. *CRASH*'s reviewers were unguarded about it: "**The graphics are
THE BEST that I have seen on a Spectrum shoot 'em up**", and, from another, "usually any
game I wait for with such anticipation is bound to disappoint me, but this game really is
the exception to the rule". It took **91%**.

Then *Amtix*'s readers voted it **Best Shoot 'Em Up of 1986**, with 24% of the vote — ahead
of Mirrorsoft's *Strike Force Harrier* and Ocean's *Top Gun*, and, in another category,
pipping Elite's *Ikari Warriors*.

*Shockway Rider* followed on the same label.

This is the part of the Gargoyle story usually left out, and it is the part that shows the
two of them understood their own position. A studio celebrated for slow, dense, literate
games — and charting in the Adventure Top 30 rather than the shelf everyone walks past —
did not keep making the same thing and hoping. **It opened a second front, under a
different name, and won the arcade category at the first attempt.**
