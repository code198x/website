Llamasoft began as Jeff Minter’s one-person label in 1982 and quickly became a cult favourite. Operating from rural Wales, the studio mailed brightly packaged cassettes filled with laser-fast shooters, experimental light synths, and in-jokes about llamas, camels, and sheep.

⚠ **"Rural Wales" is wrong for the period this Vault covers.** Llamasoft's own address, printed
in the magazines through the late eighties and into the nineties, is **49 Mount Pleasant,
Tadley, Hampshire RG26 6BN** — telephone 07356 4478. Minter moved to Wales later; the games of this period were
mailed out of Hampshire.

## Fast facts
- **Flagship titles:** *Gridrunner*, *[Attack of the Mutant Camels](/vault/games/attack-of-the-mutant-camels)*, *Revenge of the Mutant Camels*.  
- **Platforms:** Started on VIC-20 and C64, later expanded to Atari 8-bit, Amiga, [Atari ST](/vault/systems/atari-st), Nuon, iOS, and VR.  
- **Distribution:** Direct mail order plus partnerships with publishers like Human Engineered Software (HES) for international releases.

## The five-pound honesty box

The clearest surviving description of how Llamasoft did business comes from
*Amiga Computing* in October 1991, which cover-mounted ***Llamatron*** and explained the
terms:

> Llamatron is a **shareware** game. This means that if you like it and want to play it some
> more, and **you're an honest person**, you'll send **£5** to: LLAMASOFT, 49 Mount Pleasant,
> Tadley, Hants RG26 6BN. In return, you'll receive **another Llamasoft game, a brilliant
> poster of a gun-toting llama, and a Llamasoft newsletter**. For five quid it has to be a
> bargain.

That is the whole operation in one paragraph, and it is worth reading against the rest of
this Vault's company entries. No distributor, no shelf, no advertising spend, no review
embargo — a game given away, a price set on the buyer's conscience, and the reward for
paying being **more relationship rather than more product**: a second game, a poster, and a
newsletter.

The same magazine's summary of the label's standing is worth keeping: Llamasoft as
"**possibly the world's freakiest software house**", following "a determined path of sheep,
llama, and camel-fixation".

## Not making games, on purpose

By 1989 Minter was spending, in *ACE*'s phrase, "**an uncommercially large chunk of his
programming** [time]" on something that was not a game at all: **Trip-A-Tron**, a light
synthesiser containing **over 28,000 lines of assembler**.

He was also working with **Alex Blok** on **Project Flashdance** and the **Bit Bopper** —
an attempt at integrated visual entertainment systems running across linked Archimedes
machines and driving "devices such as lasers or other effects equipment, as well as
monitors".

*ACE*'s sign-off tells you how the trade regarded this: "if you don't share the
**llama-loving hippy values** which run deep in at least one of its creators… **Go on, give
a talented old hippy a bit of support!**"

The point is not the eccentricity. It is that a commercially viable games programmer chose
to spend his best years building tools for making light shows, and the press covered it as a
career rather than a breakdown. Few people in this Vault had that option, because few
few had a direct relationship with their customers.

## Cultural impact
Llamasoft proved a studio could have a consistent, eccentric voice and still ship commercially. Their newsletters fostered community decades before social media.
