## Overview

**Superior Software Ltd** was the leading publisher for [Acorn](/vault/companies/acorn-computers)'s
home machines — the BBC Micro and the Electron — operating from Leeds while most of the
British industry clustered around Manchester, London and the Midlands.

It is remembered for two games, *Repton* (1985) and *Exile* (1988). This entry is mostly
about what came before them, because that is what this library's Acorn coverage
holds.

⚠ **Scope warning.** This Vault's magazine corpus is weighted towards Spectrum, Commodore
and Amiga titles. Acorn coverage runs to *Acorn User*, *Acorn Programs*, *Micro User* and
*Electron User*, and it is thin. What follows is grounded in the company's own advertising
rather than in editorial, and is a snapshot of **1984** — before the games it is famous for.

## Fast facts

- **Address, as printed:** **Superior Software Ltd, Dept. AP2, 69 Leeds Road, Bramhope, Leeds.** Tel: 0532 842385.
- **Platforms sought:** **BBC Micro, Electron and Oric-1** — the last of which is not the association the company is usually given.
- **Prices, 1984:** **£7.95** for a 32K cassette game (a few at £6.95), **£11.95** on 5¼-inch disc.
- **Royalties offered:** **up to 20%**, raised in later insertions to **25%**.
- **Distribution:** named dealers across Britain plus "**more than 300 other dealers throughout the UK and overseas**", and selected branches of **Boots**.

## The 1984 catalogue

Before *Repton*, Superior sold what everyone sold — arcade clones and card games, at £7.95 a
cassette:

*Space Fighter*, *Galaxians*, *Star Trek Adventure*, *Invaders*, *Fruit Machine*,
*Lost City*, *Cribbage*, *Gideon's Gamble*, *Pontoon*.

There is nothing distinctive in that list, which is the point. The company that would publish
*Exile* began by selling a *Galaxians* clone to BBC Micro owners, and got where it got by
being reliable rather than by being visionary.

## The guarantee

The most interesting thing in Superior's advertising is not the games. Every insertion
carries a numbered guarantee, and it reads as a direct attack on how the rest of the industry
behaved:

> **WE GUARANTEE THAT:**
>
> 1. **All our software runs correctly on all current operating systems and BASIC ROMs.**
> 2. **All our software is available before we advertise.**
> 3. **All our software is despatched within 48 hours by first-class post.**
> 4. In the unlikely event that any of our software fails to load, return your cassette or
>    disc to us and **we will immediately send a replacement.**

Read the second one again. **"Available before we advertise"** is a promise that the game in
the advertisement exists — which is only worth printing in a market where that could not be
assumed.

It could not. This Vault records [Imagine](/vault/companies/imagine-software) selling the
Megagames to the press for a year with, on the Receiver's evidence, barely one disk to show
for it; [Beyond](/vault/companies/beyond-software) announcing *Eye of the Moon* and never
shipping it; and *Time and Magik* "endlessly delayed" under Rainbird until
[Level 9](/vault/companies/level-9) walked. A Leeds publisher made not doing that into a
selling point, in a numbered list, next to the price of *Pontoon*.

Point 1 is the Acorn-specific version of the same discipline. The BBC Micro shipped in
several operating-system and BASIC ROM revisions, and software that worked on one could fail
on another. Guaranteeing compatibility across all current ROMs is a testing commitment, not a
slogan.

## What the royalty rate says

Superior advertised for submissions in the same space as it advertised games — "**we pay up
to 20% royalties for high quality BBC Micro, Electron and Oric-1 programs**", and in a later
insertion **25%**.

Set that against the rest of this Vault. The [bedroom-coder](/vault/phenomena/bedroom-coder)
entry records typical Spectrum terms of **10–25%** of dealer price in 1985–87, and
[Graftgold](/vault/companies/graftgold)'s Andrew Braybrook telling *ACE* in 1991 that about
**7%** of a £25 game reached the programmer. Superior was advertising the top of the
contemporary range, publicly, as a recruitment tool — a small publisher on a minority
platform competing for authors on terms rather than on reach.

## Repton, and the reach of it

*Repton* (1985) is the game the company is remembered for, and the clearest surviving measure
of its influence in this library comes from an unexpected direction. The Fred Fish public
domain catalogue — reprinted in Amiga guides from 1990 through 1994 — describes the PD game
*Wanderer* like this:

> The idea for *Wanderer* came from games such as **Boulder Dash**, **XOR**, and **the Repton
> games from Superior Software**.

An Amiga PD author, years later and on another platform, citing a BBC Micro game as a
founding influence alongside *Boulder Dash* — which is the company that spent 1984 selling
*Galaxians* clones ending up in the same sentence as one of the decade's landmark designs.
