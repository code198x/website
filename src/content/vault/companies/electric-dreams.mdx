Electric Dreams was the label [Activision](/vault/companies/activision) paid for so that British programmers would write for it. Rod Cousens had run [Quicksilva](/vault/companies/quicksilva) until its sale to Argus Press, and in the summer of 1985 he was planning a new software house from an office above an insurance company in Southampton. Activision signed it before it had released a game. Over the next four years it published one of the most admired 8-bit games of 1986, *Spindizzy*, a pair of rival games of the same film, and the Spectrum *R-Type*.

## Fast facts

- **Launched:** at the Personal Computer World Show, September 1985.
- **Founder:** Rod Cousens, formerly managing director of [Quicksilva](/vault/companies/quicksilva).
- **Owner:** [Activision](/vault/companies/activision), which handled sales, distribution and finance.
- **Base:** 31 Carlton Crescent, Southampton. Mail orders went to Activision (UK) Ltd in Wellingborough, Northamptonshire.
- **Best-known games:** *I, of the Mask* (1985), *Spindizzy* (1986), *Aliens* (1987), *Super Hang-On* and *R-Type* (1988).
- **Price:** usually £9.99 on cassette for the Spectrum, Commodore 64 and Amstrad CPC.

## Out of Quicksilva

In September 1985 *CRASH* found Cousens "on the top floor of an Insurance company office in Southampton", after what he called a "period of exile". He had joined Quicksilva in 1981 and left after Argus Press bought it in 1984 and moved it to London. Earlier in 1985 he had organised *Soft Aid*, a charity compilation for the Ethiopian famine appeal which *CRASH* said had passed more than £350,000 to the Band Aid Trust. His new company, the magazine reported, "may well surface under the name Electric Dreams" at that month's show, with its games chosen by Paul Cooper, formerly Quicksilva's software editor. "It would hardly be surprising," it added, "if a few famous names from Quicksilva made their mark on the new label."

A month later *CRASH* had the rest of the story: Electric Dreams was signed to Activision, which said the deal fitted a long-term plan to start "a second or maybe a third label". In 2012 Cousens told *Retro Gamer* that he had been about to fly to San Francisco to pitch for another company's European rights when Activision's Greg Fischbach rang him the day after a meeting and asked: "How would you like to be funded?" Cooper looked after product development, Clare Hirsch ran marketing, and Activision handled the sales, distribution and finance.

Cousens described the label to *Your Computer* in February 1986 as "an umbrella for beleaguered independents": "Activision can provide worldwide market support, even in the States. No other software house can provide that." He promised about three licensed games a year from television, film or pop, and insisted that the former Quicksilva people had come to him, not the other way round. "I see Electric Dreams as taking all the strengths of the old Quicksilva and, well, just carrying on."

## The first games

The first release came from [Sandy White](/vault/people/sandy-white), who had written *[Ant Attack](/vault/games/ant-attack)* for Quicksilva. *I, of the Mask* was a 3D maze in which the player collected the parts of a robot, feet first, and finally the mask itself. *CRASH* reviewed it in December 1985 at £9.95, called the graphics "the most remarkable" one reviewer had seen on a Spectrum, and gave it 92%. Looking back two years later, the magazine said the label's arrival "smacked of corporation tactics and echoed the music industry", with a parent company running separate labels, and that "no-one was quite clear what Electric Dreams would do that was different from Activision".

The film licence came next. Activision had made *Ghostbusters*, and *Your Computer* reported that its good fortune there had made Universal Pictures look kindly on its request for *Back to the Future*. Mark Eyles, another Quicksilva veteran, designed it. He told *Sinclair User* in 1986 that "it was a bit of a rush. We knocked it out in about two months flat."

Not everything worked. When *Spindizzy* reached *CRASH* in June 1986, one reviewer greeted it with relief: "Electric Dreams have finally put out a good Spectrum product. *Spindizzy* almost makes up for the crime of releasing *Winter Sports*." *Amtix!* said much the same about the Amstrad version.

## Spindizzy

*Spindizzy* was written by Paul Shirley, with Phil Churchyard credited on the Spectrum version. The player is a cartographer sent to map Hangworld, a landscape of ramps, lifts, catwalks and trampolines floating in space, in a craft called GERALD that can switch between three shapes. Energy runs down all the time, and falling off an edge costs more of it; crystals scattered across the 385 screens buy more time. The view is isometric and can be rotated to four angles, and the craft slides and overshoots with real momentum. *CRASH*'s reviewers compared it to Atari's *[Marble Madness](/vault/games/marble-madness)* and to Ultimate's isometric games. *ZZAP!64*'s preview reported that Shirley had shown the Amstrad original to Cousens, who was impressed enough to sign it straight away.

It was reviewed first on the Amstrad:

*Amtix!* thought it would "probably be used as a yardstick for future Amstrad releases", and its readers voted it the best game of 1986 and gave it their State of the Art award. *ACE* was still recommending it in 1988, noting that it had taken a year to develop, and put the complete game on its cover cassette that summer.

## Aliens, twice

Activision's connections won Electric Dreams the licence for James Cameron's *Aliens*, and the label ended up publishing two different games of it. Eyles designed the British one, in which the player watches the colony through the helmet cameras of Ripley's squad and switches between them; *CRASH* gave it 84% in February 1987. Activision's American game, a sequence of scenes from the film, followed on the Spectrum as *Aliens (US Version)* and scored 45%. The [Aliens entry](/vault/games/aliens) tells both stories.

By January 1987 *Your Computer* counted Electric Dreams among "the top eight U.K. software houses". Cousens was now running all of Activision's British and European business, and a development centre called Software Studios, set up in April 1986 under Jon Dean and Dave Cummins, was producing Electric Dreams and Activision games for every market outside the United States. [Charles Cecil](/vault/people/charles-cecil) later led it.

## Arcade conversions

From the end of 1986 the label turned to arcade licences. *[Tempest](/vault/games/tempest)* came first, written for the Amstrad and then the Spectrum; *CRASH* gave the Spectrum version 62%, and *Amtix!* readers voted it the third-best shoot 'em up of 1986. Atari's *Super Sprint* followed in 1987, and Sega's *Super Hang-On* at the turn of 1988. Its advertisements sent mail orders to Activision (UK) Ltd in Wellingborough and carried the line "Electric Dreams Software. Authorised User" under Sega's copyright. *Sinclair User* gave the Spectrum *Super Hang-On* 10 out of 10 and called it the best road race on the machine, "and that includes Out Run". Data East's *Karnov* reached *CRASH* in May 1988 at £9.99.

The biggest was Irem's *[R-Type](/vault/games/r-type)*. Activision advertised it for the Commodore 64, Spectrum, Amstrad, Atari ST and Amiga under the slogan "It's behind you", and defended the licence hard enough that U.S. Gold withdrew *Katakis*. [Bob Pape](/vault/people/bob-pape)'s Spectrum version took 92% from *CRASH* at Christmas 1988.

## Absorbed into Activision

In 1988 Activision renamed itself Mediagenic, and *ST Action* visited its London offices in Hampstead that September to meet Cousens, now head of Mediagenic Europe, whose "UK interest" included Electric Dreams. In May 1989 *CRASH* reported that Activision had "turned Electric Dreams into a label for all their original products". It did not last much longer. *Retro Gamer* found that the label "quietly disappeared late in 1989". Cousens explained that it had never taken hold in the United States, and that as Activision's international head he had "merged the resources and concentrated on the main brand".

Cousens stayed in the industry. *Retro Gamer* recorded that he became Activision's international president, moved to Acclaim in 1991 and became its chief executive in 2003, and was appointed chief executive of [Codemasters](/vault/companies/codemasters) in 2005; he was awarded the CBE in 2010 for services to the industry. In his view, Electric Dreams had let Activision, "who had a US-skewed product slate", stay relevant in Europe, and had built a pool of local developers with a global distributor behind them.
