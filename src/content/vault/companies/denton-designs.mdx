## Overview
Denton Designs were five people who came out of the largest failure in British
games publishing and carried on. *CRASH* profiled them in June 1985 and found
them in **Rodney Street, Liverpool** — the city's medical district — where the
magazine noted they were sometimes addressed as "Denture Designs" by medical
supply firms who could not believe a software house had set up there.

## Fast facts
- **Incorporated:** September 1984.
- **The founding five:** John Gibson, Karen Davies, Steve Cain, Graham Everitt and Ally Noble.
- **Previously:** all at [Imagine](/vault/companies/imagine-software), where they worked on the Megagames.
- **Structure:** a co-operative — everyone listed as "Director" on the business cards.
- **Model:** a facilities house, not a publisher.

## Out of the Imagine collapse
Imagine went under on **9 July 1984**, and the crew who became Denton were made
redundant into the middle of the receivership argument over *Bandersnatch* —
one of the two Megagames that had been sold to the press for a year. *CRASH*
records the Receiver's difficulty believing how little existed to show for it:
barely one disk, with most of the storyline and design still in the heads of
the people who had just been let go. *Bandersnatch* and Eugene Evans went to
Fireiron; the rest planned for a few weeks and incorporated.

Their first significant capital purchase was the **Sage development systems
Imagine had used** — the machines that cross-compiled code down into the target
micro. The tooling outlived the company that bought it.

Karen Davies described the start plainly to *CRASH*: they sat down and rang
round the major software houses offering their services, and found the response
"invariably favourable" — businesspeople wary of the Imagine name, but
programmers and artists who had heard of them through it.

## A facilities house, run flat
Denton deliberately did not publish. They sold the whole range — straight
conversions, game design, programming, package design, or all of it together —
to whoever commissioned the work. That is why their catalogue appears under
other people's labels: [*Shadowfire*](/vault/games/shadowfire) and *Enigma
Force* for Beyond, *Gift from the Gods* and *Frankie Goes to Hollywood* for
Ocean, later *The Great Escape* and *Where Time Stood Still*.

The internal structure was as deliberate. Davies told *CRASH* that nobody was
anybody's boss, that they were not frightened to criticise each other's work,
and that there were no prima donnas — which the equal "Director" ranking on
every business card made structurally true rather than aspirational.

## What they were known for
*Shadowfire* (1985) is the one that made the case: a real-time squad game driven
entirely by icons, with no text parser at all, which took **96%** from *CRASH*
and 10/10 from *Personal Computer News*. *Frankie Goes to Hollywood* followed
for Ocean at £9.95 in 1985, again credited to Denton Designs as a whole rather
than to individuals.

*CRASH* readers placed Denton **third** in the State of the Art category of its
1985 awards — an unusual result for a studio whose name never appeared on a box
spine as publisher.
