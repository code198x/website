## Overview

Level 9 was a family business. The games were the Austin brothers — **Mike, Nick and Pete** — writing text adventures that rivalled Infocom on hardware Infocom did not bother with; *CRASH* visited in February 1988 and described the company as "the offices, workshops and homes of the Austin brothers", which is a fair summary of the scale of it.

**Margaret Austin** ran the advertising. *Amtix* met her on the patio in August 1986 and describes her as "the lady responsible for Level 9's advertising", over from **Reading**, where her office was; she is also the name trade listings give as Level 9's contact.

Their compression techniques fit sophisticated games into 32K and 48K machines, while their parser understood genuinely complex sentences.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1981, Berkshire, UK.
- **Speciality:** text adventures with advanced compression.
- **Innovations:** complex parser, multi-platform simultaneous release.
- **Output:** approximately 20 adventure games.

## The house
*Amtix* visited in August 1986 and the description is the best single account
of what this company physically was. A narrow, pitted road up a hillside to a
house whose gleam in spring sunlight the writer could picture as
something colder on a foggy winter night — the source, he suggests, of Austin's
"chilling descriptive prose".

Inside, "what would normally be a living room was filled with computers of all
descriptions", from an IBM PC and an Atari ST to a Commodore 128D driving a
26-inch monitor. Off it was **Pete Austin's office**, with a heavily expanded
BBC system on the desk used as a development machine running CP/M, and shelves
of folders along one wall.

A living room of computers, an advertising office thirty miles away in Reading,
and a catalogue that outsold most of the industry.

## Where they worked
The press was consistently taken with the domestic scale of the operation.
*CRASH* placed them in August 1986 in "their woodland retreat that nestles in
the Mendips"; *Sinclair User* in February 1987 described the company as run by
the Austin family "from a decaying mansion". By February 1988 *CRASH* reported
four Level 9 titles on sale in the United States, and the brothers worrying
that Welsh and Gaelic names in their games were hard for Americans to pronounce.

## Technical achievements

### Text compression

Level 9's A-code compression achieved remarkable results:
- Full games in 32K or 48K
- Text compression ratios rivalling or exceeding Infocom
- Multiple platform support from single source

### Parser sophistication

Their parser handled:
- Full sentence input
- Multiple commands
- Pronouns and context
- Complex preposition phrases

### Multi-format releases

Games released simultaneously on:
- ZX Spectrum (48K/128K)
- BBC Micro
- Commodore 64
- Amstrad CPC
- Later: 16-bit versions

## A-Code system

Pete Austin's A-code virtual machine:
- Bytecode interpreted adventure engine
- Portable across platforms
- Efficient memory use
- Supported graphics in later versions

## Partnership with [Rainbird](/vault/companies/rainbird) — and the end of it

Later releases through Rainbird/Telecomsoft brought better packaging and presentation, wider
distribution, and graphics in adventures like *Knight Orc*.

It did not last. *ACE*, March 1988:

> **Level 9 have severed relations with Rainbird Software** and have tied up an **exclusive
> distribution deal with a new label called Mandarin**. The label has been established by
> **Europress International** (owners of Database Software, publishers of *Mini Office*) and
> is being run by **Chris Payne**, who old-timers may remember used to work for MicroPower.

The stated reason is a grievance about delay. *Time and Magik* would now go out through
Mandarin "instead of Rainbird" because, as the column's readers knew, "**the product has been
endlessly delayed under**" the old arrangement. *Lancelot* followed on the new label.

So the two best-regarded British adventure houses had opposite experiences of the same
publisher. [Magnetic Scrolls](/vault/companies/magnetic-scrolls) stayed with Telecomsoft
until British Telecom sold the whole operation; Level 9 walked in early 1988 and went to a
magazine publisher's software arm instead.

**Where that label went next matters more than Level 9's own remaining years.** Two years
after signing Level 9, Mandarin published **[AMOS](/vault/tools/amos)** — François Lionet's
BASIC-derived game-creation language for the Amiga, and the tool a large share of British
Amiga owners learned to make games with. The imprint set up to distribute *Time and Magik*
became the imprint that put game development in the hands of anyone who could type.

There is a neat symmetry in it. Level 9 left a publisher over delays in shipping an adventure
built on Pete Austin's in-house A-code virtual machine; the label they left for went on to
sell the general-purpose equivalent to the public.

## Hand-drawn, on purpose

A small technical decision worth recording, because it runs against the direction the market
was moving. Where rivals were moving to digitised images, *ACE* reports that Level 9 stuck
with **hand-drawn art**, on the grounds that it "**works better for interior locations**" —
and the magazine's own view was blunt: against Magnetic Scrolls, "there's no denying that the
company have some catching up to do".

## What the critics said when they were unkind

The score table above is uniformly excellent, and it is worth setting one dissent beside it.
*Amstrad Action*, February 1987, reviewing the *Jewels of Darkness* trilogy, was unimpressed
by both the graphics and Rainbird's press release, which had promised multi-tasking:

> These adventures are **already beginning to look long in the tooth. They are programmed
> according to yesterday's rules.**

The same review is scathing about the claimed "multi-tasking" — which amounted to the picture
drawing while you typed — and notes that by 1987 even [GAC](/vault/tools/graphic-adventure-creator)
offered a large vocabulary and multi-command sentences. Level 9's technical lead, real in
1984, was being caught by off-the-shelf authoring tools within three years.

## Pete Austin predicts the end

The most valuable Level 9 document in this library is not a review. It is
**Pete Austin** writing to *ACE*'s conference pages in May 1989,
two years before his company closed, forecasting the collapse of the genre he led:

> **Text adventures are going to keep on selling, but the real growth area is fantasy
> role-playing rather than 'pure' adventures. Graphics are going to oust the text-only games
> from the market. I don't necessarily think that's a good thing** — … On the larger machines,
> there can no longer be any conflict between text, puzzles, and graphics.

He was right on all three counts, and he was not in denial about it: the same message reports
that Level 9's "own **animated adventure system** [is] almost finished" and that he had "been
looking at the other RPGs currently available".

"Declining sales led to closure in 1991" is true, and passive. Level 9 saw it coming, said
so publicly, and was building for it.

## End of an era

Declining adventure game sales led to closure in 1991. The family had created some of Britain's finest interactive fiction on hardware others dismissed as incapable.

## The scores, and the two scales

Level 9's *CRASH* record needs a caution before it can be read at all.

| Game | Score | Issue | Scale |
|---|---|---|---|
| *The Price of Magik* | **94%** | 30 | percentage |
| *The Secret Diary of Adrian Mole* (Mosaic/Level 9) | **90%** | 30 | percentage |
| *Jewels of Darkness* (Rainbird compilation) | **94%** | 36 | percentage |
| *The Worm in Paradise* | **9/10** | 5 | out of ten |
| *Snowball* | **9/10** | 6 | out of ten |
| *Return to Eden* | **8/10** | 11 | out of ten |

⚠ **These are two different scoring systems and they are not interchangeable.** *CRASH*
rated main-review games as percentages and adventures in its **Adventure Trail** column out
of ten. A 9/10 in the Adventure Trail is not 90%, and converting between them — in either
direction — produces a number the magazine never printed.

This matters more for Level 9 than for almost any other company in the Vault, because most
of its catalogue was reviewed on the ten-point scale. Any claim that Level 9 "averaged" some
percentage is arithmetic performed on two incompatible units.

## Legacy

Level 9 proved that:
- Sophisticated adventures could run on cheap hardware
- British developers could match American quality
- Good compression solved "impossible" technical challenges
