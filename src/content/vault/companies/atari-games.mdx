"Our claim always was that we're the real Atari — the original Atari," Dan Van Elderen of Atari Games told the journalist David Sheff. He had a case. When Warner Communications broke up Atari in 1984, the name and the home computers went to [Jack Tramiel](/vault/people/jack-tramiel), but the engineers who had built Atari's coin-ops stayed behind in the arcade division. As Atari Games they went on to make *[Gauntlet](/vault/games/gauntlet)*, *[Paperboy](/vault/games/paperboy)* and *[Hard Drivin'](/vault/games/hard-drivin)*. They also spent the end of the decade in court with [Nintendo](/vault/companies/nintendo), after their Tengen label copied the chip that locked other publishers out of the NES.

## Fast facts

- **Formed:** 1984, when Warner sold Atari's home business to Tramiel and kept the coin-op division.
- **Owners:** [Namco](/vault/companies/namco) from 1985; Warner, Hide Nakajima and a group of employees from 1987; WMS Industries, the parent of [Williams](/vault/companies/williams-electronics) and [Midway](/vault/companies/midway), from 1996.
- **Headquarters:** Milpitas, California.
- **Arcade games:** *[Marble Madness](/vault/games/marble-madness)*, *[Gauntlet](/vault/games/gauntlet)*, *[Paperboy](/vault/games/paperboy)*, *[Hard Drivin'](/vault/games/hard-drivin)*, *S.T.U.N. Runner*, *Klax*, *Primal Rage*, *Area 51*.
- **Home label:** Tengen, set up in 1987 because Atari Games could not sell home games under the Atari name.
- **End:** renamed Midway Games West in 1999; closed in 2003.

## Out of the split

In July 1984 Warner sold Atari's home computer and video game business to Tramiel and kept the coin-operated game division, *Electronic Games* reported. Tramiel's company became Atari Corporation, and the two Ataris, in Van Elderen's words, were "both of which don't like each other". The [Atari](/vault/companies/atari) entry tells the story of the split.

Warner did not keep the arcade company for long. Sheff tells how Masaya Nakamura of Namco, which had run Atari's business in Japan, had already told his American manager, Hide Nakajima, that he wanted to buy Atari. Nakajima knew the Warner executives and got Atari Games for Namco for "a little over $10 million", while Warner kept 40 percent. Namco got talented engineers, a factory in Ireland, some 230 staff and a company that was losing money. "We were buying the potential," said Dennis Wood, Namco America's lawyer. *Gauntlet* helped put the company back in shape. Nakamura and Nakajima argued over how to run it, and in 1987 Namco sold out to Warner, Nakajima and a group of employees. According to Sheff, Warner held roughly 80 percent and Nakajima and the employees 20 percent, and Nakajima left Namco's board to run Atari Games from its headquarters in Milpitas.

## The coin-ops

*Gauntlet* (1985) let up to four players explore a dungeon together, and in Britain it became a home computer hit through [U.S. Gold](/vault/companies/us-gold), which signed it along with *720°*. *Computer and Video Games* readers made U.S. Gold's *Gauntlet* their Game of the Year for 1986. *Paperboy* went to [Elite Systems](/vault/companies/elite-systems), whose compilation adverts carried the line "Paperboy 1984 Atari Games, Inc." Not every licence was a bargain. Elite's Steve Wilcox recalled that one of Atari's agents offered his company *720°* for £50,000 up front, and Elite walked away.

*Hard Drivin'* (1989) was a driving game drawn in solid polygons, built around how the car handled. *ACE* noted in January 1990 that Tengen had "concentrated their efforts on making the car handle like a true racing simulation", and that it looked "very simple — basic geometric line drawings and blocky angular shaped vehicles on the road". Squeezing it into home computers was hard work. For Domark's conversions, Mike Day spent his first month optimising the polygon routines for the Spectrum version, which ran at five frames a second; Jürgen Friedrich, who wrote the Atari ST version, said the 16-bit versions ran at eight to ten frames a second, with more than 150 objects on screen. Friedrich expected to write the 16-bit versions of *S.T.U.N. Runner* next.

In the mid-1990s its arcade games included the fighting game *Primal Rage* and the light-gun game *Area 51*.

## Tengen and Nintendo

Because Warner's agreement with Tramiel stopped Atari Games from selling home video games under the Atari name, Nakajima, Wood, Van Elderen and Randy Broweleit set up a new company in 1987 to make games for the NES. Nakajima named it Tengen, after the centre point of a go board. Nintendo accepted it as a licensee, and the licence was signed in January 1988. Tengen's first NES games, shown at the 1988 Consumer Electronics Show, were *Pac-Man*, *RBI Baseball* and *Gauntlet*.

Nintendo's licence let it decide how many cartridges each publisher could have made, and in 1988, with chips in short supply, Tengen received a fraction of its orders. By then Atari Games was already working on a way round Nintendo's lockout chip. By August 1988 it was making NES cartridges with its own chip, which its engineers called the Rabbit. On 12 December 1988 Atari Games sued Nintendo, accusing it of monopolising the market, and announced that it had built a key to "unlock the lock-out system". Wood asked in a press release, "Who gave Arakawa, Lincoln, and Main the power to decide what software the American public can buy?" *Compute!'s Gazette* explained the case to its readers in April 1990: Nintendo cartridges had to contain a proprietary chip that only Nintendo made, and Tengen had found a way round it.

Nintendo countersued. In April 1989 *Amazing Computing* reported that it had filed a patent suit against Atari Games over "those cartridge clones that Atari Games is now manufacturing". The courts found that Atari Games had not simply reverse-engineered the chip. Its lawyers had obtained a copy of Nintendo's lockout program, the 10NES, from the US Copyright Office by stating that it was needed for a lawsuit, when no suit had been filed. "Atari lied to the Copyright Office in order to obtain the copyrighted 10NES program," Judge Fern Smith wrote when she granted Nintendo a preliminary injunction in March 1991, and in 1992 the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit upheld it. The two companies settled in 1994.

*[Tetris](/vault/games/tetris)* was a second front. Atari Games held NES rights to the game through Mirrorsoft, but in March 1989 Nintendo signed for the console rights with the Soviet agency Elorg, and after Tengen shipped its NES *Tetris* that May, a preliminary injunction stopped it in June. *ACE* reported in January 1990 that Nintendo had "just won its long court battle with Tengen" over the game, and that "there is still another $100 million lawsuit between the two companies". *ACE* described that suit as one over alleged patent infringement; Sheff gives $100 million as the damages Atari Games claimed in its monopoly suit of December 1988. The [Tetris legal battles](/vault/phenomena/tetris-legal-battles) entry tells the full story.

While it fought Nintendo, Tengen was one of the few publishers supporting Atari Corporation's handheld. In October 1990 *ACE* found software for the [Lynx](/vault/systems/atari-lynx) "very scarce" and reported that "Tengen is the only software developer really supporting the Lynx", with conversions of *Paperboy*, *Klax* and *Vindicators* due before Christmas.

## Time Warner, WMS and Midway

Time Warner took a controlling interest in Atari Games in 1994 and folded Tengen into its new Time Warner Interactive. In 1996 WMS Industries bought Atari Games. *GamePro* described it that June as "the arcade division of Time Warner Interactive" and "the group that created *Primal Rage* and *Area 51*". Two years later WMS spun off Midway as an independent company.

In November 1999 Atari Games was renamed Midway Games West. Some of its people had been there since the 1970s: *Edge* reported in December 2002 that [Ed Logg](/vault/people/ed-logg), whose career went back to *Asteroids*, still worked with "the same group of developers from the late '70s — now known as Midway Games West", and was finishing *Dr Muto* for the PlayStation 2, Xbox and GameCube. Midway closed the studio in February 2003. "Both Atari Games and the Midway Games West studio that inherited the company have closed their doors," *Edge* wrote in 2005, as Midway's San Diego studio remade *Gauntlet* as *Gauntlet: Seven Sorrows*.

The games outlived the company. In 2005 Elite Systems was selling *Paperboy* for mobile phones under a licence from Midway Games West. When Midway went bankrupt in 2009, most of its assets, the Atari Games catalogue among them, were sold to Warner Bros.
