Martech started on a living-room carpet near Eastbourne. David Martin taught chemistry by day and designed board games in his spare time, and when Sinclair's computers arrived he began writing games at home and duplicating the tapes at weekends. Within a few years his company was paying a stunt rider, a judoka, a strongman and Samantha Fox to put their names on its cassettes. "Obviously, a licence helps with marketing," Martin told *CRASH* in 1986, "but we find that a licence often helps to prompt or develop a game idea." Its highest-scoring game in *CRASH* came from a licence too, but not a famous person's: *Zoids*, based on a range of toy robots that Martin had been building on the floor with his children.

## Fast facts

- **Founded by:** David Martin, with his brother-in-law John Barry, a solicitor, as partner; the company traded as Martech for Software Communications Ltd.
- **Based:** 9 Dillingburgh Road, Eastbourne (1983); then Martech House, Bay Terrace, Pevensey Bay, East Sussex, above Barry's legal practice.
- **Formats:** Spectrum, Commodore 64 and Amstrad CPC, with conversions for the BBC Micro, Electron and MSX; the Amstrad PCW, Amiga and PC from 1988.
- **Licensed names:** Eddie Kidd, Brian Jacks, Geoff Capes, *Zoids*, Samantha Fox, Heather Couper's *The Planets*, *Tarzan*, *2000 AD*'s *Nemesis the Warlock* and *Sláine*, and Nigel Mansell.
- **Without a licence:** *Crazy Comets*, *Mega Apocalypse*, *W.A.R.*, *The Armageddon Man*, *Catch 23*, *Vixen*, *Hellfire Attack*.

## Board games first

Martin's first idea was to use the computer to referee board games. Martech's early releases were *Conflict* and *Galaxy Conflict* for the Spectrum, Commodore 64 and BBC Micro, *Quest of Merravid* for the Commodore 64 and VIC-20, and *Gisburn's Castle* for the BBC. Computer-moderated board games did not last. "One game, for instance, had 140 counters," Martin said, "and they all took up a disproportionate amount of shelf space in the shops." By then the business had outgrown his home. "We got to the point where we were manufacturing all weekend, and one day, having just manufactured ten games in a morning and just received our first large order for WH Smiths, we realised we were going to have to sub-contract." He gave up teaching for the software business full time.

## Buying a name

Martech's first licence was *Eddie Kidd Jump Challenge* (1984). "We were looking for an exciting personality to endorse a motorbike game," Martin said. "Eddie is the world record holder for motorbike jumps, and we approached him, signed a licensing deal and then developed the game with his help." Kidd appeared on the Martech stand at the PCW Show in London to promote it. *CRASH* found it more simulation than game and worried that players would "soon tire" of it, but the name sold. *Acorn User*'s BBC and Electron chart had it at number 10 in January 1985 and at number 7 in February and again in April, among *Elite*, *Sabre Wulf* and *Jet Pac*.

More names followed: the judoka Brian Jacks for *Brian Jacks Superstar Challenge*, a decathlon-style game; the strongman Geoff Capes for *Geoff Capes Strongman*, an icon-driven athletics game, which *CRASH* gave 78%; and Jacks again, as "technical consultant" on the judo game *Uchi Mata* (1986). *The Planets* (1986), 85% in *CRASH*, was a space game built around the astronomer Heather Couper's Channel 4 series and book. Martin said Martech had been "looking for a space game and then found the licensing angle".

*Sam Fox Strip Poker* (1986) used a different kind of fame. Martin said the poker game "had been on Martech's shelves for a while" before the tie-in with the model, and called the Fox element "just meant as a bit of fun". *CRASH* reported that one computer magazine refused to carry its advertising. *Vixen* (1988), a platform game with a whip-wielding heroine, was advertised with the model Corinne Russell; *CRASH*'s preview expected it "to reach new depths of sexism in software", and readers wrote in to complain about the advertisements.

A famous name did not make a good game. *Uchi Mata*, with Brian Jacks's endorsement, scored 36%. When Alternative Software reissued it on budget, its boss Roger Hulley renamed it *Judo Uchi Mata*: "The name didn't click so we put the sport first."

## Zoids

*Zoids* began with a shopping trip. Martin, *CRASH* reported, "had come back from a shopping expedition, having bought some toys and was on the carpet with his youngsters assembling and playing with a couple of Zoids". "They were great," he said. "On the Monday I got in touch with Tomy who make Zoids, and eventually a deal was tied up." Martech hired the Electronic Pencil Company to write it, and the Commodore 64 version had music by [Rob Hubbard](/vault/people/rob-hubbard). The player steers a pilot across a planet of hostile robot Zoids, gathering the scattered parts of a giant one, Zoidzilla.

*CRASH* gave it a Smash at 96% in February 1986. Looking back two years later, the magazine said it "made mincemeat of the notion that a licence from something as childish as a series of toy monsters means a thinly-disguised piece of marketing schlock. *Zoids* is Martech's monument for posterity." *Amtix*, looking back over 1986, recalled that "Accolade numero uno went to Martech for their excellent *Zoids*".

## The games underneath

Martech's unlicensed games could be good too. *Crazy Comets* (1985) for the Commodore 64 was a shoot-'em-up that *ZZAP!64* traced to an obscure arcade game, *Mad Planets*, with "yet another classic Rob Hubbard creation" on the soundtrack. *Mega Apocalypse* also had a Hubbard theme. The company's way of working was to keep its programmers in-house. In 1986 *CRASH* found two teams of about half a dozen programmers each, in Brighton and Portsmouth, meeting at the Pevensey Bay office. Ian McArdle, who wrote *Eddie Kidd*, was in the Brighton team.

The biggest of the later licences was *Nigel Mansell's Grand Prix* (1988), programmed by Dave Looker. *CRASH* gave the Spectrum version 72% at £9.99. One reviewer called it "the best race simulator (as opposed to *Enduro Racer*-style race game) on the market today", and another found it "a very cheap clone" of Firebird's *Revs*.

## The end

Martech moved onto the 16-bit machines and the Amstrad PCW in 1988. Its last new releases came in early 1989, among them *Hellfire Attack*, which *ZZAP!64* scored 15%, and *Phantom Fighter* for the Amiga. Later in 1989 Martin's games began appearing under a new label, Screen 7, among them a game of the film *Jaws*. By 1991 Martin was buying licences for [Gremlin Graphics](/vault/companies/gremlin-graphics); Gremlin's Ian Stewart told *Amiga Power* that "David Martin who used to run Martech/Screen 7 handles most of our licence acquisition".
