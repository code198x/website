## Overview

Most British publishers of the eighties sold games on the strength of the game. Martech sold them on the strength of **whose name was on the box** — a stunt rider, a shot putter, a judoka, a Formula One driver — and did it consistently enough that the approach is the company's defining feature, not an occasional tactic.

## Fast facts

- **Press evidence in this library:** reviewed in *CRASH*, *ZZAP!64* and *Amstrad Action* from **1984 to 1989**, mostly at **£7.95–£9.99**.
- **Licensed names:** Eddie Kidd, Geoff Capes, Brian Jacks, Nigel Mansell, Samantha Fox, Tarzan, and 2000AD's Nemesis the Warlock and Sláine.
- **Unlicensed too:** *Crazy Comets* and *Mega Apocalypse*, and *Tomahawk* — the games that get remembered on their own terms.

## Buying an audience

*Eddie Kidd Jump Challenge* set the pattern in 1984. *CRASH* reported that the stunt rider — then holder of a 190-foot world record — had "been closely involved with the game's programmers for several months", and that he would be **making personal appearances on the Martech stand at computer fairs**. The licence was not just a name printed on a cassette inlay; the man came with it.

What followed reads like a roster: *Geoff Capes Strongman* (1985), endorsed by the Olympic shot putter and World's Strongest Man; *Brian Jacks Superstar Challenge*; *Uchi Mata* (1986), a judo game with **Brian Jacks credited as technical consultant**; *Nigel Mansell's Grand Prix* (1988), reviewed at £9.99 and programmed by Dave Looker, which *CRASH* called "undoubtedly the best race simulator on the market today". *Samantha Fox Strip Poker* and *Vixen* used a different and considerably less defensible kind of celebrity, and the advertising that went with them drew complaints at the time.

The logic is straightforward and worth understanding, because it explains a great deal of eighties British software. A game by an unknown programmer competed against forty others on a shop shelf, all in similar boxes at similar prices. **A game with a famous name on it competed against nothing** — it had its own reason to be picked up, borrowed from outside the industry entirely. Buying that name cost less than building a reputation.

## The games underneath

The licence strategy tends to obscure that Martech also published straight, unendorsed games that were good. *Crazy Comets* (1985, £10.95) and its sequel *Mega Apocalypse* are fast C64 shooters with [Rob Hubbard](/vault/people/rob-hubbard) soundtracks — among the tracks that made his name. *Tomahawk*, the Spectrum helicopter simulation, is the other title that people bring up unprompted.

By 1989 the run was over. *Hellfire Attack* took a savaging in *ZZAP!64*, and the name stops appearing.

## Why it mattered

Martech is the clearest British example of the **licence as a substitute for marketing**, a decade before the practice became routine and industrialised. The endorsements it bought were sports personalities and television faces, not film franchises, which makes the catalogue a strange and specific record of who was famous in Britain in 1985.

It also shows the limit of the approach. A licence gets a game picked up; it does not make the game good, and it does not build anything that lasts once the name stops being famous. Martech's own best-remembered releases turn out to be the ones with nobody's name on them.
