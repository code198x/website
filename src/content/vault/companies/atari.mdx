In December 1982 the American magazine *Video Games* ran a long history of Atari under the headline "from a $500 lark to a $2 billion business in 10 short years". The $500 was Nolan Bushnell's royalties from his first arcade game. The $2 billion was Atari's projected revenue for 1982, up from $165 million in 1979, and the magazine reckoned Atari was earning about two-thirds of the profits of its owner, Warner Communications. Within two years that business had lost hundreds of millions of dollars and been split in two, and its home half spent its last twelve years as an independent company under [Jack Tramiel](/vault/people/jack-tramiel), the man whose Commodore price war had helped to wreck it.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1972, by [Nolan Bushnell](/vault/people/nolan-bushnell) and Ted Dabney. *Video Games* dated the incorporation to 27 June 1972; Dabney recalled setting up in Santa Clara, California.
- **First hit:** *[Pong](/vault/games/pong)* in the arcades, 1972; a home *Pong* sold through Sears for Christmas 1975.
- **Owner from October 1976:** Warner Communications, for a reported $28 million.
- **Console:** the Video Computer System, later the [Atari 2600](/vault/systems/atari-2600), 1977, at $180.
- **Computers:** the [Atari 400 and 800](/vault/systems/atari-8-bit) from 1979, the [ST](/vault/systems/atari-st) from 1985.
- **Split, July 1984:** Warner sold the home computer and video game business to Jack Tramiel for $240 million and kept the arcade division, which became [Atari Games](/vault/companies/atari-games).
- **Atari Corporation:** the ST, the XE computers, the 7800, the [Lynx](/vault/systems/atari-lynx) (1989) and the Jaguar (1993).
- **1996:** merged with the hard drive maker JTS Corporation.

## Syzygy, then Atari

Bushnell's first game was *Computer Space*, his coin-operated version of *Spacewar!*, a computer game he had played at the University of Utah. He sold it to Nutting Associates. "We built 1,500 and had to sell some by force," Bill Nutting told *Video Games*. Bushnell then reminded Ted Dabney, a colleague from the electronics company Ampex, that they had once planned to start a company together, and they did it with Bushnell's $500 in *Computer Space* royalties.

Atari's own magazine, *Atari Connection*, asked Dabney about the name in June 1981. There had been three founders, all from Ampex: Bushnell, Dabney and Larry Bryan. Each was to put in $100, "and Bryan didn't ante up his $100". Bryan had found their first name while thumbing through a dictionary: Syzygy, "the straight-line configuration of three celestial bodies". Another California company already had it. They considered "BD, Inc." and "DB, Inc.", which were too close to Black & Decker and Dun & Bradstreet. Both men played the Japanese board game Go, so they sent the state a list of Go terms. First was *sente*; second was *atari*, which the magazine glossed as "similar meaning to the chess word 'check'". A few weeks later the incorporation papers came back: the Secretary of State's office "had selected their second choice, ATARI". If it had approved the first, the magazine noted, "you might now be reading 'THE SENTE CONNECTION.'"

## Pong

*Pong* was shown at the industry's annual trade show in Chicago in 1972. Bushnell defined it; Al Alcorn, a young engineer he knew from Ampex and Atari's first full-time engineer, built it. "He defined it, and I built it, though there were little things like the sound that I added to it," [Alcorn](/vault/people/al-alcorn) told *Video Games*. Bushnell got the credit, and joked about it: "I get the credit because I have more access to the press." The game was a hit, and every company copied it.

Three years later Atari took *Pong* home. The engineer Bob Brown asked a colleague, Harold Lee, whether the game could be put on a single chip, and Atari found a retailer in Sears, whose sporting goods buyer, Tom Quinn, took the gamble. "We went into production with the idea of selling 50,000 units," Brown said. "We ended up doing double that in the Christmas '75 season. People were waiting two hours in line to sign up on a list just to get a Pong game."

## Warner and the VCS

Success did not leave Atari with money. The cash from home *Pong* went back into stock that would not sell until Christmas, and Gene Lipkin, then in charge of marketing, recalled a list of ten companies Atari would be willing to merge with. Warner was not on it, but a contact led to a meeting: "Then boom-boom, the deal was made. I mean, it was that quick." Warner bought Atari in October 1976. *Electronic Games* and *Vidiot* both gave the price as $28 million; *Video Games* said $32 million.

The next product was the Video Computer System, code-named Stella. *Video Games* reported that it was conceived at Atari's Grass Valley think tank "primarily by Steve Meyer" (Steve Mayer) and developed at Los Gatos by engineers led by [Jay Miner](/vault/people/jay-miner), who designed its graphics chip. It was shown at the summer Consumer Electronics Show in 1977 and sold for $180, and at first it sold slowly. With Warner's money Atari built 800,000 in 1978 and was left with about 400,000 in the warehouse. At Warner's budget meeting that November, Bushnell and Warner's Manny Gerard shouted at each other for hours; Bushnell was demoted from chairman to director and soon left, and [Ray Kassar](/vault/people/ray-kassar), a former executive of Burlington Industries who had been running the consumer division since the start of the year, took charge.

The VCS took off after Atari licensed *Space Invaders* in 1980. "No company has ever grown this fast," Don Osborne of Atari's coin-op division told *Video Games* in 1982. How many consoles it sold depends on who was counting: *Video Games* put the number of American owners at "nearly 12 million" in June 1983, and *ANALOG Computing* reported "more than 25 million 2600s" sold worldwide by March 1986. Some of the programmers who wrote its games left to found [Activision](/vault/companies/activision) and sell cartridges of their own. The [2600 entry](/vault/systems/atari-2600) tells how the machine worked.

## The arcade division

Atari never stopped making coin-operated games, and they kept it going when home products did not. *Tank* carried the company through 1975, according to its designers. *Breakout* came from a young Steve Jobs, whom Bushnell paid a bonus for every chip he saved; the engineer Steve Bristow recalled Jobs bringing the design "down from 80 to 30 ICs". *Video Games* gave the division's two biggest sellers as *[Asteroids](/vault/games/asteroids)*, released on 6 November 1979, at 70,000 machines, and Donna Bailey's *[Centipede](/vault/games/centipede)* at 50,000. See [the golden age of arcade games](/vault/phenomena/golden-age-arcade).

## The crash

The boom ended in the [1983 video game crash](/vault/phenomena/1983-crash). Too many consoles and cartridges met falling demand, and Atari's own big releases disappointed: the 2600 *[Pac-Man](/vault/games/pac-man-atari-2600)*, and *[E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial](/vault/games/et-the-extra-terrestrial)*, which its programmer, [Howard Scott Warshaw](/vault/people/howard-scott-warshaw), had five weeks to write. Atari lost $539 million in 1983. That September, the *New York Times* reported, it dumped 14 truckloads of surplus cartridges and equipment in a landfill at Alamogordo, New Mexico.

## Two Ataris

In July 1984 Warner sold Atari's home computer and video game divisions, "representing over 75% of Atari's total sales", to Jack Tramiel for $240 million. *Electronic Games* reported that the sale left Warner with a pre-tax loss of $425 million for the quarter, and that Warner was keeping Atari's coin-operated game division and Ataritel, a home communications venture.

*ANALOG Computing*, a magazine for Atari computer owners, had the news just as it went to press:

> Warner Communications had sold its loss-plagued Atari division to Jack Tramiel, former head of Commodore. Thus ended months of half-baked speculation and rampant rumors about the fate of Atari, once the fastest growing company in history.

The same editorial reminded readers that "Commodore's participation in the bloody price wars of 1982 and 1983 severely damaged Atari".

Tramiel's company became Atari Corporation. The arcade business became Atari Games, and the two did not get on. "Our claim always was that we're the real Atari — the original Atari," Dan Van Elderen of Atari Games told the journalist David Sheff. Sheff reports that Warner sold control of Atari Games to the Japanese arcade company [Namco](/vault/companies/namco) for a little over $10 million, and that in 1987 Namco sold it on to Time Warner and a group led by Hide Nakajima, who had run Namco's American subsidiary. Because the agreement with Tramiel stopped Atari Games from selling home video games under the Atari name, it set up a new label, Tengen, to publish games for the Nintendo Entertainment System.

## The Tramiel years

Tramiel's first new machine was the [ST](/vault/systems/atari-st), shown at the Consumer Electronics Show in January 1985 under the slogan "Power without the Price". Wags in Las Vegas, *Personal Computer News* reported, called it the "Jackintosh". Germany became its largest market, and its built-in MIDI ports took it into music studios.

The console Atari had been about to launch fared worse. *K-Power* reported in 1984 that Atari had "recently introduced" the 7800 ProSystem. *Amazing Computing* looked back in 1988: the 7800, "which could have been the rage of 1985, instead fell by the wayside during the Warner Communications/Tramiel changeover. By the time the 7800 wobbled out of the warehouse two years later, Japan's Nintendo captured the new generation Atari had ignored."

Another machine got away. Under Warner, Atari had put money into Amiga, a start-up that Jay Miner had joined, and hoped to license its chipset. Commodore bought Amiga instead, and Tramiel's Atari took Amiga to court. *Amazing Computing* reported in June 1987 that the suit had been dismissed on "mutually satisfactory terms", and that the Amiga engineers kept a sign reading "Crashin' tosh" beside an Atari ST in their office.

In 1989 came the [Lynx](/vault/systems/atari-lynx), a hand-held games console with a colour screen. "The kids just go crazy over the Lynx," Sam Tramiel, Atari's president, told *ST Format* that September, and of the next five years: "We'll dominate the hand-held market and kill off Nintendo!" By Christmas British mail-order dealers were taking orders at £199.90 with *California Games*.

The last console was the Jaguar. *Atari ST User* reported in September 1993 that IBM would manufacture it in North Carolina, and in November that Atari was promoting it as a 64-bit RISC machine with 16.7 million colours. It was launched in New York that autumn at $249, to be rolled out in New York and San Francisco first. Atari's share price, below $1 a share early in 1993, had reached $11 5/8 by Christmas. In February 1994 Atari claimed 40,000 Jaguars delivered in the United States; the first shipment to Britain, according to one insider quoted by the magazine, was "embarrassingly small".

## JTS

In April 1996 *ST Format* reported that Atari had merged with JTS Corporation, a maker of hard drives, after months of rumours of liquidation and job cuts. The merger followed a reported $25 million investment by Atari in JTS. "Atari will cease to exist as a stand-alone company, but the name Atari will survive as a brand name," the magazine wrote, and the Jaguar's future was "clouded in uncertainty". The following month it reported that the arcade company, Atari Games, had been sold to WMS Industries, the owner of the Williams, Bally and Midway names. According to *Retro Gamer*, Jack Tramiel came back to oversee the JTS merger and then retired.
