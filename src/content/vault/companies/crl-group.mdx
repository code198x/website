## Overview

CRL was a London software house with a catalogue broad enough to include arcade games, a licensed comic tie-in and a Ninja Hamster — but it is remembered for something narrower and stranger: it put **film certificates on computer games**, years before anyone was required to.

## Fast facts

- **Based:** London.
- **Seen in the press:** reviewed in *CRASH*, *ZZAP!64* and *Amstrad Action* from **1985 to 1988**, at **£7.95–£9.95**, with budget re-releases at **£1.50**.
- **Known for:** *Dracula*, *Frankenstein*, *Jack the Ripper*, *Tau Ceti*, *Blade Runner*, *Oink!*, *Ninja Hamster*.

## The certificate

In the mid-eighties there was no ratings system for computer games in Britain. Games were sold to children by default, and the question of whether any of them were unsuitable had barely been asked in public.

CRL submitted its horror titles to the **British Board of Film Classification** anyway, and the resulting **15 certificate** on *Dracula* became the marketing. A game rated like a video nasty was news, and news sold copies.

Whether this was principled self-regulation or an extremely effective publicity instinct is a fair question, and the honest answer is probably both. What is not in doubt is that it put the ratings question into the British games press years before the industry had to confront it — and that the games themselves, text-and-graphics horror adventures, were considerably tamer than the certificate implied.

## The rest of the catalogue

The horror titles are the hook, but CRL's output was ordinary British publishing of the period: *Tau Ceti*, a well-regarded 3D space game; *Blade Runner*, licensed — carefully — from Vangelis's score rather than the film itself; *Oink!*, tied to the comic; *Ninja Hamster*, which is what it sounds like. The press evidence shows the standard trajectory too — full-price releases in the mid-eighties, then the same titles reappearing at **£1.50** as the budget market took over.

## Why it mattered

CRL is a small company with an outsized place in one specific story: **how Britain started thinking about age ratings for games**. That conversation ran through the tabloids, the trade and eventually Parliament, and it began in part because a publisher decided a certificate would look good on a cassette inlay.
