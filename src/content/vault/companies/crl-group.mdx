At the end of 1986 a text adventure became the first computer game in Britain to carry a film certificate. CRL had sent Rod Pike's *Dracula* to the British Board of Film Classification, which gave it a 15. A year later CRL did it again with *Jack the Ripper*, and got the first 18. The company's founder, Clement Chambers, argued that he was simply obeying the law. Most of the press called it a publicity stunt. Both readings fit a company that had spent its first years looking for things people already recognised and getting them noticed.

## Fast facts

- **Founded as:** Computer Rentals Limited, 140 Whitechapel Road, London E1; later CRL Group, at CRL House, 9 Kings Yard, between Hackney, Bow and Stratford.
- **Run by:** Clement Chambers, founder and managing director, 21 years old in 1985.
- **Known for:** *Test Match* and *Derby Day*; licences including *The Magic Roundabout*, *Terrahawks* and *War of the Worlds*; [Pete Cooke](/vault/people/pete-cooke)'s *Tau Ceti* (94% in *[CRASH](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine)*) and *Academy*; Rod Pike's horror adventures.
- **Certificates:** *Dracula* (15), *Frankenstein* (15), *Jack the Ripper* (18).
- **Distributed by:** [Electronic Arts](/vault/companies/electronic-arts), by early 1988.
- **Closed:** April 1991, giving way to On-Line Entertainment.

## Computer Rentals

In October 1982 Computer Rentals Limited, of 140 Whitechapel Road in the East End, was advertising for ZX81 programs on "any subject: Games, Education, Business, Home Finance", and offering authors a royalty of £1.50 a cassette. Its early catalogue was mostly sport. Looking back over 1983 in its first issue, *CRASH* recorded that "CRL gave us *Test Match* and *Derby Day*", and *Test Match* was still in *Big K*'s best-seller lists through 1984.

*CRASH* visited in the summer of 1985 and found a company that had "strenuously avoided a 'house style'". Chambers had been written about as a teenage whizzkid in the early days of the boom. He was 21, ran a public limited company, and told the magazine that CRL had been able to go public because in the early 1980s "it was a simple matter to make a lot of money from selling computer games". The software development manager, Ian Ellery, was 23 and also drew cartoons for *[Computer & Video Games](/vault/magazines/computer-and-video-games)*. The in-house programmers were teenagers: Jeff Lee, who wrote *Rocky Horror Show*, was 18.

## Buying recognition

Through 1984 CRL went after names. In March *Acorn User* reported a game of *War of the Worlds* being developed with Jeff Wayne, and Chambers was precise about what he had bought: "It's going to be something special, based on the record rather than directly on the book." In September *Big K* reported that the "famed East End software house CRL" had acquired *The Magic Roundabout*. In October it announced an agreement with Gerry Anderson, and a *Terrahawks* game written for the 48K Spectrum by Richard Taylor, who also wrote a column for *Big K*. *Rocky Horror Show* followed in 1985 and scored 79% in *CRASH*.

*Blade Runner* shows how far the habit went. CRL could not get the rights to the film, so it licensed Vangelis's soundtrack album instead; the box called the game "a video game interpretation of the film score by Vangelis". *Commodore User*'s gossip column explained that "when CRL decided to make the game, they couldn't trace the film rights", and thought it "ironic... that the game's music should be the best aspect". *CRASH* agreed: its 58% review found that "the only thing that really stands out about this game is the tune on the title screen".

## Tau Ceti

CRL's hit came from somewhere else. Pete Cooke, a former maths teacher, answered an advertisement from Ian Ellery with a lorry-driving game, *Juggernaut* (1985), and followed it with *Tau Ceti*. Recalling its first interview with Cooke, in November 1985, *CRASH* wrote that there had seemed no doubt the game would be a success for CRL, "a long-lived software house in search of a hit game". It was a Smash at 94% that December, and its sequel *Academy* scored 92% a year later. When Ellery moved to Nexus, Cooke went with him. "I like Clement Chambers, the boss of CRL," he told *CRASH*, but the working relationship had been with Ellery.

## The certificates

*Dracula*, released at the end of 1986, was written by Rod Pike, whose earlier CRL adventure was *The Pilgrim*. It followed Bram Stoker's novel in three parts, in text with occasional pictures. *Amtix* ran the news as "THE FIRST CENSORED GAME", reporting that it was "primarily the... language of the text" that led the Board to give it a 15. *Amstrad Action* wondered how "(heaven knows how) CRL has managed to get the British Film Censors to slap a '15' certificate on the game". *CRASH*'s adventure reviewer gave it 89% and noticed "the conspicuous lack of blood and gore in the pictures that would warrant such a certificate".

Pike's *Frankenstein* also got a 15. By November 1987 *[ACE](/vault/magazines/ace-magazine)* expected CRL to submit *Jack the Ripper*, an adventure by the St Brides team written with Gilsoft's [PAW](/vault/tools/paw), "repeating the publicity stunt of their *Dracula* launch". It received an 18, the first for a computer game. *CRASH* scored it 72% and treated it as an ordinary game rather than an adventure, because its notoriety would attract people who never played adventures.

## Chambers's argument

Chambers's case rested on the Video Recordings Act 1984, passed during the "video nasties" panic. The Act defined a video work as "any series of visual images" stored on disc or tape. Games were exempt unless, to any significant extent, they showed sexual activity or gross violence. In Chambers's reading, a horror adventure with digitised pictures had to be classified, and in February 1988 *ACE* agreed that, read that way, many violent games on sale should have been submitted. Only CRL had done it: the Board had classified three games, all from CRL, which sent them on videotape with a printout of the text.

Chambers told *ACE* that he felt he had been "the victim of a backlash from other figures in the software industry, because *Jack the Ripper* has brought attention to people breaking the law". Asked whether the game had needed a certificate at all, he answered: "I'm not a solicitor."

The next title tested the approach. In March 1988 *ACE* reported a new Rod Pike werewolf adventure that it "can't even bring ourselves to name", with what CRL's public relations agency called "eroticism" and "no nudity". The news put Headlines, CRL's PR agency, "in rather a tight spot": it was anxious to dissociate CRL from any hint of trouble. *ACE* also asked what Electronic Arts, which distributed CRL's games, made of it. *Wolfman* was later reported to have been rated 18 as well.

By December 1989 nobody had followed CRL to the Board. A spokesperson quoted by *ACE* said that the certificates "probably did quite well in terms of publicity for them but we've had nothing since". Chambers still insisted that "what he did was merely follow the letter of the law", and he had a line about the genre too: "People have always said horror is exploitation and some people have taken that exploitation element and not delivered."

## After CRL

In March 1990 *ACE* found Chambers planning a hypermedia version of Dennis Wheatley's *Herewith the Clues*, a 1930s crime dossier sold as a folder of documents, photographs and exhibits in little bags. A year later CRL was gone. *The One for ST Games* reported in May 1991 that "the veteran London-based publisher finally closed up shop last month", and that a new company, On-Line Entertainment, had formed "from the ashes".

On-Line Entertainment, run by Chambers and Michael Hodges, brought modem games to Britain; *Atari ST User* described its multiplayer service in 1994, and in 1998 *[Edge](/vault/magazines/edge)* called Chambers the "pioneering founder of Online Plc". He then ran the financial information website ADVFN as chief executive for twenty years, stepping down in February 2022.
