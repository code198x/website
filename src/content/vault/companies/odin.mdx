Odin Computer Graphics were a **Liverpool** house — the city that produced
[Bug-Byte](/vault/companies/bug-byte), Imagine and
[Software Projects](/vault/companies/software-projects) — publishing arcade adventures with
a distinctive drawn look, under a **Norse** house identity that extended to a second label
called **Thor**.

*Commodore Format*, in a 1992 A–Z of the departed, wrote the epitaph and could not resist
the obvious joke:

> **ODIN** — or **Thor**, as they were sometimes known. From their Valhalla came the ace
> arcade adventures… But everyone who worshipped the Norse gods after whom the company was
> named **stopped believing in them**.

## Marc Dawson, and three closures

The most useful account of Odin in this library is not British. It is a 1989 interview in
the Danish magazine *Amiga Interface*, given by **Marc Dawson** — and its value is that it
catches one programmer's whole career shape in a paragraph.

Dawson started at **Imagine** at eighteen. After Imagine collapsed he made games for what
the magazine calls "**the now closed software houses, Odin and Thor**". His own favourites
among his work were ***Hypa-ball*** on the C64, published by Odin, and ***Nodes of Yesod***
and ***Robin of the Wood*** — the two that took **93%** and **94%**.

By 1989 he had set up his own development house with another programmer, **Steve
Weatherill**, and the magazine notes drily that **three of his employers had now gone
under** — while making clear this was not held to be Dawson's doing.

He also offered the most unglamorous description of games programming in this Vault, as a
warning to readers thinking of trying it:

> The problem with programming is that **the majority of game development consists of
> boring programming**. You start with the interesting part, where you work out all your
> ideas, but the ending is very, very tedious.

That is worth keeping precisely because it is the opposite of how the period is usually
narrated. The bedroom-coder story is one of inspiration; the man who wrote a 94% game says
the inspiration is the short part.

## Why it mattered

Odin's two big games score as highly as anything in this Vault's working set, and almost
nobody outside Spectrum owners of a certain age can name the company. That gap is the entry.

It also sits in a specific place geographically. **Liverpool produced Bug-Byte, Imagine,
Software Projects and Odin inside five years**, and every one of them was gone or
diminished by the end of the decade. The city's software industry was not short of talent
or of hits; what it lacked was any company that survived its own success. Odin is the least
dramatic instance — no famous bankruptcy, no court case, no documentary — just a
well-reviewed publisher that stopped.
