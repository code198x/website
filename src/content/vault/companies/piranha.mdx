Piranha was **Macmillan's games imprint** — a book publisher of long standing deciding, in the mid-eighties, that software was another kind of publishing. It lasted about two years, and its catalogue is a near-perfect snapshot of what a licensing-minded publisher thought British teenagers wanted.

## Fast facts

- **Parent:** Macmillan Publishers.
- **Press evidence in this library:** reviewed in *CRASH*, *[ZZAP!64](/vault/magazines/zzap-64)* and *Amstrad Action* from **1986 to 1988**, at **£6.95–£8.95**, with budget re-releases at **£1.98**.
- **Known for:** *The Trap Door* and its sequel *Through the Trap Door*, *Rogue Trooper*, *The Colour of Magic*, *Nosferatu the Vampyre*, *Strike Force Cobra*, *Flunky*, *Lifeforce*.

## Licences, and where they came from

What makes the catalogue worth looking at is **where the licences were drawn from** — not arcade cabinets, which is where most publishers shopped, but the properties a book-and-media company had relationships with:

- ***The Trap Door*** — the stop-motion children's television series, and a genuinely well-liked game.
- ***Rogue Trooper*** — the *2000 AD* strip.
- ***The Colour of Magic*** — Terry Pratchett's first Discworld novel, adapted as an illustrated adventure while the series was still young.
- ***Nosferatu the Vampyre*** — Herzog's film.

That is not a games publisher's list. It is a **publisher's** list, assembled by people whose instinct was that a strong property transfers to any medium.

## Piranha sinks

*ACE* reported the end in **July 1988** under that headline, and was blunt about the cause:

> The [latest] software house to sink is **Piranha**, the label set up by book publishers
> **Macmillan** to dip the corporate toe in the games software market. While the name
> conjures up images of aggressive marketing, **Piranha never really cut it** — [hits] were
> few and far between, with the result that **the Macmillan board pulled the plug**.

The more interesting account came five months later, in *ACE*'s round-up of the year's
failures, and it is about what a closure does to the things a company was holding:

> The ravenous Piranha [had acquired] **Roy of the Rovers and a clutch of 2000AD characters
> (collected for a mere £1,000 [apiece], so the rumour has it)**. Piranha spent a lot of time
> and money developing games before parent company **Macmillan pulled the plug out of the
> aquarium, asphyxiating Piranha and leaving the licences high and dry**. So far,
> **[Gremlin](/vault/companies/gremlin-graphics) have picked up Roy**, but as for the
> rest…

⚠ The £1,000 figure is reported as rumour by the magazine and is carried here as such.

Two things worth taking from that. **British comic characters were cheap** — a thousand
pounds an item for *Roy of the Rovers* and 2000AD properties, against the sums
[Ocean](/vault/companies/ocean-software) and [US Gold](/vault/companies/us-gold) were paying
for film rights. And a licence is not an asset that survives its holder: when Macmillan
closed the label, the rights sat unusable until somebody else bought them, and most of them
never produced a game at all.

## Why it mattered

Piranha is a small, short-lived label that illustrates something structural: in the mid-eighties, British software looked lucrative enough that established media companies came in to try it — and mostly found that a games catalogue is not much like a book list.

The Macmillan experiment ended quickly, as did most of them. But the pattern recurs across the decade — [Newsfield](/vault/companies/newsfield) starting [Thalamus](/vault/companies/thalamus), Mirror Group running Mirrorsoft, Virgin and Telecom launching labels. Piranha is one of the clearest examples, because its licences make the parent company's DNA so visible.
