Mikro-Gen made the Wally Week games, Spectrum arcade adventures about an everyman called Wally Week and his family that *CRASH* scored 92%, 93%, 90% and 93% between 1984 and 1986. Then, in 1985, it tried to change the Spectrum itself. The Mikro-Plus was a plug-in interface whose ROM took over from the Spectrum's own and gave a game an extra 16K. It was sold only with a game, *Shadow of the Unicorn*, at £14.95, and the company's advertisements promised that it would beat the pirates. *CRASH*'s tips writer predicted that the price would "do more damage to sales than the pirates ever could". One of Mikro-Gen's programmers later said the game "went out there and it died", and within two years Mikro-Gen had been sold.

## Fast facts

- **Founders:** Mike Meek and Andrew Laurie, the technical director.
- **Based:** 24 Agar Crescent, Bracknell (1982); 44 The Broadway, Bracknell (1984–85); Unit 15, The Western Centre, Western Road, Bracknell (from late 1985).
- **Wally Week games:** *Automania* (1984), *Pyjamarama* (1984), *Everyone's a Wally* (1985), *Herbert's Dummy Run* (1985), *Three Weeks in Paradise* (1986).
- **Programmers included:** Chris Hinsley, [David Perry](/vault/people/david-perry), [Raffaele Cecco](/vault/people/raffaele-cecco) and Nick Jones.
- **The hardware:** the Mikro-Plus interface, 1985, sold with *Shadow of the Unicorn*.
- **The end:** sold to Creative Sparks Distribution, which was in receivership by October 1987.

## From the ZX81 to Wally Week

Mikro-Gen began in 1982 selling ZX81 programs and add-ons, among them an analogue-to-digital converter board. Meek did not pretend it was a hobby. "We started Mikro-Gen to make money," he told *Sinclair User* in 1985. He had been at the first ZX Microfair and had once run the Sinclair User Club; Laurie had been a founder member of the Sunbury Computer Club and once built a 256-byte computer with binary switches. A club member wrote *Bomber* for the Commodore PET and helped convert it to the ZX81. Until *Automania*, Meek said, most of the programming was subcontracted. Chris Hinsley joined on 1 January 1984. He was the lead programmer on *Pyjamarama* and then wrote *Everyone's a Wally*, and David Perry later said that working with Hinsley's code "changed everything for me".

*Automania* introduced Wally Week, a mechanic in a car factory; *CRASH* gave it a Smash at 88%. In *Pyjamarama* (92%) he was trapped in a dream, and *Everyone's a Wally* (93%) gave the player five characters to control. Mikro-Gen previewed *Everyone's a Wally* at the LET trade show in February 1985 with, as *Retro Gamer* remembered it, "a bloke in a large foam Wally Week suit and perhaps the most annoying song ever recorded". "We were very chuffed to see *Everyone's a Wally* knock *Alien 8* off the top," Meek told *Sinclair User*; *Alien 8* was [Ultimate](/vault/companies/ultimate)'s new game that month. Laurie was proud of the graphics. Mikro-Gen talked about "replacement graphics", which, *Sinclair User* explained, meant Wally could move behind objects in *Everyone's a Wally*, where in *Pyjamarama* "his legs and belly tended to change colour". "Every last bit of the Spectrum is used up," Laurie said.

The games sold. "Overall, *Pyjamarama* has sold best," Meek said in mid-1985, "but *Everyone's a Wally* will probably surpass it." For *Herbert's Dummy Run* (90%), in which Wally's baby son is lost in a department store, Mikro-Gen sent disposable nappies to people in the trade printed "There's a big one coming your way". *Three Weeks in Paradise* (93%), in 1986, was David Perry's.

## The Mikro-Plus

In August 1985 *CRASH* put the Mikro-Plus on its cover: "At last the 64K Spectrum! MIKRO-PLUS — 16, 48 now 64K of Program Power." Inside, the magazine explained how it worked. The interface, which looked like a joystick interface and had a joystick port built in, carried a ROM holding part of the game. When it was plugged in, that ROM replaced the Spectrum's own, and the rest of the game loaded from cassette into the normal 48K of RAM. Meek claimed a programmer could write a program 50% larger than usual. Each interface was specific to its own game, so the hardware and the game were sold together, for £14.95. The extra code also gave the game a one-command Microdrive copy and, because Meek worried about poorly aligned tape recorders, a routine for adjusting the cassette head.

Two games were planned to launch it, the shoot-'em-up *Battle of the Planets* and *Shadow of the Unicorn*, a role-playing adventure with ten characters and a book to set the scene. *Sinclair User* had heard about the book in June: Mikro-Gen had "commissioned a non-wally to write a fantasy novel on which a game will be based", and Laurie described the game as "*Lords of Midnight*-ish but animated". In the end *Shadow of the Unicorn* was the only game sold with the Mikro-Plus. David Perry later recalled that *Three Weeks in Paradise* had also been meant for the hardware, and that he was left "sitting there with a version of *Three Weeks In Paradise* far too big to fit in a normal Spectrum".

The reviews were lukewarm. *CRASH*'s adventure column found the game "far inferior in terms of basic structure" to the eighteen-month-old *[Lords of Midnight](/vault/games/lords-of-midnight)*. *Sinclair User* thought "most of the extra memory seems to be about having a really enormous playing area. The graphics themselves are not particularly special". *Your Sinclair*'s first issue nevertheless showed it new in its top ten at number five.

## "It went out there and it died"

*CRASH*'s tips writer, Robin Candy, saw the problem for his own column in December 1985:

> With the advent of Mikro Plus pokes will be a thing of the past because the EPROM inside the interface takes over the Spectrum's ROM so there is no BASIC. As a result, if you can't complete a game that uses Mikro-Plus then it's tough.

Pokes, the short memory changes that gave a player infinite lives, were typed into BASIC before a game loaded. With no BASIC to type them into, the magazine could no longer help. Candy went on:

> Mikro-Gen's advertisements in the trade press boast of beating the pirates but I would venture to suggest that the £15 price mark carried by Mikro Plus games is likely to do more damage to sales than the pirates ever could.

Perry told *Retro Gamer* in 2008 that the idea came when "the games were selling very well and Mikro-Gen was making a lot of money — almost to the point where we were starting to get a little cocky". He praised Laurie's design, "a very clever piece of electronics", but described the result plainly: "*Shadow Of The Unicorn* shipped first. It went out there and it died. Like corporately, it was a disaster, retailers were upset, so much so that Mikro-Gen couldn't afford to do a second game using the technology." *ACE*, listing the industry's failures in December 1988, put it in one line: "Mikro-Gen faded away not long after their unicorn."

## Creative Sparks

Mikro-Gen carried on for another year without the interface. Raffaele Cecco's *Equinox* (87%), *Stainless Steel* (89%) and *Frost Byte* (86%) all reviewed well, and at the end of 1986 the company ran a competition to find its top games player of the year, with the finalists playing its new game *Cop-Out*. By spring 1987 the company had been sold. *Computer Gamer* noted in April that "Mikro-Gen has made Creative Sparks glow brighter", and in June listed Mikro-Gen with Melbourne House, PSS and Thalamus among companies that had sought "the shelter of larger companies". In October *CRASH* reported that Creative Sparks Distribution had "recently went into receivership".

The Wally games outlived the company. Alternative Software reissued *Everyone's a Wally* on budget, and *CRASH* reported in 1988 that Alternative's re-releases usually sold between 40,000 and 100,000 copies each. In 2025 the Spectrum Next magazine *Next* reported a deal to bring the Wally games to the Next; see the [Alternative Software](/vault/companies/alternative-software) entry.
