Mikro-Gen, of **44 The Broadway, Bracknell, Berkshire**, published a run of Spectrum arcade
adventures built around a recurring everyman — **Wally Week** — that reviewed as well as
anything on the machine. *Commodore Format*, looking back in 1992, gave the fair summary:
"**Mikrogen's approach to the arcade adventure was novel**."

It is also one of the era's clearest cautionary tales, and the two facts are connected.

## Fast facts

- **Based:** 44 The Broadway, Bracknell, Berkshire.
- **Press evidence in this library:** *[CRASH](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine)*, *Amtix*, *Amstrad Action*, *ACE* and
  *[Computer & Video Games](/vault/magazines/computer-and-video-games)*, **1984 to 1988**, at **£6.95–£14.95**.
- **The Wally series:** *Pyjamarama*, *Everyone's a Wally*, *Herbert's Dummy Run*,
  *Three Weeks in Paradise*.
- **The hardware:** the **Mikro-Plus** interface, 1985.

The shape is worth reading: four early games in the fifties, sixties and seventies, then a

made it four times, and then stopped.

## The foam suit

Before the Mikro-Plus, Mikro-Gen knew how to sell a game. *Retro Gamer*'s account of the
**LET trade show of February 1985** has the company previewing *Everyone's a Wally* with a
man in a **large foam Wally Week suit** and a theme song the magazine remembers as about the
most annoying ever recorded.

The show mattered: *Dun Darach* and *Alien 8* were being previewed in the same halls. The
game went on sale for the Spectrum the following month and took **93%** — the joint-highest
score in the table above.

⚠ Worth holding beside what follows. This is a company that understood mascot marketing,
trade shows and a recurring character years before that was standard practice, and whose
games were reviewing at the top of the machine. The Mikro-Plus was not the mistake of a
company that did not know what it was doing.

## The Mikro-Plus

In August 1985 *CRASH* put it on the cover: "**At last the 64K Spectrum! MIKRO-PLUS — 16,
48, now 64K of Program Power.**"

The Mikro-Plus was an interface that plugged into a Spectrum and, in *CRASH*'s description
three months later, "**upgrades your Spectrum's memory to 64K by using a shadow ROM**" —
and let programs written for it be transferred to Microdrive. Games that used it shipped
**with the hardware in the box**, at around **£15** against a normal full price of £9.95.

The game was ***Shadow of the Unicorn***.

The pitch was anti-piracy: you cannot copy a cartridge onto a C90. *CRASH*'s own pokes
columnist, in December 1985, worked through what that meant for the reader and did
not like the answer:

> **With the advent of Mikro Plus pokes will be a thing of the past, because the EPROM
> inside the interface takes over the Spectrum's ROM so there is no BASIC.** As a result,
> if you can't complete a game that uses Mikro-Plus then it's tough.

Then he made the commercial prediction, in print, in 1985:

> Mikro-Gen's advertisements in the trade press boast of beating the pirates, but I would
> venture to suggest that the **£15 price mark carried by Mikro-Plus games is likely to do
> more damage to sales than the pirates ever could**.

He was right. *ACE*, three years later, in a feature on the industry's great failures, filed
Mikro-Gen under **Company Closed**: "**Mikro-Gen faded away not long after their
unicorn.**"

## What that episode is about

The Mikro-Plus reads as a company being stupid about copy protection. It is
more interesting than that, and the details matter.

**It solved the wrong problem competently.** The hardware worked. It genuinely gave the
machine 64K and genuinely could not be pirated. What it could not do was be *bought* — a
teenager choosing between one £15 game and one and a half £9.95 games is not weighing
copy protection.

**It broke the reader's relationship with the game.** *CRASH*'s objection is not economic
first, it is cultural. Pokes, cheats and infinite-lives listings were a core part of what
Spectrum magazines were *for*. A machine state with no BASIC underneath it is a machine you
cannot get into. Mikro-Gen shipped a product that made its own games unhackable and, in the
same stroke, made them undiscussable in the pages that sold them.

**It bet the company on a format nobody else adopted.** A proprietary interface is only
worth owning if there are games for it. There were not, because no other publisher was
going to put Mikro-Gen's hardware in their box.

## Afterwards

*Commodore Format*'s 1992 retrospective traces the tail: after the Wally games and a
licence — *Battle of the Planets*, **71%** — came "a brief foray with the 16-bits" and then
the Sega Mega Drive. The company that had produced five straight Spectrum games at 89% or
better did not produce anything comparable again.

## Why it mattered

Mikro-Gen is **piracy panic as a commercial decision**, and a study in what happens
when a publisher answers a real problem with a solution its customers did not ask for.

Every British publisher of the period was losing money to home taping. Most responded with
inlay cards, holograms ([Elite](/vault/companies/elite-systems)), replacement guarantees
([PSS](/vault/companies/pss)) or complaint. Mikro-Gen responded by **changing the
computer**, and the cost of that decision is legible in *CRASH*'s pokes column in December
1985 and in *ACE*'s obituary column three years later.

The Wally games deserve to be remembered on their own terms — 93, 93, 92, 90 is a run
almost nobody matched. It is a real loss that the company is remembered for the box it put
them in instead.
