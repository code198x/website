## Overview
Graftgold is the development studio behind a striking share of the best-regarded
British games of the 1980s — *Paradroid*, *Uridium*, *Quazatron*, *Avalon*,
*Ranarama* — and it is worth an entry separate from its two famous programmers
because it is a **different kind of company** from most in this Vault.

It did not publish. It made games under contract, put its own name on the team
rather than on the label, and survived the collapse of more than one publisher
it worked for.

## How it started
[Steve Turner](/vault/people/steve-turner) quit his job as a commercial
programmer in **1983** to write Spectrum games full time, trading as **ST
Software**. He hired a close friend, [Andrew Braybrook](/vault/people/andrew-braybrook),
initially to convert those games to the **Dragon**. ST Software incorporated as
**Graftgold** in **1984**.

*CRASH* caught them at exactly that moment. Its March 1984 issue introduces "the
professional programmer Steve Turner and his assistant, Andrew Braybrook", who
"have recently entered the field of writing software for home computers on a
full time basis" — Braybrook as Turner's hire, which is how it began, rather
than as the co-founder later accounts describe.

That relationship did not stay one-directional for long. Braybrook went on to
write *Paradroid* and *Uridium*, and Turner then reimagined *Paradroid* on the
Spectrum as *Quazatron*.

## The Hewson years
Graftgold's early success came through [Hewson
Consultants](/vault/companies/hewson-consultants), and the pairing defined both
companies. Turner held the Spectrum side from Hewson's first serious year;
Braybrook held the Commodore 64 side through its peak.

Hewson's practice of crediting authors on the inlay is part of why Graftgold's
name is known at all. Most contract development houses of the period are
invisible.

## The Telecomsoft move
In late 1987 Graftgold signed to **Telecomsoft** — British Telecom's software
operation, the parent of [Firebird](/vault/companies/firebird), Rainbird and
[Beyond](/vault/companies/beyond-software).

*CRASH* reported it in December 1987 as a team "signed to Telecomsoft amid
contractual controversy", and by March 1988 was describing *Morpheus* and
*Magnetron* as "the first products of Graftgold's long-term exclusive contract
with Firebird". Leaving the publisher that had made its reputation, for the
phone company, was not a quiet transaction.

The same issue notes a separate dispute involving [Level
9](/vault/companies/level-9), also a Telecomsoft signing — the aggressive
acquisition of British talent by BT's labels was becoming a story in its own
right.

## It went to the High Court

"Contractual controversy" is *CRASH*'s phrase and it understates what happened. *ACE*,
February 1988, headline **"BT GET GRAFTGOLD GAMES"**:

> After months of wrangling, Telecomsoft have announced that they will be bringing out the
> disputed games ***Morpheus*** and ***Magnetron***. The titles fell into their hands at the
> PCW show when Graftgold programmers Steve Turner and Andrew Braybrook, **long time
> associates of Hewson**, signed long term programming contracts.
>
> …**BT successfully restrained Hewson from publishing the titles** until the case can be
> tried… **Hewson had already geared up to publish the games themselves**, so Telecomsoft
> would have to make good whatever financial loss their injunction caused.

So [Hewson](/vault/companies/hewson-consultants) had manufactured for two games it was then
injuncted out of selling, and the two titles were split between the phone company's labels:
*Magnetron* to [Firebird](/vault/companies/firebird), *Morpheus* to
[Rainbird](/vault/companies/rainbird).

⚠ Note what this says about Graftgold's position, which "signing exclusively" does not
convey. The dispute was between two publishers over who owned
games Graftgold had written. A contract development house is the asset being fought over, not
usually a party to the fight.

## Who was in it
*CRASH* had to print a correction in December 1987 after leaving **Andrew
Whittaker** out of its list of Graftgold members. The team was always more than
its two famous names, which is the usual condition of development houses and the
usual omission.

## Two of the five best games of 1986

*CRASH*'s readers voted on the year, and the results printed in issue 38 (March 1987) place
Graftgold's work twice in the top five of **Best Game Overall**:

| # | Game | Publisher | Share of vote |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *Starglider* | Rainbird | **12.5%** |
| 2 | *The Great Escape* | Ocean ([Denton Designs](/vault/companies/denton-designs)) | **9%** |
| 3 | ***Uridium*** | **Hewson (Graftgold)** | **6%** |
| 4 | ***Quazatron*** | **Hewson (Graftgold)** | **5.8%** |
| 5 | *Ghosts 'n' Goblins* | Elite | **5%** |

*Uridium* also took **Best Shoot 'Em Up** outright, with 26% of the vote.

Two things follow. The first is the obvious one: a five-person contract studio put two games
in a national readership's top five, and neither carried its name on the spine. The second is
that the top four places went to **three development houses and no in-house publisher team**
— Realtime, Denton and Graftgold. That is the case for contract development made by the
readers rather than by a critic.

## The signing, dated by an apology

*ACE* dated the Telecomsoft move by accident, in a correction. Having run the wrong
photograph, the magazine explained itself in September 1988:

> The photo in question dates from **last year's PCW Show, when Graftgold signed up with
> Telecomsoft**, and has something of a jinx attached to it.

The PCW Show ran in September, which places the signing at **September 1987** — consistent
with *CRASH* reporting it that December "amid contractual controversy".

The blunder itself is worth keeping, because it says something about how the two men were
seen. *ACE* had a photograph with **both** Graftgold programmers in it, cropped the wrong one
out, and ran **Andrew Braybrook's** picture captioned as **Steve Turner**:

> Poor old Steve Turner must be a bit miffed… a mistake was at least halfway understandable
> given that we were using a photo with both of the Graftgold programmers in it, and cropped
> the wrong one out.

## What 7% looks like

Graftgold is this Vault's clearest case of a studio that made famous games without owning
them, and *ACE* got its senior coder to put a number on what that meant. April 1991, asking
[Andrew Braybrook](/vault/people/andrew-braybrook) whether developers were paid a fair share
of what their games earned:

> "**Of course not.** On the whole there are two sorts of deal that we get involved in,
> conversions and original titles."

The magazine's own breakdown of a £25 game:

> Of the **£25** that you fork out for the latest top game, on average only about **7%** of
> that revenue will end up in the **programmer's pocket**.

Roughly **£1.75** a copy, split across a team, for the people who wrote it. That is the
economic reality behind Graftgold outlasting the publishers it worked for: it never carried a publisher's costs, and it never took a publisher's margin
either.

## Rainbow Islands

Missing from the catalogue above, and one of the studio's best-regarded jobs. *ACE*,
September 1989, reviewing the home conversion:

> **Steve Turner, Andy Braybrook and company can congratulate themselves on this fine
> conversion** — nearly identical to the coin-op in almost every department.

The review also records what had to go, which is the usual honest detail of an 8-bit
conversion: **memory limitations** forced the omission of the three secret islands and the
Prince of Darkness, though Graftgold "managed to include **both** of the possible animated
end-game sequences".

## Afterwards
Graftgold continued into the 16-bit era with *Fire & Ice*, *Uridium 2*,
*Virocop* and *Realms*, working across the Amiga and Atari ST. It closed in
**1998** — fifteen years, which is a long life for a British studio of that
generation, and longer than Hewson Consultants, Beyond or Telecomsoft itself
managed.

⚠ The founding sequence is corroborated by *CRASH* in March 1984; the
incorporation dates and the 1998 closure come from present-day sources.
