"At our peak we had 17 employees," Andrew Braybrook told *Edge* in 1997, "but to be honest I would probably have earned more money if I'd stuck to programming mainframes for Marconi." Graftgold wrote *Avalon*, *Paradroid*, *Uridium* and *Quazatron*, and some of the best-regarded home conversions of arcade games. It never published anything itself. It wrote games under contract, and most of the publishers it worked for went bust, were sold, or fought over its games.

## ST Software

[Steve Turner](/vault/people/steve-turner) left a job as a commercial programmer in 1983 to write Spectrum games for [Hewson Consultants](/vault/companies/hewson-consultants), trading as ST Software. He worked in his dining room with a Spectrum and a black-and-white television, he recalled in *Retro Gamer*, and kept office hours, 9am to 5pm. Working alone was lonely, so he asked his friend [Andrew Braybrook](/vault/people/andrew-braybrook), a programmer at Marconi, to join him. It was a risk: Turner had saved about £10,000, and taking on an employee meant their games had to earn royalties within the first year.

*CRASH* introduced them in March 1984 as "the professional programmer Steve Turner and his assistant, Andrew Braybrook", who "have recently entered the field of writing software for home computers on a full time basis". Turner wrote for the Spectrum, and Braybrook converted his games to the [Dragon 32](/vault/systems/dragon-32). That was a lesson, Turner said: the Dragon went out of production and only a few hundred copies of each game sold. Braybrook moved to the [Commodore 64](/vault/systems/commodore-64). Andrew Whittaker, who left school in 1984, joined the team too.

Turner formed a limited company, Graftgold, when the Hewson royalties started to rise, to keep the money for future games and pay the programmers a steady wage. By March 1986 Braybrook was telling *ZZAP!64*: "ST Software, or Graft Gold as we're known now", and the team was about to move into offices in Witham, in Essex.

## The Hewson games

Hewson credited its authors by name, so Graftgold's games were known as Turner's and Braybrook's. Turner wrote *Avalon*, *Dragontorc*, *Astroclone*, *Quazatron* and *Ranarama* on the Spectrum. Braybrook wrote *Gribbly's Day Out*, *[Paradroid](/vault/games/paradroid)*, *[Uridium](/vault/games/uridium)* and *Alleykat* on the Commodore 64. *Quazatron*, Turner recalled, borrowed *Paradroid*'s gameplay and married it to a graphics engine he had built with no game to use it in.

*CRASH*'s readers put two of the team's games in their top five for 1986. In the Best Game Overall poll, printed in March 1987, *Starglider* won with 12.5% of the vote, *The Great Escape* came second, and "the two Graftgold goodies published by Hewson", *Uridium* and *Quazatron*, came third and fourth with 6% and 5.8%. *Uridium* also won Best Shoot 'Em Up with 26%. (The Spectrum *Uridium* was converted by Dominic Robinson, then at Hewson, from Braybrook's original.)

Turner's own account is less rosy. The Hewson deal gave Graftgold complete creative freedom and no advances, and every Spectrum game "seemed to sell less than the previous one". *Dragontorc* sold far below *Avalon*, *Astroclone* "did not fit into a neat category", and *Ranarama* did better in Spain than in Britain.

## To Telecomsoft, and to court

At the PCW Show in September 1987 Graftgold signed with [Telecomsoft](/vault/companies/telecomsoft), British Telecom's software business and the owner of [Firebird](/vault/companies/firebird) and [Rainbird](/vault/companies/rainbird). *CRASH* reported that Turner and Braybrook would be joined by Dominic Robinson and John Cumming, "both from Hewson". Turner later filled in the background: Robinson and Cumming had rung him, thinking Hewson was finished, and Hewson's second-in-command had left for Telecomsoft and introduced Graftgold there. With four programmers, Graftgold doubled in size and moved into its first offices, above a greengrocer's in Witham.

Hewson had been preparing to publish Braybrook's *Morpheus* and Turner's *Magnetron*, and challenged Graftgold's right to take them elsewhere. *ACE* reported in February 1988 that "after months of wrangling" Telecomsoft had won:

> The titles fell into their hands at the PCW show when Graftgold programmers Steve Turner and Andrew Braybrook, long time associates of Hewson, signed long term programming contracts.

A High Court injunction, *ACE* said, had "restrained Hewson from publishing the titles until the case can be tried". *Magnetron* went to Firebird and *Morpheus* to Rainbird. *CRASH* called them "the first products of Graftgold's long-term exclusive contract with Firebird". Turner said that Hewson had never paid Graftgold advances or given it a contract, and that the two publishers eventually settled out of court.

The move left one more trace in *ACE*. In September 1988 the magazine apologised for printing a picture of Braybrook captioned as Turner. It had cropped the wrong man out of a photograph of both Graftgold programmers, taken "at last year's PCW show when Graftgold signed up with Telecomsoft".

## Rainbow Islands

Telecomsoft paid Graftgold to convert Taito's arcade game *Flying Shark*; Robinson wrote the Spectrum version in six weeks, and Turner had 14 days to convert it to the Amstrad. The bigger job was Taito's *[Rainbow Islands](/vault/games/rainbow-islands)*. Braybrook's diary dates the work from September 1988 to June 1989. Braybrook led it, using Robinson's 16-bit engine; David O'Connor wrote the Spectrum version and Gary Foreman the Commodore 64 one, Turner recalled.

*ACE* reviewed the Atari ST version in September 1989. "Steve Turner, Andy Braybrook and company can congratulate themselves on this fine conversion," it said: "nearly identical to the coin-op in almost every department". Memory limits had forced them to leave out the three secret islands, but they had managed to include both of the possible animated endings.

Then the game disappeared. Telecomsoft had been sold to MicroProse, whose deal with Taito fell through, and it could not release the conversions. In March 1990 *ACE* reported that Ocean had signed a new agreement with Taito and bought Graftgold's code from MicroProse, for a sum rumoured to be around £50,000. Ocean published it in 1990. Six years later Graftgold converted *Rainbow Islands* again, for a PlayStation and PC compilation from Acclaim; Turner told *Edge* that the arcade code was not available, so, as for the Amiga version, the team worked from video of the entire game.

## Where the money went

Braybrook knew what a developer earned. In April 1991 *ACE* asked him whether programmers got a fair share of their games' income. "Of course not," he said. The magazine's own estimate was that about 7% of the £25 price of a top game ended up "in the programmer's pocket".

## The 16-bit years

Graftgold's later publishers came and went quickly. Braybrook's *Paradroid 90* was written for Hewson, which collapsed; the rights went to Activision. *Realms*, a strategy game, was also begun for Activision, which then closed its operation. Graftgold bought *Realms* back and licensed it to Virgin, which advertised it for the autumn of 1991. *Fire & Ice*, Braybrook's platform game, was part-financed by Mirrorsoft, which then collapsed; [Renegade](/vault/companies/renegade) published it in 1992 and went on to publish Braybrook's *Uridium 2* (1993) and Iain Wallington's *Virocop*, which *Amiga Power* reviewed in 1995.

Other work was lost altogether. Graftgold wrote *Empire Soccer* for the Super Nintendo in time for the 1994 World Cup, and it was never released; a Mega Drive *Fire & Ice* and a CD32 version were finished or nearly so, and also never released. *Edge*'s 1997 profile of Braybrook listed Telecomsoft, Mirrorsoft and Renegade as publishers that "all changed hands within months of signing Graftgold".

## The end

Renegade was bought by Warner, which published Graftgold's motorbike racer *International Moto X* on the PC and PlayStation. Turner said it had no marketing budget, but it became Graftgold's biggest profit-maker. Warner also signed, then cancelled, a large game based on *Avalon*. Graftgold then signed a PlayStation tank game with the Japanese publisher Coconuts. When Coconuts tried to cut the monthly advances to a third, Turner sold a majority of the company to Perfect Entertainment, the developer of the *Discworld* games.

The tank game became *Hard Corp*. *PC Zone* listed it among Perfect's forthcoming releases in February 1998, from "developers Graftgold", due in the spring. It was never finished. Perfect found extra money from a publisher, but the funding stopped before the game was complete, and, Turner wrote, "so 15 years of developing games came to an end". Looking back, he said that most of the publishers Graftgold had worked with "either went bust, sold out or simply did not publish the game to our expectations, despite tight contracts".
