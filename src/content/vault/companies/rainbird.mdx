**Rainbird** was the premium imprint of **Telecomsoft**, British Telecom's software
operation. Where its sibling [Firebird](/vault/companies/firebird) sold £2.50 cassettes off a
newsagent's rack, Rainbird sold full-price simulations and adventures — and, increasingly,
16-bit games at £24.95.

The name is a person. **Tony Rainbird** was persuaded away from his own label, Micro-Gold, in
June 1984 to get BT's software operation moving; the premium imprint carries his name. The
fuller account of Telecomsoft's founding, its budget model and its 1989 sale sits in the
[Firebird](/vault/companies/firebird) entry — this one covers the label above it.

## Fast facts

- **Parent:** Telecomsoft, a division of British Telecom.
- **Siblings:** [Firebird](/vault/companies/firebird) (full-price and, as Silverbird, budget).
- **Prices:** **£14.95** on 8-bit, rising to **£24.95** on ST and Amiga.
- **Published:** *Starglider* and *Starglider 2*, *The Pawn*, *Carrier Command*, *Jewels of Darkness*, *Time and Magik*, *Morpheus*, *Zarch*.
- **Legal status:** "Rainbird" was a **registered trademark of British Telecom** — printed as such in its own advertising.

## What it published, and how it scored

Rainbird's catalogue is short and unusually strong.

*Starglider* did more than review well. *CRASH*'s readers voted it **Best Game of 1986
overall**, taking 12.5% of the vote — ahead of *The Great Escape*, *Uridium* and *Quazatron*
— despite costing, as the magazine noted, "nearly twice as much as most 'full price'
Spectrum games".

## The strategy: buy the best developers

Rainbird's method was to sign the strongest independent houses in the country rather than
develop in-house. At the April 1986 Consumer Electronics Show it announced tie-ins with
**Magnetic Scrolls and Level 9 simultaneously** — the two best-regarded British adventure
developers, on one label, in one announcement.

Its 16-bit line came the same way: *Starglider* and *Starglider 2* from
[Argonaut](/vault/companies/argonaut), *Carrier Command* from Realtime, *Zarch* from
[David Braben](/vault/people/david-braben) — the ST and Amiga versions of which *ACE*
reported in May 1988 were running late and might "not even be called *Zarch*", the working
title having become ***Bird of Prey***.

## Both adventure houses, and how each ended

Rainbird held the two names that mattered in British adventure publishing, and lost them in
opposite ways.

**[Level 9](/vault/companies/level-9) walked.** *ACE*, March 1988: "Level 9 have severed
relations with Rainbird Software and have tied up an exclusive distribution deal with a new
label called Mandarin." The grievance was delay — *Time and Magik* had been "endlessly
delayed" under Rainbird.

**[Magnetic Scrolls](/vault/companies/magnetic-scrolls) stayed, and had planned for it.**
Anita Sinclair had written a change-of-control clause into the BT contract, so when
Telecomsoft was sold to [MicroProse](/vault/companies/microprose) in 1989, the distribution
rights to the Scrolls catalogue did not transfer with it.

One label, two of the country's best developers, and neither relationship survived it.

## The Graftgold injunction

The sharpest thing Telecomsoft ever did was take a rival's authors and then go to court to
keep their games. *ACE*, February 1988, under the headline **"BT GET GRAFTGOLD GAMES"**:

> After months of wrangling, Telecomsoft have announced that they will be bringing out the
> disputed games *Morpheus* and *Magnetron*. **The titles fell into their hands at the PCW
> show when Graftgold programmers Steve Turner and Andrew Braybrook, long time associates of
> Hewson, signed long term programming contracts.**

Rights were "disputed fiercely", and the matter was settled — temporarily — in the High
Court:

> **BT successfully restrained Hewson from publishing the titles** until the case can be
> tried… **Hewson had already geared up to publish the games themselves**, so Telecomsoft
> would have to make good whatever financial loss their injunction caused.

The spoils were split across the two labels: "**Firebird will get *Magnetron* while
*Morpheus* goes to sister label Rainbird.**"

This is the clearest single illustration of what the phone company's arrival meant. A
publisher with BT's legal department behind it could injunct a mid-sized British software
house out of publishing games it had already manufactured for.

## "Big is bad, bad, bad"

By 1988 the industry was saying so out loud, and *ACE* put the accusations to Telecomsoft's
**Paula Byrne** in August:

> Rainbird, Silverbird and Firebird are beginning to prove **a very potent combination**, and
> some people in the industry actually seem to be afraid of our feathered friends. They
> reckon that in Telecomsoft's case, **big is bad, bad, bad** — a company which represents a
> deadly form of **cheque-book publishing**, a company who can march into the market and
> spend millions.

Byrne's answer was that the money bought support, not just a higher bid:

> The reason Telecomsoft puts out such high quality product is that **top programmers want to
> work with Telecomsoft as they trust the name of British Telecom**… Telecomsoft has a large
> development team of **14 people who offer our programmers support**.

⚠ Both things were true. The catalogue above is genuinely excellent, and it was assembled by
a state-privatised telecoms monopoly outspending independent publishers for the country's
best developers — then, in Graftgold's case, injuncting one of them.

## The end

Telecomsoft was sold to [MicroProse](/vault/companies/microprose) in 1989 and the Rainbird
name went with the rest of the catalogue. *The One*'s verdict in October 1991 covers both
halves of the Graftgold affair: Hewson "never really recovered from Turner and Braybrook's
defection", and "**Telecomsoft never really recovered either — its labels were sold to
MicroProse not long afterwards.**"
