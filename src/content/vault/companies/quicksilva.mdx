## Overview

Quicksilva belongs to the earliest days of British commercial software — the stretch around 1980 when a "software company" might be two people, a duplicating deck and a small ad in the back of a magazine. It started in Southampton, selling programs for the ZX80 and ZX81 by post, and grew into one of the first recognisable labels of the Spectrum era.

## Fast facts

- **Based:** Southampton — **95 Upper Brownhill Road, Maybush** in 1982, **15 Palmerston Road** by 1984.
- **Trading as:** Quicksilva, and **QS** on the ZX81 labels.
- **In the press from:** 1982, at **£4.95–£5.95** for ZX81 and early Spectrum tapes, rising to £6.95–£14.95 by 1984.
- **Known for:** *Ant Attack*, *Time Gate*, *Meteor Storm*, *Aquaplane*, *Bugaboo (The Flea)*, *Games Designer*, *Velnor's Lair*.
- **Also sold:** hardware and tools — a ZX81 character generator, *Music Maker*, *Ultisynth*, *BeebArt*.

## What it sold

The early catalogue is more specific than "one of the first British software
labels" suggests, and the specificity is the interesting part.

In **May 1982**, *Computer & Video Games* listed Quicksilva of 95 Upper Brownhill
Road, Maybush, Southampton as having three games on the market: "all versions of
popular arcade games, and include **space invaders, defender and asteroids**, and
all cost £5.50." On the ZX81 these went out under the **QS** prefix — *QS Defenda*
by **Nick Lambert**, *QS Asteroids* by **John Hollis**, *QS Invaders* — later at
**£3.95** each.

That is what an early British software house was: not an original-design studio,
but a shop reproducing arcade games on a machine with 1K of screen and no sprites.
*C&VG* gave them the compliment that mattered in September 1982 — Quicksilva "have
already won themselves a good reputation for producing good **arcade replicas
within the limitations of the ZX81**".

**It also sold hardware.** The same 1982 round-up lists a Quicksilva
**programmable character generator at £26.00** for the ZX81, letting the user
define lower-case letters, scientific notation "and space invader-type meanies",
supplied with a demonstration cassette of machine-code routines. A software house
selling you a ROM to change your computer's character set is a good marker of how
undivided the market still was.

## Racing Bug-Byte to the same games

When the Spectrum arrived, Quicksilva went straight at it and straight at its
competitors. *C&VG* in November 1982: "Southampton firm Quicksilva were **hot on
the heels of Bug Byte** with their version of Invaders for the Spectrum — *Space
Intruders*."

The magazine reviewed three Spectrum *Space Invaders* clones side by side —
Quicksilva's, [Bug-Byte](/vault/companies/bug-byte)'s and Psion's — and Quicksilva
lost: "for my £5 Bug Byte offers the best game of Space Invaders on the Spectrum so
far." Two months later it ran the same exercise for *Asteroids* clones and
Quicksilva won, its *Meteor Storm* judged "marginally better because of its extra
facilities and graphics", and the only one of the three that let you enter your
initials in a top-ten table.

Three companies shipping the same arcade game to the same machine in the same
season, reviewed against each other by price, is what the market looked like
before original design was the point.

By **1984** the advertisements read differently. Quicksilva's full-page listings —
under the slogan "**Quicksilva are the game lords**" — credit their authors by

## Games, and not only games

The press evidence catches something the era tends to obscure about early
publishers. *Music Maker*, *Ultisynth* and *BeebArt* are **tools, not games**, and
they sat in the same advertisement as *Ant Attack* at similar or higher prices.

The line between "software" and "games" had not yet hardened. A publisher of this
era might sell you a game, a utility, a music package, a programming aid or a
character-generator ROM from the same catalogue, because the customer was
understood as someone with a computer, not someone who wanted to play something.
That distinction sharpened over the decade, and Quicksilva's lists are a record of
the period before it did.

## GOSH

In 1983 Quicksilva was one of the companies that founded the
**[Guild of Software Houses](/vault/phenomena/guild-of-software-houses)** — the
industry's first attempt at self-regulation, and the body that published a
Customers' Charter promising mail-order delivery times, replacement of defective
tapes, and no advertising of products before they existed.

*Commodore Computing International* named the instigators in October 1983:
"leading software houses such as **Quicksilva, Virgin Games and Bug-Byte founded
the guild** in response to the public's need for a little more discipline from the
software houses themselves."

The three founders are the three oldest mail-order houses in the business. They
had been taking postal orders since before there was a distribution network, and
they were the ones who knew exactly which promises the trade was breaking.

## Bought, 1984

Quicksilva did not fade out; it was acquired — in **1984**, by **Argus Press Software**, "part of the massive **BET group**". *CRASH* reported the consequence in September 1985 in a sentence worth quoting in full:

> Sadly, **much of the pioneering firm's identity was submerged** when Quicksilva was bought by Argus, part of the massive BET group, last year.

A companion piece records that Quicksilva "was sold to Argus Press Software… and the team disbanded". By April 1985 *CRASH* was treating the purchase as old news, joking about "**Argus Press Software's shopping list for other companies**" at the LET trade show — where Argus also took over production and marketing for **Lothlorien**, and Quicksilva bought exclusive rights to seven **Bug-Byte** titles.

**Rod Cousens**, Quicksilva's MD, is the figure quoted throughout, promising "considerable expansion in the field of publishing third party software".

So by 1985 the mail-order pioneer had become an **acquisition vehicle inside a magazine publisher's software arm**. Argus Press Software was itself renamed a couple of years later — it is the company this Vault covers as [Grandslam](/vault/companies/grandslam).

Everything described above about Quicksilva's early years therefore belongs to **1979–1985**. After that the name continues and the company behind it does not.

## Why it mattered

Quicksilva is a good answer to "who was doing this first". Not the largest publisher, not the longest-lived, but present at the point where selling programs to home users stopped being a hobby and became a trade — and its catalogue shows what that looked like before anyone knew the shape it would take.
