## Overview

Quicksilva belongs to the earliest days of British commercial software — the stretch around 1980 when a "software company" might be two people, a duplicating deck and a small ad in the back of a magazine. It started in Southampton, selling programs for the ZX80 and ZX81 by post, and grew into one of the first recognisable labels of the Spectrum era.

## Fast facts

- **Based:** Southampton.
- **Seen in the press:** reviewed in *CRASH* and *ZZAP!64* from **1985 to 1987**, at **£6.95–£14.95**, with a music package at **£29.95**.
- **Known for:** *Ant Attack*, *Aquaplane*, *Glider Rider*, *Agent Orange*, *Max Headroom*, *Yabba Dabba Doo!*, *Terramex*, *Ice Palace*.
- **Also sold:** *Music Maker* and *Ultisynth* — music tools, not games.

## Before there was a market

The early catalogue matters less for individual titles than for what it represents. In 1981 there was no distribution network for computer software in Britain, no shelf in a shop to put it on, and no press to review it. What existed was **mail order**: an advertisement, a postal order, and a cassette back in the post a fortnight later.

Quicksilva was among the companies that turned that into a business, and by the Spectrum's arrival in 1982 it was positioned as an established name in a market that had barely existed two years earlier. *Ant Attack* — Sandy White's isometric 3D game, one of the first of its kind on any home computer — is the release that still gets cited, and it came out in 1983.

## Games, and not only games

The press evidence catches something the era tends to obscure about early publishers: *Music Maker* and *Ultisynth*, reviewed side by side in *ZZAP!64*'s first issue, are **music tools, not games**.

The line between "software" and "games" had not yet hardened. A publisher of this era might sell you a game, a utility, a music package or a programming aid from the same catalogue, because the customer was understood as someone with a computer, not someone who wanted to play something. That distinction sharpened over the decade, and Quicksilva's early lists are a record of the period before it did.

## Bought, 1985

⚠ Quicksilva did not fade out; it was acquired — in **1984**, by **Argus Press Software**, "part of the massive **BET group**". *CRASH* reported the consequence in September 1985 in a sentence worth quoting in full:

> Sadly, **much of the pioneering firm's identity was submerged** when Quicksilva was bought by Argus, part of the massive BET group, last year.

A companion piece records that Quicksilva "was sold to Argus Press Software… and the team disbanded". By April 1985 *CRASH* was treating the purchase as old news, joking about "**Argus Press Software's shopping list for other companies**" at the LET trade show — where Argus also took over production and marketing for **Lothlorien**, and Quicksilva bought exclusive rights to seven **Bug-Byte** titles.

**Rod Cousens**, Quicksilva's MD, is the figure quoted throughout, promising "considerable expansion in the field of publishing third party software".

So by 1985 the mail-order pioneer had become an **acquisition vehicle inside a magazine publisher's software arm**. Argus Press Software was itself renamed a couple of years later — it is the company this Vault covers as [Grandslam](/vault/companies/grandslam).

Everything described above about Quicksilva's early years therefore belongs to **1979–1985**. After that the name continues and the company behind it does not.

## Why it mattered

Quicksilva is a good answer to "who was doing this first". Not the largest publisher, not the longest-lived, but present at the point where selling programs to home users stopped being a hobby and became a trade — and its catalogue shows what that looked like before anyone knew the shape it would take.
