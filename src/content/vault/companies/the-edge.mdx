The Edge published for about four years and is remembered for one game above all others. *CRASH* reviewed *[Fairlight](/vault/games/fairlight)* in **issue 22, November 1985**, and gave it **95% overall** — with **97% for graphics**, the highest single figure in the panel.

⚠ *Fairlight* is widely quoted at **96%**. The review says 95; the 97 for graphics is the likeliest origin of the error.

## Fast facts

- **Based:** 31 Maiden Lane, Covent Garden, London. Distributed through **EMI Distribution**.
- **In the press:** *[CRASH](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine)*, *[ZZAP!64](/vault/magazines/zzap-64)* and *Amstrad Action*, **1984 to 1988**, at **£7.95–£9.95** cassette, £12.95 disk.
- **Published:** *Fairlight* and *Fairlight II*, *Brian Bloodaxe* (86%), *Bobby Bearing*, *Starbike*, *Psytraxx*, *Inside Outing*, *Skimmer*.
- **Fairlight's author:** [Bo Jangeborg](/vault/people/bo-jangeborg), Swedish, working from The Edge's London office and then from home.
- **Its engine:** [Worldmaker and Grax](/vault/tools/worldmaker), which The Edge planned to sell as a utility in its own right.

## Fairlight

*Fairlight* (1985) is an isometric adventure in the family that [Ultimate](/vault/companies/ultimate)'s *Knight Lore* had opened the year before, and it took the idea somewhere different: **objects with weight and physical behaviour**, stackable and pushable, in rooms where a puzzle might be solved by piling one thing on another instead of by finding the right key.

Getting that onto a 48K Spectrum meant an engine that could describe a room as geometry and objects as things with properties, not as pictures. It also meant drawing filled, overlapping, correctly ordered solids fast enough to be playable in an [attribute-constrained](/vault/techniques/colour-clash) display — which is a rendering problem, and one that whole later industries went on to solve with hardware.

That it was written by **a programmer in Sweden**, published in London and reviewed as a British game is worth stating plainly. The 8-bit scene is often narrated as a national story, and it was not one. The machines were the same everywhere, the magazines travelled, and the work crossed borders long before the industry noticed.

## The rest of the catalogue

*Brian Bloodaxe* (£7.95, credited by *CRASH* to Charles Bystram) is a wilfully strange platform game with a Viking loose in a Britain of national stereotypes; it took 86%. *Bobby Bearing* is another isometric game, this one about momentum: you steer a ball bearing that keeps going after you stop pushing, which is a physics puzzle before that phrase existed. *Inside Outing* and *Skimmer* carried the label into 1987 and 1988.

The pattern is a publisher with **a taste for the technically ambitious and the slightly odd**, at a scale of a few games a year.

The Edge's own advertising fills in the unglamorous half. The company sat at 31 Maiden Lane, Covent Garden, and told shops that "authorised dealers can order directly from **EMI Distribution**". A small London label with a handful of clever games still needed a record company's lorries to reach Sheffield — the same arrangement, from the other end, that [Ariolasoft](/vault/companies/ariolasoft) was built on.

## Why it mattered

The Edge belongs to **isometric 3D as a British speciality**. Between *Knight Lore*, *Fairlight*, *Bobby Bearing*, *Head Over Heels* and *Batman*, a particular way of drawing a solid world with no 3D hardware was worked out on the Spectrum and the C64 in the space of about three years, and *Fairlight* is one of the places it went furthest.

It is also the reminder that a 95% score in 1985 was a technical verdict as much as a critical one. What reviewers were reacting to was a machine doing something it manifestly should not have been able to do.
