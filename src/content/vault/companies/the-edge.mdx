The Edge began, in the autumn of 1984, as a press release. Tim Langdell, managing director of the London software house Softek, announced that Softek's games team had asked to become "an independent division" and would now be called The Edge. *[CRASH](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine)* wondered how a breakaway group fitted into Softek's "tiny two-room offices" in London. Within two years that small Covent Garden label had published *[Fairlight](/vault/games/fairlight)*, which *CRASH* gave 95%, and *Bobby Bearing*, which it gave 94%. Both drew solid-looking rooms in [isometric projection](/vault/techniques/isometric-projection) on a 48K Spectrum.

## Fast facts

- **Parent:** Softek International, run by Tim Langdell; The Edge was its games label from 1984.
- **Addresses:** 31 Maiden Lane, Covent Garden, in 1984–85; 12/13 Henrietta Street, Softek's address, on the *Fairlight* adverts of early 1986; 36–38 Southampton Street from September 1986.
- **Distribution:** its 1985 adverts told shops that "authorised dealers can order directly from EMI Distribution".
- **Best-known games:** *Fairlight* (1985) and *Fairlight II: A Trail of Darkness* (1986) by [Bo Jangeborg](/vault/people/bo-jangeborg); *Bobby Bearing* (1986) by Robert and Trevor Figgins; *Garfield: Big, Fat, Hairy Deal* (1988).
- **Other labels:** Softechnics, for utilities such as *The Artist*, and ACE, for arcade conversions, from 1987.
- **Later:** The Edge Interactive Media in London in 1990, then a company in Pasadena, California, by 1991, later known as Edge Games.

## Megagames

The Edge introduced itself with big claims. Its first games, *CRASH* reported in October 1984, were billed as "megagames", "the first ever". The Commodore 64 game *Quo Vadis* had already been *Personal Computer Games*' game of the month, and the Spectrum shoot-'em-up *Starbike* had been "voted" into *[Computer and Video Games](/vault/magazines/computer-and-video-games)*' Hall of Fame before it was even released. *CRASH* reviewed *Starbike* in November: £6.95, written by Paul Curtis, with speech through Currah's Microspeech add-on and a high-score table its reviewers admired. It scored 77%. "Let's hope Softek doesn't go right over The Edge," the news page joked.

The next big hit was stranger. *Brian Bloodaxe* (March 1985, £7.95, by Charles Bystram) is a Viking who has spent centuries frozen in a block of ice. He thaws out in 1983, sets off to finish conquering Britain, and has to steal the Crown Jewels and sit on the throne. There are 104 screens of platform jumping, objects to carry three at a time, and the Monty Python theme tune as its music. *CRASH* gave it 86%. The game claimed to run on a system called "Primary Imbalance", and one reviewer decided that this meant it took so many attempts to load.

## Fairlight

*Fairlight* came from Bo Jangeborg, a Swedish programmer who had written the drawing program *The Artist* for Softek. He came to London in 1985 meaning to stay a week. He stayed seven months, lodging at Langdell's house, and wrote *Fairlight* there. In November 1985 *CRASH* gave it 95%, with 97% for graphics. The magazine's index of the year's reviews printed the score as 96%, the figure that is sometimes quoted for it. Its rooms are full of objects with weight, which the player pushes, stacks and climbs. Jangeborg later told *[Edge](/vault/magazines/edge)* that it had sold more than 50,000 copies.

The rooms were drawn with Grax, the tool behind what The Edge called its Worldmaker technique (see [Worldmaker and Grax](/vault/tools/worldmaker)). When Jangeborg went home to Sweden, *CRASH* reported, Grax stayed behind with The Edge as the basis for more adventures and perhaps a product readers could buy. In September 1986 The Edge said it was planning Dreamscape, an icon-driven adventure-writing system that "just might include" Grax. *Fairlight II: A Trail of Darkness* followed at Christmas 1986. *CRASH*'s reviewers were cooler about it, and by April 1987 one of them wrote that "The Edge have been disappointing lately, Fairlight II was pretty but unplayable".

Jangeborg did not work for The Edge again. In May 1987 *CRASH*'s John Minson reported that he had "had enough of life on The Edge". Two years later Jangeborg told the Swedish magazine *Atari-STen* that "the conflict with The Edge" had taken up much of his time, and that it was "something I would rather forget". *Edge* magazine later said that he had left after a contractual dispute.

## Bobby Bearing

The other isometric game is about a ball bearing. Bobby has to find his brothers, lost in a three-dimensional maze called the Metaplanes, and nudge them home one at a time, while black bearings with huge teeth try to knock him senseless. Robert and Trevor Figgins wrote it, and The Edge called the technique "Curvispace 3D". The ball rolls up ramps and round curves and bounces off walls, and *CRASH*'s reviewers were struck by how convincingly it all behaved. They compared it with *Spindizzy* and *[Marble Madness](/vault/games/marble-madness)*, called it "a technically stunning game", and gave it 94% in August 1986, at £7.95.

## Licences and conversions

From 1987 The Edge looked for bigger names. *The Games Machine* reported in October 1987 that "all quiet at The Edge" had given way to a burst of new titles and a new label, ACE, for arcade conversions. Its games included the shoot-'em-up *Xecutor* and a conversion of Sega's *Alien Syndrome*.

The Edge's own catalogue kept its interest in detailed 3D rooms. *Inside Outing* (February 1988, £8.99, by Michael St Aubyn) sends a thief through a widow's house to find the jewels her late husband hid, among furniture and plates that can each be moved on their own. *CRASH*'s reviewers could not agree on it. One gave it 94% and called it "the closest thing to real life on the Spectrum". Another gave it 75% and pointed out that the telephone was twice the size of the tables. The overall mark was 82%.

The biggest licence was Jim Davis's cat. *Garfield: Big, Fat, Hairy Deal* took 90% in *CRASH* in March 1988. Langdell told the magazine that Garfield had "a following from five to 95". His wife and colleague, Cheri Langdell, described "very firm guidance from above as to the image of Garfield": the cat could not swear or kill anyone, and could not die in the game, only fall asleep. Only one of The Edge's proposed scenes was cut. In it Garfield used "an instrument to do something that might be considered aggressive".

By 1990 The Edge was selling *Darius+*, its version of Taito's shoot-'em-up, for the Spectrum, Amstrad, Commodore 64, Amiga, ST and PC. That summer a recruitment advert in *The Games Machine*, signed by Langdell for "The Edge Interactive Media", listed licences including Garfield, Snoopy, Asterix, MTV and Marvel comic characters.

## Edge Games

In December 1991 *The One* found Langdell in California, running a company from Pasadena that it called "The Edge Interactive Multimedia Inc" and developing for CD platforms. The Italian author Alessandro Grussu writes that Langdell later dissolved the British company and set it up again in California as Edge Games. Grussu also calls Langdell one of the industry's most controversial figures, for suing other companies over their use of the word "edge".

Those claims reached a federal court. In October 2010, according to the trade paper *MCV*, Judge William Alsup of the US District Court for the Northern District of California ordered the US Patent and Trademark Office to cancel Edge Games' "edge" trademarks. The order said there was "scant evidence that Edge Games has invested any amount of funds into the development of recent products and services bearing the asserted marks". It also required Langdell to tell every licensee that the marks had been cancelled.
