## Overview

Renegade was set up by the people behind [Sensible Software](/vault/companies/sensible-software) — [Jon Hare](/vault/people/jon-hare) and Chris Yates — with Tim Chaney, and its premise was straightforward: **developers should own the label**.

In an industry where studios signed to publishers and handed over the rights, that was unusual. Renegade's catalogue is short, almost entirely Amiga, and unusually strong, because it was assembled by people choosing what they wanted to publish, not filling a release schedule.

## Fast facts

- **Founded by:** Jon Hare and Chris Yates of Sensible Software, with Tim Chaney.
- **Seen in the press:** reviewed in *Amiga Power* from **1991 to 1995**, almost all at **£25.99** — full price, no budget tier.
- **Published:** *Sensible Soccer*, *The Chaos Engine*, *Magic Pockets*, *Turrican 3*, *Uridium 2*, *Ruff 'n' Tumble*, *Elfmania*.

## A catalogue with no filler

Look at that list again. *Sensible Soccer* and *Uridium 2* are Sensible Software's own. *The Chaos Engine* and *Magic Pockets* are the [Bitmap Brothers](/vault/companies/bitmap-brothers). *Turrican 3* is Factor 5's. *Ruff 'n' Tumble* is Renegade's own commission.

That is a publisher whose entire output consists of titles by **the most respected Amiga developers working at the time** — and the reason is structural. A developer-owned label could offer other developers terms and treatment that a corporate publisher would not, and the people running it had credibility with exactly the studios worth signing.

The absence is as telling as the presence: **no budget imprint, no licensed tie-ins, no compilations of other people's back catalogue.** Nearly every publisher in this Vault has at least one of those. Renegade published fewer than a dozen games and appears to have wanted it that way.

## Why it mattered

Renegade is the counter-example to the standard eighties and nineties arrangement, where the people who made games and the people who sold them were different companies with divergent interests. It shows the alternative was possible on a small scale, with a strong enough reputation behind it.

It is also a good vantage point on the late Amiga. By 1993 the machine was in decline commercially, and yet *The Chaos Engine*, *Sensible Soccer* and *Turrican 3* are all from these years — a body of work made by people who knew the hardware completely, at the point where that knowledge was about to stop being worth anything.
