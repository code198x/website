## Overview

Elite Systems, based in Walsall in the West Midlands, made its name doing one thing extremely visibly: bringing coin-op games home. Where other British publishers grew from bedroom programmers selling their own work, Elite's business was **licensing arcade titles and converting them** to the machines people owned — and for several years it was good at it.

## Fast facts

- **Based:** Walsall, West Midlands.
- **Business:** arcade licences and conversions, later original titles and re-releases.
- **Press evidence in this library:** reviewed across *CRASH*, *ZZAP!64*, *Amstrad Action* and *Amiga Power* from **1985 to 1991**, at prices from **£7.95 to £25.99** — a span that tracks the whole 8-bit-to-16-bit transition.
- **Known for:** *Bomb Jack* and *Bomb Jack II*, *Commando*, *Ghosts'n Goblins*, *Paperboy*, *Ikari Warriors*, *Space Harrier*, *Frank Bruno's Boxing*.
- **Also published:** TV and film licences (*Thundercats*, *Scooby Doo*, *The Dukes of Hazzard*) and originals (*Kokotoni Wilf*, *Alter Ego*, *Hopping Mad*).

## The conversion business

An arcade conversion in this era was a peculiar commercial proposition. The licence bought you a title everybody had already played and a cabinet's worth of expectation — but the target machine had a fraction of the coin-op's memory, no dedicated sprite hardware in most cases, and a colour system designed for television.

The result was a genre of its own: games judged not against other home titles but against a machine in an arcade that nobody could beat. Reviews of the period read accordingly, weighing "how close is it?" as heavily as whether the game stood up on its own terms. Elite's conversions landed across that whole range, and its best were among the most respected of the type.

**The catalogue is not only arcade conversions**, though, and it is worth correcting the shorthand. Alongside the coin-ops sit television and film licences — *Thundercats*, *Scooby Doo*, *The Dukes of Hazzard* — and outright originals such as *Kokotoni Wilf* and *Alter Ego*. The through-line is licensing as a business, not the arcade specifically: Elite bought recognition, from whichever medium was selling it.

The arcade side of the catalogue is still a map of what the cabinets were doing. *Bomb Jack*, *Commando* and *Ghosts'n Goblins* are all mid-eighties coin-op staples, arriving on the Spectrum and C64 within a year or two of the cabinets — which is why an Elite release list doubles as a record of what British teenagers were feeding ten-pence pieces into.

## Why it mattered

Elite is a useful corrective to the tidy story of British software as a cottage industry of teenage auteurs. That story is real, but alongside it ran a business of licences, deadlines and conversion teams working to a specification — closer to how the industry would look everywhere a decade later.

By the late eighties the company was publishing across the 8-bit machines and into the Amiga, and it survived the transition that killed many of its contemporaries, continuing in various forms long after the era that made it.
