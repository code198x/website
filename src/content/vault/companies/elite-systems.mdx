Elite Systems Ltd, **Anchor House, Anchor Road, Aldridge, Walsall, West Midlands WS9
8PW** — the address is on every advertisement it ever placed, and it was still on the
copyright line of a 3DO release in 1995.

The company was run by **Steve Wilcox**, and its business was **buying recognition and
converting it**. By early 1987 it was describing itself in recruitment advertising as
"**Britain's Number 1 independent Entertainment Software Publisher**".

## Before the arcade

The shorthand "Elite made arcade conversions" is roughly right and gets the order wrong.

Elite's first hit was an original. ***Kokotoni Wilf*** — a time-travelling arcade adventure
with a dragon amulet — was in *Commodore Computing International*'s top twenty in **October
1984** at £6.95, and took **84%** in *CRASH*. Its most fondly remembered title is also not a
licence: ***Frank Bruno's Boxing*** (**86%**) was, as *ACE* put it plainly in 1988,
"effectively a version of the arcade scrapping game, **Punch Out**" — an unlicensed
arcade-alike sold on a **personality** licence instead. Elite bought a boxer's name because
it was cheaper than buying Nintendo's game.

That is the company in miniature. What Elite dealt in was **recognisability**, from
whichever source was selling it: coin-ops (*Commando*, *Bomb Jack*, *Ghosts 'n Goblins*,
*Paperboy*, *Space Harrier*, *Ikari Warriors*, *Buggy Boy*), television and film
(*Scooby Doo*, *Thundercats*, *The Dukes of Hazzard*, *Airwolf*), and people
(*Frank Bruno's Boxing*).

## The conversion factory, advertised in public

Most publishers of the period were coy about how games got made. Elite ran the process as a
classified advertisement in *Atari ST User*, in early 1987:

> A small number of top-flight programmers are sought by **Britain's Number 1 independent
> Entertainment Software Publisher** for exciting projects on the "Atari ST" and "Commodore
> Amiga". **Initially conversions of high-profile coin-op titles are offered**, with
> exceptional sales potential. An exceptional financial package is available to the right
> individual. For further details contact **Steve Wilcox**.

There is no myth in that. It is a specification, a contract and a rate, addressed to
freelancers — the shape the whole industry would take, printed in the back pages while
the cottage-industry version of British software was still the story everyone told about
it.

Wilcox was equally undramatic about new hardware. Asked by *8000 Plus* in October 1986
whether the Amstrad PCW was worth publishing for, he gave the answer of a man who counts:
"obviously it's an extension to the market, but **we've really got to suck it and see**."

## Best Software House and Least Pleasing Game, same poll

*Amtix*'s Readers' Awards for 1986, printed in April 1987, gave Elite **Best Software
House**, ahead of Gremlin Graphics and Gargoyle Games. *Ikari Warriors* took **Best Sound
FX**, which pleased Wilcox for a specific reason — that readers were noticing production
detail rather than the licence on the box.

Two pages later, in the same readers' poll, ***Commando* placed joint third in Least
Pleasing Game.**

⚠ The two columns are printed interleaved and the percentage figures cannot be safely
assigned to either result, so no vote share is quoted here. The placings themselves are legible and
unambiguous.

That contradiction is not an embarrassment; it is the conversion business working as
designed. *CRASH* gave the Spectrum *Commando* **94%** and a Smash. *Amtix*'s Amstrad
readers, playing a different conversion of the same licence on a different machine, ranked
it among the year's biggest letdowns. **One licence, several machines, several teams, and a
review score that says as much about which version you bought as about the game.**

## The compilation

Elite's least-remembered contribution is the one that changed how games were sold.

*ACE*, reviewing the Christmas 1988 market: "**Chief compilation station in the late
eighties has to be Elite.** It seems to us that it was the first firm to bring out a
blockbusting compilation."

*Frank Bruno's Big Box*, *Fists and Throttles*, the **Thrilltime** collections — several
full-price hits on one tape at a fraction of the combined price, "lavishly packaged in
unique collector boxes and mastered onto premium quality Gold and Platinum cassettes",
as Elite's own advertising had it, with each title on its own cassette side "for maximum
reliability".

A publisher with a deep back catalogue and no further development cost on any of it had
found a way to sell the same games twice. Every publisher copied it, and the multi-game
box became a permanent feature of the market.

## Budget, and the move to 16-bit

In May 1988 *ACE* reported Elite pushing its catalogue down to **£1.99** — *Commando*,
*Airwolf*, *Frank Bruno's Boxing*. Company spokesman **Pete Kury** was open about the plan:
"We're still examining all the options available to us, but **if we do it all in-house
we'll definitely be launching a new label**."

The money had a destination. *ACE* noted that budget revenue was intended to fund
development of more expensive 16-bit releases, and that Elite was "particularly proud of
ST *Buggy Boy* and *Space Harrier*". The three-stage lifecycle — full-price release,
compilation, budget re-release, each funding the next — is fully formed here in 1988.

## The holographic sticker

Small, and worth recording. From January 1986 Elite's advertisements carried a line no
other publisher in this library ran:

> Every single **ELITE** product carries the unique ELITE Holographic sticker. **Make sure
> your copy does** — it's your guarantee of quality.

Holograms were expensive and hard to reproduce in 1986. A publisher putting one on a
£7.95 cassette is doing what banknote printers do, and telling the customer to check —
which is a publisher who believes counterfeit copies of its games are on sale in shops,
not just taped in bedrooms.

## Afterwards

Elite survived every transition that killed its contemporaries. It was still trading in
1995, publishing *Virtuoso* on the 3DO under licence from Motivetime, from the same Anchor
House address it had used a decade earlier.

By then the press was less kind. *ACE* in December 1989, reviewing yet another Elite
release: the company's "most successful titles have always been those that are the least
complex and rely most on addictive gameplay — ***Kokotoni Wilf*, *Bombjack*, *Bombjack II*,
*Ghosts and Goblins* — remember those?**" — before dismissing the new one as "another
*Ikari Warriors* clone".

The complaint has a point and misses one. Elite was never in the business of originality;
it was in the business of putting a recognisable thing on a shelf at a price. That worked
brilliantly when the recognisable things were coin-ops nobody could own at home, and worked
progressively less well as home machines caught up with the arcades.

## Why it mattered

Elite is the corrective to the story of British software as teenage auteurs in bedrooms.
That story is true and it is not the whole industry. Alongside it ran a business of
**licences, deadlines, contract programmers and a back catalogue worked three times over** —
recognisably the modern games industry, operating from an office in Aldridge in 1986.

Its two lasting inventions were commercial rather than creative: **the compilation**, and
**the full-price-to-budget lifecycle** that funded new development from old stock. Both
outlived every game Elite published.
