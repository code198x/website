In 1985 Steve Wilcox and his father, Brian, were shown into a large hotel suite in London to bid for the home computer rights to Capcom's arcade game *[Commando](/vault/games/commando)*. Ocean's Jon Woods had told them not to bother. Brian offered £25,000; Steve, sure the game would sell in six figures, raised it to £65,000 on the spot. "My old man, to his credit, didn't even flinch," he told *Retro Gamer* twenty years later. Elite's *Commando* was the number one game that Christmas, and for the next two years the small publisher from Aldridge, near Walsall, turned out one arcade conversion after another: *[Bomb Jack](/vault/games/bomb-jack)*, *[Ghosts 'n Goblins](/vault/games/ghosts-n-goblins)*, *[Paperboy](/vault/games/paperboy)*, *[Space Harrier](/vault/games/space-harrier)*.

## Fast facts

- **Formed:** August 1984, as Elite Systems Ltd, out of Richard Wilcox Software. Companies House records the company's incorporation on 14 August 1984.
- **Run by:** [Steve Wilcox](/vault/people/steve-wilcox) and his father, Brian, who in 1987 owned the company between them. Steve's younger brother, Richard, wrote its first game.
- **Address:** Anchor House, Anchor Road, Aldridge, Walsall, from the mid-1980s to the mid-1990s; later Burntwood, Staffordshire.
- **Licences:** *Airwolf*, *The Fall Guy*, *The Dukes of Hazzard*, *Scooby Doo*, *Frank Bruno's Boxing*; coin-ops from [Capcom](/vault/companies/capcom) (*Commando*, *1942*, *Ghosts 'n Goblins*), [Tecmo](/vault/companies/tecmo) (*Bomb Jack*), [Atari Games](/vault/companies/atari-games) (*Paperboy*), [Sega](/vault/companies/sega) (*Space Harrier*) and Tatsumi (*Buggy Boy*, licensed through Data East USA).
- **Awards:** Software House of the Year in *Computer and Video Games*' Golden Joysticks and in *Amtix*'s readers' awards, both for 1986.
- ***CRASH* scores:** *Ghosts 'n Goblins* 95%, *Commando* 94%, *Bomb Jack* 92%, *Scooby Doo* 91%, *Airwolf* 90%, *Paperboy* 88%, *Frank Bruno's Boxing* 86%, *Kokotoni Wilf* 84%, *Space Harrier* 77%.

## A shop, a brother and Blue Thunder

Elite began in a computer shop. Steve Wilcox had a chemistry degree and ten months in industry behind him when he opened a shop in a Walsall back street. He called it Bowies, after his favourite musician, and by autumn 1983, *Commodore Computing International* reported, he was selling Spectrums "by the truckload". His younger brother Richard had taught himself to program on a computer their father brought home from a failed venture into business software, and had written a shoot-'em-up for the Atari 400. Their father told them they should be making games, not just selling them. Richard converted his game for the Spectrum as *Blue Thunder*, and when they took it to W H Smith's head office, Steve recalled, the buyers ordered 5,000 copies, about £12,000 worth.

*Blue Thunder* came out in 1983 under the name Richard Wilcox Software, when Richard was 15. He was still at school, so Steve hired four local teenagers to write more. Their first game, *Kokotoni Wilf*, was the first release under the new name. *CRASH* reported in October 1984 that "Elite Systems Ltd., formed from Richard Wilcox Software" had launched it, with Steve as sales and marketing manager, and gave the game 84%. It was a 63-location arcade adventure for the Spectrum at £5.95, which Elite's advertising said would "match or best *Jet Set Willy*". *Commodore Computing International* called it Elite's first Commodore game and said it reached number four in the charts.

## Paying for a name

The Wilcoxes noticed that the games that sold were the ones that looked like something from the arcade or the television. Elite's first official licence, *The Fall Guy*, was a television tie-in; "It was pretty awful," Steve Wilcox admitted. *Airwolf* was better business. By Steve Wilcox's account, Elite offered £3,000 for the licence through MCA's British agent, and kept it by appealing directly to MCA when Ocean offered more. Richard wrote the game in three weeks, *Commodore Computing International* reported, and *CRASH* gave it 90%. Accounts of its sales differ: over 200,000 in the 1987 profile, over 100,000 in Steve Wilcox's account in 2005.

*Frank Bruno's Boxing* (1985) used a person instead of a programme. Steve went to Frank Warren's gym in East London to pitch it: "None of them knew what a computer was!" *ACE* later called the game "effectively a version of the arcade scrapping game, Punch Out"; its designer, Paul Holmes, said it was "quite heavily influenced by *Super Punch-Out!!*".

## Commando and the Tokyo show

The *Commando* deal, done in August 1985, came through Electrocoin, a British arcade operator that did business with Capcom's Kenzo Tsujimoto. *Your Computer* noted that Elite's version was "developed with the aid of Capcom" and had "the cachet of being the official version". Elite defended that position hard. Alligata's *Who Dares Wins* looked very like *Commando*, and *ZZAP!64* reported in December 1985 that Elite, having bought the rights to the arcade game, had objected to it; Alligata released a *Who Dares Wins II* instead. In Wilcox's account, Elite obtained a High Court order that let it seize Alligata's stock. *Commando* shifted 200,000 copies, by his count, and on the Commodore 64 it carried music by [Rob Hubbard](/vault/people/rob-hubbard).

Before *Commando* was even out, Wilcox flew to the Tokyo game show in September 1985. He found himself, he said, the only British publisher there, phoned his father, and was told to sign everything worth having. He came back with *Paperboy*, *Space Harrier* and *Bomb Jack*, among others. Paul Holmes and Andy Williams wrote the Spectrum *Bomb Jack*; Holmes recalled locking the whole game to the 50Hz screen refresh so that it moved as smoothly as the arcade machine, and *CRASH* gave it 92%. *Ghosts 'n Goblins* scored 95%, "a genuine first rate copy of the arcade game". *Paperboy*, Wilcox said, sold more than 400,000 copies on home computers.

## Software House of the Year

1986 was Elite's best year. *Computer and Video Games*' readers voted it Software House of the Year in the Golden Joysticks, and *Amtix*'s readers did the same, ahead of Gremlin Graphics and Gargoyle Games; *Ikari Warriors* also took *Amtix*'s award for best sound effects, and *Commando* shared third place for best music. According to Wilcox, turnover rose from about £600,000 in Elite's first year to £1.5 million, and passed £3 million in 1986.

*Commodore Computing International* visited in June 1987 and found the company above a tandoori restaurant and a ladies' hairdresser, with a black Jaguar and a gold Lotus Esprit in the car park. It described 27-year-old Steve as "reputedly" the youngest software millionaire in Britain, and credited Brian, a retired foundry owner, with bringing "quality control" to games. Elite's method, it reported, was to hand each conversion to a different programmer or team for each machine so that the Spectrum, Commodore 64 and Amstrad versions could be released at once. The company was also willing to throw work away: "We've invested £50,000 in a project and thrown it away." It planned twelve releases for 1987, six of them arcade conversions. New machines had to prove themselves first. Asked by *8000 Plus* in October 1986 whether Elite would publish for Amstrad's PCW, Wilcox said: "obviously it's an extension to the market, but we've really got to suck it and see."

Much of the work was done by freelancers. In early 1987 Elite advertised in *Atari ST User* for "a small number of top-flight programmers" to work for "Britain's Number 1 independent Entertainment Software Publisher" on the Atari ST and Amiga: "Initially conversions of high-profile coin-op titles are offered." Holmes remembered the change from in-house programmers to contractors around the time of *Bomb Jack*.

Not everything was finished. Elite's *Scooby Doo* began as an attempt at a cartoon game in the style of the laserdisc arcade game *Dragon's Lair*. The Italian magazine *K* reported that Elite spent a great deal of money and time, under Richard Wilcox's direction, on this "cinematic" version before giving it up. The game that finally came out in 1986, a platform game programmed by [Gargoyle Games](/vault/companies/gargoyle-games), scored 91% in *CRASH*.

Elite also worried about fakes. From January 1986 its advertisements carried a warning: "Every single ELITE product carries the unique ELITE Holographic sticker. Make sure your copy does — it's your guarantee of quality." Holograms were still, in *CRASH*'s words a few months earlier, "a comparatively rare commodity".

## Compilations and budget re-releases

By the late 1980s Elite was selling its back catalogue again. Its compilations included *Frank Bruno's Big Box*, *Fists and Throttles* and the *Thrilltime* collections, "lavishly packaged in unique collector boxes and mastered onto premium quality Gold and Platinum cassettes", with each game on its own side "for maximum reliability". *ACE* called Elite the "chief compilation station in the late eighties" in January 1989: "It seems to us that it was the first firm to bring out a blockbusting compilation."

In May 1988 *ACE* reported that *Commando*, *Airwolf* and *Frank Bruno's Boxing* were heading for £1.99, and that the money would help pay for 16-bit games; the company was "particularly proud of ST *Buggy Boy* and *Space Harrier*". The budget label became Encore, which promised that full-price games would not reach it until three years after their release (see [Budget games](/vault/distribution/budget-games)).

Wilcox later said Elite gave up on licences when agents pushed the prices up. One of Atari's agents wanted £50,000 up front for *720°*. Elite spent 1987 to 1989 building an in-house team for original games such as *Overlander*, *Beyond the Ice Palace* and *Hopping Mad*, which by his own account sold poorly. *ACE* was unimpressed by 1989's *Dogs of War*, "another *Ikari Warriors* clone", and remembered that Elite's best games — "*Kokotoni Wilf*, *Bombjack*, *Bombjack II*, *Ghosts and Goblins*" — had been "the least complex" and relied "most on addictive gameplay".

## Consoles, PCs and phones

Wilcox had seen the Famicom's success on his trips to Japan and had a subcontractor reverse-engineer it to write NES games. Elite showed Nintendo a version of *Dragon's Lair*, developed by Motivetime, at the Consumer Electronics Show in 1990, and became one of the first European publishers of NES games. In the early 1990s it published for Nintendo's consoles and the Game Boy, with the Nintendo Seal of Quality on its advertisements. Its biggest console game was *Striker*, a football game for the Super NES made with Rage Software, which by Wilcox's account sold more than 200,000 copies. Elite also published *Virtuoso*, a Motivetime shooting game for the 3DO released in December 1994. *3DO Magazine* gave it one star: "just about the worst Doom variant around".

Wilcox said Elite lost £2 million at the end of 1994, when Sony, which was distributing European publishers' games, cut the price it would pay for new products. After that Elite worked as a developer, for the PlayStation and then the PC; *Test Drive: Off-Road*, published by Accolade, was by his account its first million-seller. In 1996 *Edge* reported that the "expansion-hungry" company was offering free drinks to students on its recruiting coach.

From about 2002 Elite turned to mobile phones, re-acquiring the rights to *Bomb Jack*, *Buggy Boy*, *Ikari Warriors* and *Paperboy*, which it licensed from Midway Games West, the successor to Atari Games. "We're still here today, 21 years on, and I don't imagine I'll ever do anything else," Wilcox told *Retro Gamer* in 2005. In 2014 Elite sought £60,000 on Kickstarter for the Recreated ZX Spectrum, a Bluetooth keyboard in the shape of a Spectrum 48K, for playing its Spectrum games on phones and tablets. Elite Systems Limited is still an active company.
