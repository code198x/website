Thalion Software, founded in 1988 in Germany, is one of the clearest cases of the scene staffing the industry. Its people and its sensibility came out of the Atari ST and Amiga scene, and it showed: Thalion games tended to push the hardware the way a good demo would, then wrap that technical flair around real design ambition.

The studio's catalogue ran from the sprawling RPGs *Amberstar* and *Ambermoon* to the lavish platformer *Lionheart* and the speed-blurred *No Second Prize*. Behind them stood scene talent like the musician Jochen "Mad Max" Hippel, whose chip and synth work was scene-famous before it was game-famous.

## Fast facts

- **Active:** 1988–1994, Germany.
- **Platforms:** Atari ST, Amiga.
- **Best known for:** *Amberstar* / *Ambermoon*, *Lionheart*, *No Second Prize*.
- **Scene link:** Staffed by Atari ST / Amiga sceners, including Jochen Hippel.

## Why Thalion mattered

Thalion is a reminder that the scene-to-studio pipeline wasn't only a Scandinavian or Finnish story — the German scene fed it too. The studio's reputation for squeezing beauty out of 16-bit hardware came directly from the demoscene habits its staff brought with them.

## Crackers who went legitimate

*ACE* profiled Thalion in February 1990 and opened with the question the company posed:

> What do you get when a group of young **ex-hackers** decide to produce their own games? A
> software company with the expertise and potential to write a major blockbusting game.

The magazine is explicit about where the staff came from: **Thalion was set up in 1988**,
and "its origins are firmly rooted in **the underground subculture of the games hacking and
cracking**" scene.

That is a rarer transition than it sounds. The European cracking scene of the mid-eighties
produced enormous technical skill and had no legal route to market; Thalion is the case
where a group made the crossing intact — taking the demoscene's habits of
show-what-the-hardware-can-do into commercial development, including a Swiss programmer,
**Christian**, credited with the filled-vector 3D routines.

The name comes from **Tolkien's *Silmarillion***, which *ACE* thought "perhaps fitting" for
a company opening with a fantasy role-playing game — ***Dragonflight***, described as an
epic "which puts even the likes of *Ultima* and *Dungeon Master* to shame".

## Chambers of Shaolin

*ACE* reviewed it in April 1990 (Grandslam/Thalion, across ST, Amiga, Spectrum, C64 and
Amstrad) and singled out a design decision worth recording:

> **instead of ignoring the learning curve, German software house Thalion has actually made
> a feature of it.**

The game runs in two halves. First you build a character's skills through **six training
chambers**; then you save him to disk and enter him in the fighting contest proper. *ACE*'s
verdict was that neither half outweighed the other — "**you've got two excellent games which
are practically independent of one another**".

A beat-'em-up that makes the practice mode into a separate, savable game is the demoscene
instinct applied to design rather than graphics: the interesting part is the part where you
get better at the machine.
