Thalion grew out of one of the first demo groups on the [Atari ST](/vault/systems/atari-st). Two of its three founders, Udo Fischer and Erik Simon, belonged to The Exceptions, the German group known as TEX, and Fischer had written what he described as the first demo released for the ST, in 1986. *[ACE](/vault/magazines/ace-magazine)* introduced the company in 1990 as "a group of young ex-hackers" with "the expertise and potential to write a major blockbusting game". In five years in the small town of Gütersloh, Thalion made role-playing games, platform games and a fast 3D motorbike race for the ST and the [Amiga](/vault/systems/commodore-amiga). Its development team left at the end of 1993, and by the middle of 1994 the company was bankrupt.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** October 1988, in Gütersloh, West Germany, by Holger Flottmann, Udo Fischer and Erik Simon.
- **The name:** taken, *ACE* reported, from Tolkien's *Silmarillion*.
- **Scene roots:** Fischer ("-ME-"), Simon ("ES") and the musician Jochen Hippel ("[Mad Max](/vault/people/mad-max)") were members of The Exceptions, an early Atari ST demo group.
- **Games:** *Seven Gates of Jambala* and *Chambers of Shaolin* (1989–90, with Grandslam in Britain), *Dragonflight* (1990), *Wings of Death*, *Amberstar* (1992), *No Second Prize* (1992), *[Lionheart](/vault/games/lionheart)* (1993) and *Ambermoon* (1993).
- **German distribution:** [Ariolasoft](/vault/companies/ariolasoft) handled its games around 1990.
- **End:** most of the development team moved to Blue Byte at the start of 1994; Thalion was bankrupt by June 1994.

## The Exceptions

The French magazine *[ST Magazine](/vault/magazines/st-magazine)* devoted its demo pages to The Exceptions in November 1991 and called them the first demo group on the ST. Udo Fischer, who signed his work "-ME-", told the magazine that he had started on a Commodore 64, writing 68000 routines on paper and assembling them by hand before he owned a 68000 machine. When he got his ST he typed them in as BASIC pokes. In 1986 he wrote what he called the first demo released on the ST: "a picture and a scroller, not much really!" (in translation). His *Little Demos* and *BIG Demo* followed. Then he stopped, he said, because he was spending his time programming *Dragonflight*. Jochen Hippel, "Mad Max", had converted the Commodore 64 music used in the *BIG Demo*, and the group's graphic artist was "ES", Erik Simon.

*ACE* described Thalion's origins more bluntly in February 1990: they were "firmly rooted in the underground subculture of the games hacking and cracking". Co-founder Erik Simon told the magazine that most of the programmers came from that scene, and that "these ex-crackers are technically superb – but they find it more interesting to code rather than crack".

## A company in Gütersloh

*ACE* visited Gütersloh, which it called a picturesque German town about two hours' drive from Düsseldorf airport, where the team worked together and also met at a local bar a few minutes from the office. The magazine thought it fitting that a company named after the *Silmarillion* should start with a fantasy role-playing game. That was *Dragonflight*, which Fischer and Simon had begun as a private project a year before Thalion existed and which took another year and a half of full-time work to finish. *ACE* promised readers an epic "which puts even the likes of *Ultima* and *Dungeon Master* to shame". Fischer later admitted that the long development had been a lesson: he had changed assembler halfway through and had to convert all the code, and a game designed three years earlier was no longer up to date.

Thalion's music came from the same scene. Jochen Hippel wrote for its games, and *[The One](/vault/magazines/the-one)* referred to him in 1992 simply as "Thalion musician Jochen Hippel".

## Chambers of Shaolin

The early action games reached Britain through Grandslam. *ACE* reviewed *Chambers of Shaolin* in April 1990 and singled out its structure:

> Instead of ignoring the learning curve, German software house Thalion has actually made a feature of it.

The game runs in two halves. First the player builds up a fighter's skills in six training chambers, each devoted to an ability such as balance, speed or strength. Then the fighter is saved to disk and entered in the contest proper. *ACE*'s reviewer found the animation only adequate and the sound uneventful. But "once you've played through the Chambers once, you've got two excellent games which are practically independent of one another".

## No Second Prize and Lionheart

In 1990 *ACE* named *No Second Prize* as perhaps Thalion's most exciting project, a motorbike race with "revolutionary fast solid 3D graphics". The routines were already finished, and it was expected that autumn. It came out at the end of 1992. Its programmer was Christian Jungen, a Swiss who had become fascinated with 3D after buying *Elite* and who, *The One* reported, gave up a computer science course at ETH Zurich after half a year to program 3D for games.

*Lionheart* (1993) was a large, colourful Amiga platform game drawn by the Dutch artist [Henk Nieborg](/vault/people/henk-nieborg) and programmed by the Austrian Erwin Kloibhofer. Kloibhofer told *[Amiga Joker](/vault/magazines/amiga-joker)* how they came to Thalion. They knew each other from demo parties, "the scene is international, after all". Kloibhofer left home for the Netherlands, where the two made a small platform game called *Ghost Battle* and offered it to Thalion. It was never released, "but it got us jobs in Gütersloh" (in translation). *Lionheart* followed.

## Amberstar and Ambermoon

Thalion's role-playing games grew alongside its action games. *Amberstar* was published for the ST in 1992 and *Ambermoon*, its sequel, for the Amiga in 1993, with Erik Simon as producer. In a 1993 interview with *The One* Simon described the German market. Simulations and huge role-playing games, he said, were well accepted by German players, while action games such as *Lionheart* had done well there only for a short time. Piracy, he added, was "constantly pushing us towards the PC". *Ambermoon* had special routines to speed up its texture-mapped dungeons on the Amiga 1200.

*The One* summed up the company's attitude in the same feature: "No-one involved has become immensely rich or drives a Porsche."

## Blue Byte, and bankruptcy

The end came quickly. In February 1994 *[Amiga Format](/vault/magazines/amiga-format)* and *[CU Amiga](/vault/magazines/cu-amiga)* reported that Erik Simon, Jurie Horneman, Thorsten Mutschall, Christian Jungen and Tobias Franz, the team behind games from *Dragonflight* to *Ambermoon*, had left Thalion at the end of 1993 for the German publisher Blue Byte. *Amiga Format* found Thalion's British office still "bullish" about the company's future. In May 1994 *Amiga Joker* reported that, with the programmers gone to Blue Byte, CD conversions of *No Second Prize*, *Lionheart* and *Ambermoon* were being made in England. In June it told a reader that Thalion, "despite some very successful products", had gone bankrupt (in translation). By the end of the year Kloibhofer and Nieborg were working with [Psygnosis](/vault/companies/psygnosis) on *The Misadventures of Flink*.

## Released source code

In May 2023 Jurie Horneman, who had programmed both role-playing games, published the surviving source code of *Amberstar* and *Ambermoon* on GitHub, together with the games' German and English texts and development documents. The *Ambermoon* code lacks the base libraries for loading files, drawing graphics and managing memory, he wrote, but the majority of the gameplay logic is there. The repositories carry no licence.
