## Overview

*Amiga Format*'s industry A-Z gives Zeppelin in one paragraph, and it is a better company profile than most feature articles: **formed in Newcastle in 1987 by Dr Derek Brewster, Martin O'Donnell and Brian Jobling**, releasing more than **six titles a month**, one of the top five British budget houses by market share, with thirteen staff and a stable of freelancers.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** Newcastle, 1987.
- **Prices:** **£2.99 and £3.99** — squarely the second budget tier, above Mastertronic's £1.99.
- **Three labels at once:** Zeppelin Games (budget 8-bit), Zeppelin Platinum (budget 16-bit), Zeppelin Premier (full price).
- **Published:** *Ball Blasta*, *Sink or Swim*, *International Soccer*, and *Kenny Dalglish Soccer Manager* — its first full-price release, made with [Impressions](/vault/companies/impressions) in 1990.
- **Became:** Eutechnyx.


## Who founded it
*Amiga Format*'s company listing gives the line-up in one clause: a "software
house formed in Newcastle in 1987 by **Dr Derek Brewster, Martin O'Donnell and
Brian Jobling**".

⚠ That Martin O'Donnell is not the American composer of the same name.

[Derek Brewster](/vault/people/derek-brewster) came from *CRASH*, where he wrote
the adventure column — and *ACE* photographed him in March 1988 captioned
"Derek Brewster surrounded by the Zeppelin crew", so the crossing from press to
industry was public and drew no comment at all.

The crew was strikingly young. *Atari User* in February 1988 describes
Zeppelin's software director **Brian Jobling, aged 19**, and credits him as the
author of the company's *Speed Ace*. A nineteen-year-old running software
development at a publisher shipping several titles a month is not an anecdote
about Zeppelin; it is a fact about how young the whole British industry was in
1988.

## A publishing machine

Six titles a month is the number to sit with. At that rate a company is not making games so much as **running a production line**, and the shape of the business follows: a small core staff, a large freelance pool, and a standing advertisement.

Zeppelin's recruitment copy ran in the magazines alongside its games — a call for freelance programmers and graphic artists, promising the best payment terms "of any software house" to those whose work met its standards. That is a publisher buying finished work by the piece instead of employing developers, and it was how much of the budget end operated.

Running three labels at three price points, simultaneously, on both 8-bit and 16-bit, is the other half. By 1992 a British publisher had to decide what it was: cheap or expensive, old machines or new. Zeppelin's answer was **all of them at once**, which is unusual and evidently worked.

## Derek Brewster

One of the three founders is worth stopping on. Dr Derek Brewster wrote *CRASH*'s adventure column — the person readers wrote to for help getting past a locked door in a text adventure.

The British industry was small enough that the man reviewing adventures in Ludlow could co-found a software house in Newcastle, and nobody thought it remarkable enough to comment on. The line between press and industry, which later became a matter of policy, barely existed.

## What happened next

Zeppelin's founders did not leave the business. The company became **Eutechnyx**, which spent the following two decades making racing games — Ferrari, NASCAR, licensed work at a scale a £2.99 cassette house would not have recognised. Brian Jobling's brother Darren, listed here as Zeppelin's operations director, was still running it.

That continuity is the point. A Newcastle budget label from 1987 turns into a console-era racing studio without a break, and the connective tissue is **the same few people staying in the same city doing the next thing**.

## Why it mattered

Zeppelin is the **industrial end of budget publishing** — not the romance of a bedroom coder getting a game into shops, but the logistics of shipping six of them a month, every month, for years.

It is also one of the clearest survivor threads in this Vault. Most British publishers of the eighties ended in an acquisition or an administration; a few carried on, changed what they made, and are traceable in an unbroken line from a £2.99 cassette to a licensed racing game.
