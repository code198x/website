For its first ten years Bungie made games for the Apple Macintosh, a computer most games publishers treated as an afterthought. It was a small Chicago company, and its games were good enough that Mac owners who had watched PC players enjoy *Doom* treated *Marathon* almost as a religion, and that in 1999 Steve Jobs used Bungie's next game to show off Apple's new machines. Within a year Microsoft had bought the studio, and that game, *[Halo](/vault/games/halo)*, went on sale as the launch game of the [Xbox](/vault/systems/microsoft-xbox). Bungie left Microsoft in 2007, built *Destiny* with Activision, and has been owned by [Sony](/vault/companies/sony) since 2022.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1991, in Chicago, by Alex Seropian and Jason Jones.
- **Early games (Macintosh):** *Minotaur: The Labyrinths of Crete* (1992), *Pathways into Darkness* (1993), *Marathon* (at the turn of 1995), *Marathon 2: Durandal* (1995).
- **Strategy games:** *Myth: The Fallen Lords* (1997) and *Myth II*, for Mac and Windows.
- **Owned by [Microsoft](/vault/companies/microsoft):** June 2000 to October 2007. It made *Halo: Combat Evolved* (2001) and its sequels up to *Halo: Reach* (2010).
- **Independent:** 2007 to 2022, with a publishing contract with [Activision](/vault/companies/activision) for *Destiny* (2014) that ended in January 2019.
- **Owned by [Sony](/vault/companies/sony):** since July 2022, bought for $3.6 billion.

## Minotaur and Pathways

Bungie's early games included *Gnop!*, but the one that got it noticed was designed by Jason Jones for players on a network. *Minotaur: The Labyrinths of Crete*, which *Computer Gaming World* reviewed in October 1992, needed "a connection to at least one other Macintosh via modem, cable, or AppleTalk", and took two to seven players, each on their own Mac, for $89.99. The publisher was given as "Bungie Software Products Corporation, Chicago, IL". A year later the magazine nominated it for its Action Game of the Year award "because of its exciting multi-player capacity", calling it "a multi-player network Rogue for the Macintosh environment".

*Pathways into Darkness* (1993) went first-person. The player is an American special forces soldier who has to carry a nuclear device into a pyramid in the Central American jungle, but the parachute drop goes wrong and the soldier lands alone, with a map, a flashlight and a broken rifle. *Computer Gaming World* summed it up as "So many monsters, so little time". It played, the same magazine said later, "more like an adventure game", with items to find and puzzles to solve.

## Marathon

By 1994 Mac owners had watched PC players enjoy *[Doom](/vault/games/doom)* for a year. *Computer Gaming World*'s Christopher Breen described Mac bulletin boards full of threads titled "Where is Mac-DOOM?" and "Why no DOOM for Mac?". Bungie's answer was *Marathon*. After months of delay, Breen wrote, the full game was released to coincide with the Macworld Expo in San Francisco in January 1995, where Bungie ran a "Kill-a-thon" on networked Macs on its stand.

The player is a security officer aboard the colony ship *Marathon*, which has been invaded by an alien race, the Pfhor, and whose computer has been taken over by a rogue artificial intelligence called Durandal. It was a *Doom*-style shooter with more to think about. The player had a motion detector, and on one level an oxygen supply to watch. Unlike in *Doom*, you could aim up and down, and players discovered "grenade hopping", firing a grenade at the floor to be thrown up onto a ledge. There were 26 levels for one player and ten network scenarios, and every box included a second, network-only installation, so that two people could play on one copy. "If you have a Macintosh, you probably already own this game," Breen wrote. "If you're a PC user, eat your heart out."

*Edge* said *Marathon* "did for Macs what *Doom* did for PCs", and that "Mac owners world wide went crazy for the game, with *Marathon* add-ons sprouting all over the Internet". The sequel, *Marathon 2: Durandal*, came out at the end of 1995; *Edge* reviewed it in March 1996 at £54.99 and called its predecessor "the best-selling Mac game ever". It added water to swim through, co-operative play for up to eight people and new multiplayer games such as "kill the man with the ball". Bungie liked the name Durandal enough to use it twice: it had been the name of a mythical sword in *Minotaur* before it was the name of *Marathon*'s AI.

In January 2000 Bungie released the source code of the *Marathon 2* engine under the GNU General Public Licence. Fans built an open-source engine, Aleph One, from it, and in 2005 Bungie allowed the three *Marathon* games to be given away free. *Retro Gamer* reported in 2004 that the games "continue to be developed by the open-source movement to this day".

## Myth

Bungie's next games were not shooters. *Myth: The Fallen Lords* was a real-time battle game played across a 3D landscape, and Bungie released it for Windows as well as the Mac. "The problem is that when you say 'realtime strategy,' people think of *Command & Conquer*," Jason Jones, co-founder and lead programmer and designer, told *Edge* in March 1997. "Myth's going to be different... The emphasis is on tactics and combat. This is a game where you fight, period." There was no base to build. Every arrow followed a real arc through the air: "the archers have to solve a fourth degree polynomial," Jones explained. *Computer Gaming World*'s review in February 1998 said *Myth* "strips the standard real-time strategy game to the bone", leaving "brutal and bloody combat". *PC Player* visited Bungie's Chicago office late in 1998 to see the sequel, *Myth II*.

## Halo and Microsoft

In July 1999 Steve Jobs showed *Halo* during his Macworld Expo keynote. "At that point, Bungie was sort of a boutique favourite company for the Mac gamers," the studio's music director Marty O'Donnell told *Edge* years later. "When Steve Jobs decided he would highlight *Halo*... it just sort of changed on a dime the public perception of Bungie."

It also caught Microsoft's attention. On 19 June 2000 Bungie agreed to be bought by Microsoft. Its executive vice-president, Peter Tamte, told *Computer Gaming World* that Bungie was not in financial trouble and that other companies had offered more money: "Why sell? For the opportunity to play a key role in the launch of the X-Box." Take-Two Interactive, which had owned 19.9 per cent of Bungie, received cash, the *Myth* games, the unfinished action game *Oni* and the right to make two games with the *Halo* engine. Microsoft took *Halo* and the rest of the catalogue, including *Marathon*, and Bungie moved to Redmond, near Seattle. "Mac gamers crushed," ran the magazine's headline.

*Halo: Combat Evolved* went on sale with the Xbox in North America on 15 November 2001, and *Edge* gave it ten out of ten. The review singled out the rules that made it play differently from earlier shooters. The Master Chief could carry only two weapons at once, "a surprising concession to realism". His shield "starts recharging moments after the player has stopped taking damage", so finding cover mattered, while his health did not recover. The game saved automatically at checkpoints, and he could drive a jeep with marines aboard. *Edge* compared its multiplayer with Rare's *[GoldenEye 007](/vault/games/goldeneye-007)*, the old standard for console shooters, and concluded that it had been surpassed. The *[Halo](/vault/games/halo)* entry tells the game's story in full.

*Halo 2* followed in November 2004, and the writer and design director Joseph Staten later called its "bitter end" a key moment in the studio's history: "We tasted the heights of success, but also the bitterness of overreach... With *Halo 2* the weight of the world was on our shoulders, and we didn't do as good a job as we could have." *Halo 3* came out in September 2007.

## Independence and Destiny

In October 2007 Microsoft confirmed that Bungie would become an independent company again. Microsoft kept the rights to *Halo*, and Bungie kept a publishing agreement with it. Bungie made two more *Halo* games for Microsoft: *Halo 3: ODST* (2009) and *Halo: Reach* (2010), which *Edge* called "Bungie's last hurrah with *Halo*". Microsoft gave the series to its own new studio, 343 Industries.

In 2010 Bungie signed a ten-year publishing contract with Activision, keeping the rights to the new game it would make. By 2013 it had moved into a converted multiplex cinema in Bellevue, near Seattle, with 350 staff, no closed offices and desks on wheels, so that, in Pete Parsons's words, the studio could "move the team at will". That February it revealed *Destiny*, an online science-fiction shooter set some 700 years in Earth's future, which it intended to be a long-running series like *Halo*. *Destiny* was released on 9 September 2014, and *Destiny 2* in 2017. Both were sold as ongoing games, with paid expansions about twice a year. In January 2019 Bungie and Activision ended their partnership, and Bungie took back the publishing rights to *Destiny*. Keza MacDonald noted in *Wireframe* that Activision had spent "more than half a billion" dollars funding and marketing the game, and that, unlike *Halo*, Bungie kept this one.

## Sony

On 31 January 2022 Sony announced that it would buy Bungie for $3.6 billion. Sony's statement at the time said that "Bungie's world-class expertise in multi-platform development and live game services" would help it expand PlayStation. The purchase was completed on 15 July 2022. Bungie became an independent subsidiary within Sony, free to keep making games for other companies' consoles.

The years since have been hard. Bungie released a new game called *Marathon*, an online extraction shooter, on 5 March 2026 for Windows, PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X|S. Sony later reduced the value it placed on Bungie by more than $700 million. *Destiny 2*'s last major content update, *Monument of Triumph*, came out on 9 June 2026, and on 25 June Sony announced layoffs affecting "most" of the *Destiny* team and "some" of the *Marathon* team. A notice filed with Washington State listed 292 jobs, and the studio head, Justin Truman, stepped down. In September 2026 Bungie's new leadership said that *Marathon* "did not reach as many players as we had hoped", promised to rework it, and said it would bring back parts of *Destiny 2* that had been removed from the game.
