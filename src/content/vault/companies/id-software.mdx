In 1990 a group of programmers at Softdisk, a software subscription company in Shreveport, Louisiana, spent a weekend making *Super Mario Bros. 3* scroll smoothly on an IBM PC. PCs were not supposed to do that. They sent the demonstration to Nintendo, which was impressed but wanted Mario kept on its own machines. So they used the trick for a game of their own, *Commander Keen*, and in February 1991 four of them left to form id Software. *Wolfenstein 3D* made it, in *Computer Gaming World*'s words, the "king of the action-game hill", and *Doom* carried its games onto office networks.

id's story is mostly told through two people: [John Carmack](/vault/people/john-carmack), who wrote the engines, and [John Romero](/vault/people/john-romero), who designed levels and wrote tools. But it was a small team throughout, and the way it sold its games mattered as much as the code.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1 February 1991, in Shreveport, Louisiana.
- **Founders:** [John Carmack](/vault/people/john-carmack), [John Romero](/vault/people/john-romero), Adrian Carmack (no relation) and Tom Hall.
- **Moved:** to Madison, Wisconsin, in September 1991; to Mesquite, Texas, on 1 April 1992; to Richardson, Texas, in 2011.
- **Key games:** *Commander Keen* (1990–91), *Wolfenstein 3D* (1992), [Doom](/vault/games/doom) (1993), *Doom II* (1994), [Quake](/vault/games/quake) (1996), *Quake II* (1997), *Quake III Arena* (1999), *Doom 3* (2004), *Doom* (2016).
- **Owners:** independent until ZeniMax Media, the parent of [Bethesda Softworks](/vault/companies/bethesda), bought it in June 2009; [Microsoft](/vault/companies/microsoft) bought ZeniMax in 2021.
- **Source code:** released for its engines from *Wolfenstein 3D* onwards, including *Doom* in 1997 and *Quake* in 1999.

## From Softdisk

Softdisk's games division, Gamer's Edge, sold a subscription disk of new games for IBM PC compatibles; by December 1991 Softdisk advertised it at $10 a month, "every month". Romero, the Carmacks and Hall worked there. Calling themselves Ideas from the Deep, they used Carmack's smooth-scrolling technique, "adaptive tile refresh", for *Commander Keen in Invasion of the Vorticons*, which came out in December 1990. It was published by [Apogee](/vault/companies/apogee-software), [Scott Miller](/vault/people/scott-miller)'s shareware company: the first episode was free to copy, and players paid for the rest.

The four left [Softdisk](/vault/companies/softdisk) on 1 February 1991 and called their company id. Softdisk had not been happy that the *Keen* work was done on its computers, and id agreed to keep supplying Gamer's Edge with games, at $5,000 each, until the end of January 1992. Those contract games became id's laboratory. *Hovertank One* (May 1991) put the player inside a tank in a maze of flat-coloured walls. *Catacomb 3-D* (November 1991) added textures to the walls and put the player in a battle mage's shoes. Tom Hall later wrote that each of them "could be considered the first FPS". Meanwhile *Commander Keen: Goodbye, Galaxy!* reached Apogee's customers on 15 December 1991.

## Wolfenstein and Doom

In September 1991 the team moved to Madison, Wisconsin, where Hall and Jason Blochowiak had been at school, and in January 1992 started a game based on the Apple II classic *Castle Wolfenstein*. *Wolfenstein 3D* took four months and went out through Apogee on 5 May 1992, its first episode shareware. Tired of the Wisconsin winter, id had moved to Mesquite, a suburb of Dallas, on 1 April. "Commander Keen made good money," Hall recalled. "Wolfenstein 3D made TEN TIMES that." GT Interactive later sold it in a box.

With *Doom* id published for itself. It gave away the first episode, took orders on 1-800-ID-GAMES and let shops sell the free episode and keep the money. *Computer Gaming World* named Jay Wilbur as id's chief executive. When a *Doom* craze broke out on company networks, he had a line ready for the magazine: "If you haven't played multiplayer DOOM, you haven't played DOOM!"

## The team comes apart

Success did not keep the founders together. Hall left during *Doom*, in August 1993, over creative differences, and went to Apogee. Romero left in 1996, after *Quake*, and by January 1997 *Computer Gaming World* was reporting "John Romero's departure and Jay Wilbur's retirement" in the same sentence. Romero and Hall set up Ion Storm together in Dallas. In November 1996 the magazine named Carmack tenth among the industry's most influential people: without his "programming tricks and gaming instincts", it said, "it is questionable whether Wolfenstein 3D, Doom and Quake would have been made".

id brought its fans to Texas: [QuakeCon](/vault/events/quakecon), a players' gathering near its offices from August 1996, became an annual event that id paid for and ran.

## Engines for hire, code for everyone

id sold its technology to other studios. Raven Software, in Wisconsin, used the *Quake* engine for *Hexen II*; Ritual Entertainment announced *Sin* on it and moved to the *Quake II* engine before release; and Valve built *[Half-Life](/vault/games/half-life)* on an enhanced version. *Computer Gaming World* observed that "even games from other companies such as Dark Forces and Duke Nukem 3D owe much to the basic design of Carmack's games". The engine lineage is described under [id Tech](/vault/tools/id-tech).

id made a habit of publishing an engine's source code once it was a few years old. *Doom*'s code came out on 23 December 1997, "for your non-profit use" in Carmack's words, and was relicensed under the GNU General Public License in 1999. *Quake*'s followed under the GPL on 21 December 1999, then *Quake II* in 2001, *Quake III Arena* in 2005 and *Doom 3* in 2011. Fabien Sanglard, who has written books on both engines, notes that from 1993 to 2012 id released the code of every game it produced. The *Doom* code alone produced hundreds of "source ports", from Boom and ZDoom to Chocolate Doom, several still developed today.

## Under new owners

*Doom 3*, in August 2004, took the series towards slow-paced horror and sold more than 3.5 million copies by 2007. In June 2009 ZeniMax Media, the owner of Bethesda Softworks, bought id. Carmack became chief technology officer of Oculus VR in August 2013 and left id that November; *Wireframe* noted that most of "the big names" had gone by then, and that id "fell into relative obscurity" while its new *Doom* took years to appear.

It returned with *Doom* in 2016, praised for going back to speed, and *Doom Eternal* in 2020. In September 2020 Microsoft agreed to buy ZeniMax for $7.5 billion, and the purchase was completed in March 2021. Since then id has released an enhanced edition of *Quake* (2021), made with Nightdive Studios, *Doom + Doom II* (2024) and *Doom: The Dark Ages* (2025). QuakeCon is still held every August near Dallas.
