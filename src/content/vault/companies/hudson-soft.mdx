Hudson Soft nearly became a café. In 1973 the brothers Yuji and Hiroshi Kudo wanted to start a business in Sapporo, on the northern island of Hokkaido, and, as *Wireframe* tells it, were not especially fussy about what it sold. There was already a similar business in the same building, so they opened CQ Hudson instead, a shop dealing mostly in amateur radio equipment. Hudson's own John Greiner later remembered them selling "telecommunication devices and some art photographs". The name came from the brothers' favourite steam locomotive.

Within two years the shop was selling microcomputers, and within ten Hudson was one of the first two companies Nintendo let make games for the Famicom. It went on to create *Bomberman*, design the chips inside NEC's PC Engine and make *Mario Party* for Nintendo, before a bank collapse and a stock-market listing brought it under [Konami](/vault/companies/konami), which absorbed it in 2012.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1973, in Sapporo, by Yuji and Hiroshi Kudo.
- **First business:** CQ Hudson, a shop selling amateur radio equipment.
- **Known for:** [Bomberman](/vault/games/bomberman), [Adventure Island](/vault/games/adventure-island), [Bonk](/vault/games/bonk), the Star Soldier shooters, the Caravan tournaments, *Mario Party*.
- **Hardware:** designed the chipset of the [PC Engine](/vault/systems/pc-engine) with NEC.
- **Ended:** absorbed into Konami Digital Entertainment on 1 March 2012.

## From radio shop to software house

The shop began selling early computers such as NEC's TK-80, Sharp's MZ-80K and the Commodore PET. There was little software to run on them, so Hudson wrote its own. "The games we made were tiny," one of its first employees, Takashi Takebe, recalled in the book *Japansoft: An Oral History*. "We would include five to ten of them as a bonus with the purchase of a new computer." Hudson then sold its programs on cassette by mail order through magazine adverts, opened a second office in Tokyo, and ported its programs to the many incompatible Japanese computers of the time. Greiner claimed that Hudson was "the first to publish a PC game in Japan"; Japanese sources date its first packaged game software to 1978.

*Edge* found in 1995 that Hudson had first made its name with "programming languages and utility software", and only then turned to games. One of those tools produced its most famous game. Hudson's director of research and development, Shinichi Nakamoto, told *Edge* that the original *Bomberman* began in 1980 as a demonstration of the company's own BASIC compiler, and came out in Japan in a small way as a one-player computer game with a single type of bomb and a single enemy. *Wireframe* dates the first *Bomberman* to 1983 and credits the programmer Yuji Tanaka.

British Spectrum owners met Hudson in 1984, when Sinclair published two of its games at £5.95 each. *CRASH* reviewed both in July 1984, introducing Hudson as "the Japanese company who have been releasing games in Britain". *Stop the Express* had the player running along the carriage roofs of a hijacked train. *Eric and the Floaters* was the *Bomberman* idea under another name, an underground maze in which Eric lays bombs that go off "about two and a half seconds" later; *CRASH* gave it 60 per cent, and *Your Computer* found it "semi-addictive" but "a rather ordinary game".

## The Famicom

Nintendo's Famicom went on sale in 1983, and Hudson signed up early. With [Namco](/vault/companies/namco) it was one of the first two outside companies licensed to make Famicom games, and its first cartridges appeared in July 1984: the platform game *Nuts & Milk* and a conversion of *[Lode Runner](/vault/games/lode-runner)*. The Famicom *Lode Runner* added a scrolling view and gave the player a character that later became Bomberman. Hudson also wrote the software for *Family BASIC*, the keyboard and cartridge that turned the Famicom into a home computer, and converted Nintendo games such as *Balloon Fight* and *Mario Bros.* to the NEC PC-8801 and Sharp X1. By the middle of the decade, *Wireframe* reports, its Famicom versions of *Lode Runner* and *Bomberman* had each sold over a million copies.

Hudson made its own stars too. A marketing executive, Toshiyuki Takahashi, became famous as "Takahashi Meijin" (Master Takahashi) for how fast he could press a fire button, and appeared in comics and on television. From 1985 Hudson took him around Japan in the Caravan, a touring tournament in which players competed on one game a year in timed bouts of two to five minutes. The first Caravan game was *Star Force*, Tecmo's shooter, which Hudson had converted for the Famicom. Every Caravan game from 1985 to 1992 except one was a shooter. Hudson's own *Star Soldier* (1986) refined *Star Force*, and many of its sequels were Caravan games with timed "Caravan" score-attack modes built in.

Takahashi also got his own game. Hudson licensed Escape's arcade game *Wonder Boy* (the developer later renamed itself Westone), kept its design and put Takahashi in the lead. It came out on the Famicom in 1986 as *Takahashi Meijin no Bōken Jima*, "Master Takahashi's Adventure Island", and reached the West as *Adventure Island*, with the hero renamed Master Higgins. Sega owned the Wonder Boy name but Westone owned the game, which is how the same design could appear under two companies' labels. Hudson's *Adventure Island* series then went its own way.

## The PC Engine

By the mid-1980s Hudson was designing its own graphics chips. *Wireframe* reports that it first tried to sell one to Nintendo, without success. NEC wanted to enter the console business but, as *Retro Gamer* put it, "lacked vital industry experience". In Greiner's words, Hudson "realised they needed a partner to manufacture and market to a large base. Fortuitous timing landed NEC as a company that was interested in entering the console market."

The result was the [PC Engine](/vault/systems/pc-engine), launched in Japan in October 1987. Its chips carried Hudson part numbers: the HuC6280 processor, a 6502 relative with a memory mapper and a six-channel sound generator on the same die, the HuC6270 video controller and the HuC6260 colour encoder. The games came on HuCards the size of a credit card. In December 1988 the machine gained a CD-ROM drive, the first for any games console. The PC Engine ran second to Nintendo in Japan and failed in America as the TurboGrafx-16.

Hudson was the machine's most important publisher. It converted Irem's *[R-Type](/vault/games/r-type)*, made the caveman platformer *[Bonk](/vault/games/bonk)* (*PC Genjin*, 1989) and the shooter *Soldier Blade* (1992), and brought *Bomberman* to the PC Engine. The PC Engine's multitap, which let five joypads plug into one console, turned *Bomberman* into a party game. "We were really lucky that the PC Engine had a multitap," Nakamoto told *Edge*. "Without it, the PC Engine version would have been merely a reproduction, and would probably soon have been forgotten."

Hudson kept pushing the player count. *Hi-Ten Bomberman*, made for the 1993 Caravan, ran on Hudson's own hardware and an early high-definition television and took ten players at once. *Edge*, visiting Sapporo for its February 1995 issue, called it "arguably the best multiplayer game in the world". Ten-player battles reached homes with *Saturn Bomberman* (1996) on Sega's Saturn.

## Sapporo in the 1990s

*Edge* found Hudson "not your average Japanese videogames outfit", with interests "as diverse as television and movie production and coin-op museums". Its research centre on the outskirts of Sapporo had a miniature railway that staff could ride around the building, and its bee logo was an amateur radio joke. The radio call sign for Hokkaido is JA8, and in Japanese the word for eight, *hachi*, also means bee.

The PC Engine's successor went less well. NEC's PC-FX came out in Japan on 9 December 1994. Hudson's PC-FX team made the anime fighting game *Battle Heat*, released alongside it, and the adventure *Team Innocent*, but *Edge* reported that NEC wanted the machine's software built around anime, so games like *Bomberman* would not appear on it. *Wireframe* reports that the PC-FX sold only 400,000 before it was dropped in 1998. Hudson kept working for Nintendo: it developed *Mario Party* for the Nintendo 64, a board game of four-player mini-games. *Edge* gave it seven out of ten in March 1999, finding it thin alone but saying that "as a console multiplayer experience there is little that can claim to be its equal". Hudson went on to make a whole series of sequels.

## Konami

The late 1990s hit Hudson hard. Its main bank, Hokkaido Takushoku, failed in November 1997, and Hudson's finances suffered with it. The company raised money by listing on the stock market in 2000, which, *Wireframe* notes, left it open to a takeover. In 2001 Konami bought about 5.6 million Hudson shares. In November 2004, facing a forecast loss of ¥7.3 billion, Hiroshi Kudo resigned as president, taking responsibility for the losses; Konami then held 38 per cent. Konami took a majority in 2005, made Hudson a wholly owned subsidiary in April 2011 and merged it into Konami Digital Entertainment on 1 March 2012.

Konami kept the games. *Super Bomberman R* revived the series in 2017, the year the bee logo came down from Hudson's old headquarters, and in 2020 Konami released the PC Engine Mini, a small replica console with the games built in.
