In 1988 [Brian Fargo](/vault/people/brian-fargo) had a hit series, a post-nuclear role-playing game just out and no publisher of his own. His company, Interplay Productions, had written adventures for Activision and *The Bard's Tale* and *[Wasteland](/vault/games/wasteland)* for Electronic Arts, and other people's labels were on the boxes. That year it declared itself an independent publisher, and its first game under its own name was a chess program in which the pieces fought each other. Ten years later Interplay was selling *[Fallout](/vault/games/fallout)* and *[Baldur's Gate](/vault/games/baldurs-gate)*, two of the games credited with bringing the computer role-playing game back from the dead. Five years after that its offices were closed by state officials over unpaid wages.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1983, by Brian Fargo, in Irvine, California, as Interplay Productions; Interplay Entertainment from 1998.
- **Early games:** text-and-graphics adventures such as *Mindshadow* and *Borrowed Time* for Activision; *The Bard's Tale* (1985) and *Wasteland* (1988) for Electronic Arts.
- **As publisher, from 1988:** *Battle Chess*, *Neuromancer*, *Dragon Wars*, *The Lost Vikings*, *Descent*, *MDK*, *[Fallout](/vault/games/fallout)*, and, under its [Black Isle Studios](/vault/companies/black-isle-studios) label, *[Baldur's Gate](/vault/games/baldurs-gate)*, *[Planescape: Torment](/vault/games/planescape-torment)* and *[Icewind Dale](/vault/games/icewind-dale)*.
- **Owners:** public from 1998; controlled by Titus Interactive from 2001.
- **Founder's exit:** Fargo left in 2002 and founded inXile Entertainment.

## Adventures for other people

Fargo's first game, the text adventure *Demon's Forge*, came out before Interplay existed. *The One* reported in 1989 that he founded the company in November 1983; he was 25 when that article appeared. Interplay began, as the *Australian Commodore and Amiga Review* put it in a 1992 profile, by building "on Fargo's adventuring roots": *Mindshadow*, *Borrowed Time* and *Tass Times in Tonetown*, adventures with pictures and a parser, all released under the Activision label. Interplay was early to the [Amiga](/vault/systems/commodore-amiga), with versions of *Borrowed Time* and *Tass Times*.

Then Michael Cranford wrote *The Bard's Tale* (1985), a dungeon game in the style of *[Wizardry](/vault/games/wizardry)* in which the bard's songs worked as spells. Electronic Arts published it, and the series ran to three games and a construction set; by 1992 the three games had sold, the same profile said, "over half a million copies". Interplay turned from adventures to role-playing games, and its next was *[Wasteland](/vault/games/wasteland)* (1988), set in the American south-west after a nuclear war, which the review called "another huge success both critically and commercially".

## Publishing its own games

In 1988 Interplay left Electronic Arts' distribution and joined Activision's affiliated publishers. *[Computer Gaming World](/vault/magazines/computer-gaming-world)* reported in April 1988 that its first project under the deal would be a role-playing game based on William Gibson's *Neuromancer*, with a soundtrack by Devo. The first game out under Interplay's own name was *Battle Chess* (1988), whose pieces walked across the board and fought when they took each other, and which became, the same profile said, "another major seller". *Neuromancer* followed, produced by Fargo, and in 1989 *Dragon Wars*, a role-playing game Fargo described to *[Zzap!64](/vault/magazines/zzap-64)* as "our best ever, definitely".

Interplay also published other developers' work. It brought Delphine Software's French adventures to America, among them *Future Wars* and *[Another World](/vault/games/another-world)*, which Interplay renamed *Out of This World*. It published *The Lost Vikings* (1993) by Silicon & Synapse, the studio that became [Blizzard](/vault/companies/blizzard), and *Descent* (1995) by Parallax Software, a 3D shooter in which the player's ship could move in any direction, not just along a floor. *Computer Gaming World* thought *Descent* "the first look at what the next generation of 3D action titles will be like". Interplay bought [David Perry](/vault/people/david-perry)'s Shiny Entertainment, the makers of *Earthworm Jim*, and published Shiny's *MDK* (1997). *[Edge](/vault/magazines/edge)* thought the purchase had "gained it a lot more kudos".

## The role-playing years

Interplay returned to the post-nuclear wasteland with *[Fallout](/vault/games/fallout)* (1997), developed and published by Interplay itself after about three years of work. In 1996 Interplay had split its development into divisions, among them [Black Isle Studios](/vault/companies/black-isle-studios), set up under Feargus Urquhart to make "RPGs and only RPGs". Black Isle's name went on *Fallout 2* (1998), on [BioWare](/vault/companies/bioware)'s *[Baldur's Gate](/vault/games/baldurs-gate)* (1998), which Interplay published under the label and which sold more than a million copies, and on the division's own *[Planescape: Torment](/vault/games/planescape-torment)* (1999) and *[Icewind Dale](/vault/games/icewind-dale)* (2000). "Remember when everyone said that RPGs are dead?" *Computer Gaming World* asked in 1999; after *Baldur's Gate*, it said, the genre was "hotter than ever" (see [Western RPG](/vault/genres/western-rpg)).

## Titus

The role-playing games sold, but Interplay lost money. It went public in 1998, when *Computer Gaming World* found its share offering meeting "less initial excitement" than hoped. In 1999 it joined its European distribution with [Virgin Interactive](/vault/companies/virgin-games)'s, taking a stake of about 44% in Virgin, and the French publisher Titus Interactive, best known for *Titus the Fox* and the Blues Brothers games, began buying into Interplay. *Edge* reported that Titus had paid $35 million, and *Computer Gaming World* complained about "the French invasion".

In August 2001, *Computer Gaming World* reported, Titus "wrested control of Interplay", taking its stake to 51% by converting a $20 million loan into shares. Interplay had just lost $12.4 million in a quarter, laid off 60 staff and cancelled the role-playing game *Torn*, and its games would now be published in America by Vivendi Universal. Fargo left in 2002. Eric Caen, a founder of Titus who later ran Interplay, told *Edge* that Fargo "wasn't good at managing budgets, but he was the spirit of the company". In 2002 Interplay sold Shiny to Infogrames for $47 million; in December 2003 it laid off the whole of Black Isle, while the division was working on a third *Fallout*.

In June 2004 Interplay's Irvine headquarters were closed. *Computer Gaming World* reported that California state officials had acted on complaints from unpaid employees and found the company had no workers' compensation insurance, and that Interplay also faced back taxes and lawsuits, among them a claim by BioWare for $156,000 in *Baldur's Gate* royalties. The magazine reported that on the same day Titus declared bankruptcy.

## Fallout goes to Bethesda

Later that year [Bethesda Softworks](/vault/companies/bethesda) won the right to make *Fallout 3*, and in 2007 it bought the series outright. In 2009 it went to court to stop Interplay making an online *Fallout* game. Interplay's side was put by Eric Caen, then its president, in *Edge* in 2010. In his account, Bethesda had left Interplay a licence to make the online game on conditions it expected Interplay could not meet, and in 2009 sent a termination letter "saying: 'You did not fulfil your obligation.' But Interplay did. So all the litigation is about that." The companies settled in January 2012. As *Kotaku* reported the terms, ZeniMax, Bethesda's parent, paid Interplay $2 million, each side met its own legal costs, Interplay gave up its licence for the online game, so *Fallout Online* was abandoned, and it could sell the original *Fallout*, *Fallout 2* and *Fallout Tactics* until the end of 2013.

## Later history

Interplay carried on as a small company living on its catalogue. In 2010 Caen described it as sitting on "a pile of IPs", from *Earthworm Jim* to *MDK*, and planned to license them to other developers. In 2012 it revived the Black Isle name for a crowdfunded role-playing game, *Project V13*, which did not raise enough to be made, and in 2021 it re-released Black Isle's *Baldur's Gate: Dark Alliance* on modern consoles and the PC.

Its people went elsewhere. Urquhart and other Black Isle staff founded [Obsidian Entertainment](/vault/companies/obsidian-entertainment) in 2003, and *Fallout*'s designers had already left to found [Troika Games](/vault/companies/troika-games). Fargo's new company, inXile Entertainment, made a new *The Bard's Tale* in 2004, and in 2012 went to Kickstarter for a sequel to *Wasteland*. Fargo asked for $900,000 and raised $2,933,252 from players (see [CRPG renaissance](/vault/phenomena/crpg-renaissance)).
