Rebellion began, in its own telling, in "their basement flat in Oxford", where the brothers Jason and Chris Kingsley set up a games company in 1992. Within a year Atari had given the nine-man team two games for its new Jaguar console, one of them a licence the studio would come back to for the next 16 years: *Alien Vs Predator*. To make the Jaguar game's creatures, Rebellion built latex models and photographed them, and for one set of animations it "dressed someone up in the actual costume that Hicks wore in the Aliens movie".

The Kingsleys have run the company ever since. In 2000 they bought the comic they had read since its first issue, *2000 AD*, and with it Judge Dredd. Their *Sniper Elite* series began in 2005, and in 2006 they bought Core Design, the studio behind *[Tomb Raider](/vault/games/tomb-raider)*. By then *Edge* was calling Rebellion "the UK's biggest independent developer". It is independent still, and it now publishes comics and books and runs a film studio too.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1992 in Oxford by Jason Kingsley and Chris Kingsley; incorporated as Rebellion Developments Limited on 4 December 1992.
- **Run by:** Jason Kingsley (chief executive and creative director) and Chris Kingsley (chief technical officer).
- **First games:** *Alien Vs Predator* (1994) and a driving game, announced as *Chequered Flag II*, for [Atari](/vault/companies/atari)'s Jaguar console.
- **Best known for:** *Aliens Vs Predator* (PC, 1999), the *2000 AD* games, *Sniper Elite* (from 2005) and its *Zombie Army* spin-offs.
- **Comics:** owner of *2000 AD* and the *Judge Dredd Megazine* since 2000, and of the old Fleetway and IPC comics archive since 2016.
- **Size:** more than 60 staff in 2000 and about 200 in 2010.

## Latex aliens for the Jaguar

*Edge* visited Rebellion in January 1994. After seeing Atari's plans for the Jaguar at a conference about a year earlier, "the nine man outfit was commissioned for two games – a polygon-based driving game, *Chequered Flag II*, and a firstperson perspective adventure/shoot 'em up based on the *Aliens vs Predator* licence." Jason Kingsley, as creative director, was full of praise for the hardware: "It's superb, and so much a step up from previous consoles." Instead of drawing the graphics, the team built "small panels to make up the walls, floor and ceilings" and "latex models for many of the sprites", then photographed and digitised them. "Funnily enough," he added, "we thought the process would take longer than drawing everything, but it actually worked out quicker."

*Alien Vs Predator* came out in October 1994, and Atari needed it: *Edge* wrote that the company was "counting on the game to boost hardware sales this Christmas". The player chose to be a Colonial Marine, an Alien or a Predator, each with its own mission. *Edge* was unimpressed. It gave the game four out of ten, found its levels "completely flat" and without puzzles, and called it "a lumbering, lame and unfocused imitation of Doom". Jaguar owners saw it differently. The American fanzine *Digital Press* reported that it had "finally hit the stores and it's selling out everywhere", and called it "the best Jaguar game to date". The Jaguar did not survive, and Rebellion's future, as a later profile put it, "clearly lay elsewhere".

## Aliens Vs Predator on the PC

Rebellion returned to the licence on the PC. *Aliens Vs Predator*, published by Fox Interactive at £39.99, came out in spring 1999, with the same three sides as the Jaguar game. *PC Zone*'s review opened with the line "Everyone in the PC ZONE office could hear Richie Shoemaker scream", and ended: "If there was an award for the scariest game ever, AvP would win it hands-down." The [first-person horror](/vault/techniques/first-person-horror) entry describes how the game worked on its players: the flares, the motion tracker, the flickering lights.

In 2010 Rebellion made a third *Aliens Vs Predator*, for Sega, on the PC, PlayStation 3 and Xbox 360. *Edge* gave it five out of ten, and concluded that "terrifying and terrible are not mutually exclusive".

## 2000 AD

Asked in 2000 what the company's name meant, Jason Kingsley said: "The name is an attitude. We're rebelling against bland copy games made by committees of grey folk who don't actually play games at all. We want to make great games that are original and different – games that we want to play."

That year Rebellion bought *2000 AD*, the British science-fiction weekly that introduced Judge Dredd, from the publisher Egmont, together with the *Judge Dredd Megazine*. Jason Kingsley called the comic "not only a goldmine of intellectual properties" but "a hugely enjoyable read". A Rebellion feature that September explained the attraction: "We get to play with over 700 fully fleshed-out characters, complete with worlds, stories, cool protagonists, and amazing visual designs," he said, with Dredd "high up on the list".

The comic gave Rebellion its own characters to make games about. Its series of 2000 AD games, as *Edge* described it, was "kicked off by the valiant but flawed *Dredd Vs Death*". *Rogue Trooper*, published by Eidos in 2006 for the PC, PlayStation 2 and Xbox, told the origin of the comic's blue-skinned soldier, whose dead comrades live on as chips in his rifle, helmet and backpack. *Edge* gave it seven out of ten and praised "a powerhouse performance from Rebellion's Asura engine", the studio's own technology.

In 2016 Rebellion bought the archive of Fleetway and the IPC Youth Group from Egmont, including *Battle*, *Action*, *Tammy* and *Roy of the Rovers*, and began reprinting it as the Treasury of British Comics.

## Sniper Elite and Rebellion Derby

*Sniper Elite* (2005) put the player in Berlin in 1945 as an American sniper trying to keep German atomic plans out of Soviet hands. Previewing it, the German magazine *PC Games* picked out what made it different from other shooters: body position, wind and even the sniper's heartbeat affected the aim. Rebellion announced in September 2015, the tenth anniversary, that it had sold more than 10 million copies. Later games added what *Wireframe*, reviewing *Sniper Elite 5* in 2022, called the series' "iconic X-ray kill cam": after a good shot the camera cuts to an X-ray view of the bullet's damage. *Zombie Army 4*, "Rebellion's fourth spin-off of the Sniper Elite series", set a squad of up to four players against "Hitler's army of the undead".

In May 2006 Rebellion bought the staff and assets of [Core Design](/vault/companies/core-design) from SCi, which had taken over Eidos. *Edge* reported that the studio would stay "in situ in its offices in Derby, and all staff will be retained", 65 people in its count, and that the team had already been working with Rebellion's Asura engine. As Rebellion Derby it finished *Free Running* and made *Rogue Warrior*. In March 2010, after a review begun that January, Rebellion closed the Derby studio and cut jobs at its Oxford headquarters.

## Owning the past

Rebellion has also bought and revived older games. It made a new *[Battlezone](/vault/games/battlezone)* for PlayStation VR, and *Wireframe* noted in 2018 that it owned "a wealth of gaming IP it didn't develop too, from Evil Genius to Ground Control". In November 2019 it bought the brand and games of [the Bitmap Brothers](/vault/companies/bitmap-brothers) from Mike Montgomery. The year before, it had bought a $100 million (£78 million) site at Didcot to turn into a film and television studio, where it planned a *Rogue Trooper* film and *Mega City One*, a television series set in Judge Dredd's city.
