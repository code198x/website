Until 1980 every cartridge for Atari's Video Computer System came from Atari. Activision changed that. In 1979 four of the programmers who had written Atari's own VCS cartridges left to sell cartridges of their own for Atari's machine, and every Activision box carried the name of the person who made the game. Within three years the company was selling more than $60 million a year, and by the company's own account David Crane's *[Pitfall!](/vault/games/pitfall)* was the best-selling game of 1982. Within ten it had been renamed, had bought and dismantled Infocom, and was losing money fast.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1979 by Alan Miller, [David Crane](/vault/people/david-crane), Bob Whitehead and Larry Kaplan, VCS designers at [Atari](/vault/companies/atari), with Jim Levy as president.
- **First games:** *Dragster*, *Fishing Derby*, *Boxing* and *Checkers* for the Atari VCS, 1980.
- **Atari's lawsuit:** settled in December 1981, with Activision making games under a technology licence from Atari. See [Atari vs Activision](/vault/culture/atari-vs-activision).
- **Biggest hits:** *Pitfall!* (1982) on the VCS; *Ghostbusters* (1984) on home computers.
- **Renamed:** Mediagenic, in 1988.

## Five founders

The four designers had written a large share of Atari's VCS games, and they wanted credit and a share of what those games earned. They did not simply walk out. Through a lawyer they met Jim Levy, a former executive of the tape company GRT, who became president; a venture capital firm backed the plan; and only then, in late September 1979, did the four resign. *Video Games* reported in 1982 that the founders started with "almost a million dollars in startup money". How they left Atari, what Atari's chief executive [Ray Kassar](/vault/people/ray-kassar) is supposed to have said to them, and how Atari's $20 million trade-secrets suit ended in a settlement rather than a verdict are told in [Atari vs Activision](/vault/culture/atari-vs-activision).

Crane described the start plainly to *Compute!'s Gazette* in 1985: "There was a group of us that knew we were good, so we set out to start a company." Their first products were four VCS cartridges, *Dragster*, *Fishing Derby*, *Boxing* and *Checkers*, and Crane said the plan had always been to start on the VCS "and move into the more elaborate home computers that we knew would exist about this time".

## Original games only

*Video Games* visited in 1982, when Activision was releasing two cartridges a quarter. It noted that the company owned no arcade licences and wanted none: *Fishing Derby*, *Laser Blast*, *Freeway*, *Skiing* and *Kaboom!* were all original designs. Sales had grown from $6 million to over $60 million in three years, and in March 1982 alone Activision shipped more than a million cartridges. "I think we electrified the industry," Levy said.

The games came from the designers' own lives. Crane's *Freeway*, in which a chicken crosses ten lanes of traffic, began when he and some friends left a Chicago trade show by the wrong exit and had to dodge across Lake Shore Drive; later that day Kaplan saw a man from a bus doing the same thing. Whitehead's *Stampede* came from the annual stampede in his parents' farming town. In 1988 *Amazing Computing* summed up the company's early reputation as "pushing more color and detail out of the modest Atari VCS than anyone thought the machine was capable of".

Not all the founders stayed. *Video Games* reported in January 1983 that Kaplan, who had designed *Kaboom!*, had quit Activision.

## Pitfall!

Crane's *Pitfall!* was the company's biggest cartridge. In May 1983 *Electronic Fun with Computers & Games* reported Activision's claim that *Pitfall!* and Crane's *Laser Blast* had each sold a million copies, and that *Pitfall!* had been the best-selling game of 1982. The figures grew as they were retold: *Commodore User* wrote in 1985 that it was "reputed to have sold more than five million copies around the world". The game and how it was made are covered in [David Crane](/vault/people/david-crane#pitfall) and [Pitfall!](/vault/games/pitfall).

Activision kept its players close. A December 1984 note in *Ahoy!* offered Commodore 64 owners *Programming Pitfall Harry*, a booklet by Crane, for 25 cents postage. It contained a 102-line program that put a moving Pitfall Harry on screen, with instructions for "changing the background scenery, the figure's speed and color". "Tape your quarter to a piece of paper, please, and mail to Activision."

## From cartridges to computers

When the American cartridge market collapsed in 1983 and 1984 (see [the 1983 crash](/vault/phenomena/1983-crash)), Activision moved to home computers. *ACE* thought in 1990 that the switch "brought even greater success". Crane's *Ghostbusters* (1984), a Commodore 64 game built on the film, topped the charts in Britain and America, according to *Commodore User*. Activision also published *Hacker*, *Alter Ego* and Crane's *[Little Computer People](/vault/people/david-crane#little-computer-people)*; *ACE* called *Little Computer People* and *Alter Ego* "two of the most original computer games ever".

In Britain, Activision UK also sold other companies' games. *ACE* listed [System 3](/vault/companies/system-3), [Sierra On-Line](/vault/companies/sierra) and Lucasfilm among the labels it had handled, "including: *The Last Ninja*, *Leisure Suit Larry*, *Rescue on Fractalus*, *Koronis Rift*, *The Eidolon* and *Ballblazer*". For British players, the Activision name on a box did not always mean an Activision game.

## Infocom and Mediagenic

In 1986 Activision bought [Infocom](/vault/companies/infocom), which *ACE* called a "prestigious adventure developer". *Computer Gaming World* later reported that the deal was finalised on 19 February 1986 and valued at $7.5 million, much of it in Activision stock. Over the next three years Activision absorbed Infocom's sales, manufacturing, public relations and customer support, and in 1989 moved its development to the West Coast, leaving Infocom "only a label".

In 1988 the company changed its own name to Mediagenic. *Computer Gaming World* explained that the new name was meant to be "more reflective of the total corporate personality" than one "which still causes potential investors to think of cartridge games". The magazine was less kind in 1991, listing the change among management's "many mind changes".

By October 1990 the position was grim. *ACE* reported that Mediagenic had lost nearly $20 million the previous year and faced a $6 million bill after "losing a long and embittered court battle with Magnavox", the Philips subsidiary, which had "successfully claimed copyright infringement on a video game from the early eighties". Mediagenic was believed to be up for sale, and Activision UK, which was dropping the Amiga, ST, Spectrum, C64 and CPC to concentrate on the PC, NES and Game Boy, would be down to two people by January 1991. "The whole situation is very sad," *ACE* wrote. "Activision has sold some great games in the past."

A few months later a group led by Robert Kotick bought more than 20% of Mediagenic's stock, and *Computer Gaming World* reported in April 1991 that Kotick would become chairman and chief executive in place of Bruce Davis. The company later went back to the Activision name. Its history after the early 2000s, including its 2008 merger with Vivendi's games business, which brought in Blizzard, and its purchase by [Microsoft](/vault/companies/microsoft) in 2023, lies outside the Vault's period.
