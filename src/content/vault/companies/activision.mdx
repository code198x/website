## Overview
In 1979, four Atari programmers did something unprecedented: they quit and started their own company to publish games for Atari's console. [Atari](/vault/companies/atari) sued. Activision won. The victory established that anyone could make games for any platform—creating the third-party publishing model that still defines the industry.

## Fast facts
- **Founded:** October 1979 by David Crane, Larry Kaplan, Alan Miller, and Bob Whitehead.
- **The grievance:** Atari refused to credit programmers; top talent earned no royalties.
- **First releases:** *Dragster*, *Fishing Derby*, *Checkers*, *Boxing* (1980).
- **Breakthrough:** *Pitfall!* (1982) sold over 4 million copies.
- **Legal victory:** Atari's lawsuit failed; third-party publishing became legal precedent.
- **Modern form:** merged with Blizzard (2008); acquired by Microsoft (2023).

## The rebellion
Atari treated programmers as anonymous labour:
- Games shipped without credits
- No royalties regardless of sales
- Warren Robinett hid his name in *Adventure* as protest
- Top programmers generated millions but earned salaries

David Crane, whose games sold $20 million, received a $22,000 salary. When management sent a memo calling programmers "towel designers" (interchangeable commodity workers), the founders had enough.

## The founding four
Each brought proven talent:
- **David Crane:** *Pitfall!*, *A Boy and His Blob*, dozens of hits.
- **Larry Kaplan:** *Kaboom!*, Air-Sea Battle systems.
- **Alan Miller:** *Robot Tank*, *Ice Hockey*.
- **Bob Whitehead:** *Chopper Command*, *Stampede*.

They knew how to make games. They just needed to sell them.

## The lawsuit

⚠ **This entry previously said Atari "lost on all counts". It did not — the case was
settled.** *Amazing Computing*, April 1988, states the outcome in an aside so casual it is
evidently common knowledge:

> Activision made its name by **pushing more color and detail out of the modest Atari VCS
> than anyone thought the machine was capable of** (Atari showed its corporate gratitude by
> slapping Activision with a **massive lawsuit, which was settled out of court** — but
> that's another story).

The distinction matters. A settlement is not a precedent. Third-party publishing became
normal because Activision survived long enough to make it normal and because the settlement
left it trading — not because a court ruled that console makers may not lock out
competitors. The floodgates opened all the same: dozens of publishers followed, not all of
them maintaining Activision's quality, which fed the [1983 crash](/vault/phenomena/1983-crash).

## Pitfall!
David Crane's *Pitfall!* became Activision's defining moment:
- Smooth animation unprecedented on 2600
- 255 screens of jungle exploration
- 20-minute time limit encouraged mastery
- Over 4 million copies sold
- Proved third-party games could be best-in-class

## The home computer years
As consoles crashed, Activision pivoted:
- Published for C64, Spectrum, Amiga, Atari ST
- *Little Computer People*, *Ghostbusters*, *Hacker*
- Maintained quality reputation through the 8-bit era
- Transitioned to 16-bit and PC as markets shifted

## Twenty-five cents for the source

One artefact deserves to sit at the centre of this entry, because it is the founding
principle carried to its logical end. *Ahoy!*, December 1984:

> Activision is offering C-64 users a chance to put Pitfall Harry on their screens **for just
> 25¢**. For that postage and handling fee they'll send a copy of ***Programming Pitfall
> Harry* by Pitfall creator David Crane.** The booklet contains a short (**102-line**)
> program that will generate a moving Pitfall Harry, and instructions for **customizing the
> program by changing the background scenery, the figure's speed and color**, and so on.
>
> Tape your quarter to a piece of paper, please, and mail to Activision.

The company founded because programmers were denied credit sent children a type-in listing
**written by the author of its biggest game**, for the price of a stamp, and told them to go
and change it. That is the developers-as-artists argument stated in a form a twelve-year-old
could act on.

## Activision UK was also somebody else's publisher

Missing from this entry entirely: for much of the 8-bit era, Activision's British arm
distributed other houses' catalogues. *ACE*, October 1990, listing what it had handled:

> Activision UK also handled **System 3, Sierra On-Line and Lucasfilm games** in the past,
> including: *The Last Ninja*, *Leisure Suit Larry*, *Rescue on Fractalus*, *Koronis Rift*,
> *The Eidolon* and *Ballblazer*.

So the British route to Lucasfilm's celebrated 8-bit run, and to Sierra's adventures, ran
through Activision. As with [Mindscape](/vault/companies/mindscape) and Origin, the name on
the British box is a poor guide to who made the game.

## How it ended: Mediagenic

The entry jumps from "maintained quality reputation through the 8-bit era" to a 2008 merger,
skipping the part where the company nearly died. *ACE* reported the state of it in October
1990:

- **Activision US had been renamed Mediagenic**, and had **lost nearly \$20 million** in the
  previous year.
- It faced a **\$6 million bill** after "losing a long and embittered court battle with
  **Magnavox**" — a Philips subsidiary that "successfully claimed copyright infringement on a
  video game from the early eighties".
- It had "spent millions of dollars buying prestigious adventure developer **Infocom**, only
  to **close it down** earlier this year".
- **Mediagenic was believed to be up for sale**, "but customers have been thin on the
  ground".
- **Activision UK was down to two people** by January 1991, the company having decided to
  concentrate on PC, NES and Game Boy.

Read that beside the founding story. Activision exists because four programmers refused to
be treated as interchangeable, and because Atari's attempt to sue them out of the market
failed. Eleven years later the same company had been renamed after a marketing category, had
lost a copyright case over a video game to the owner of the patents that started the
industry, and had bought the most respected writers' studio in the business to shut
it.

*ACE*'s own verdict, and it is the right note: "**The whole situation is very sad.
Activision has sold some great games in the past.**"

## What the period press thought it was good at

The same piece is a useful corrective to a canon that remembers *Pitfall!* and little else.
Its author rates the **micro** years above the console ones — "a switch to micros in the
mid-80s brought even greater success" — and names *Pastfinder* as "classic", *Hero* as
"brilliant", *River Raid* as "playable", and *Park Patrol* as a personal favourite. It
singles out ***Alter Ego*** and ***Little Computer People*** as "two of the most original
computer games ever", and *Ghostbusters* (1984) as "one of the best selling computer games
ever".

## Legacy
Activision proved programmers had value. Their rebellion created the third-party ecosystem. Every independent developer owes something to those four programmers who walked out of Atari with nothing but their talent and won.
