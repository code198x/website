**Acornsoft** was [Acorn Computers](/vault/companies/acorn-computers)' in-house software
label — the first-party publisher for the BBC Micro and the Electron, in the way that a
console manufacturer publishes for its own machine.

It is remembered for one game, and reasonably so. ***[Elite](/vault/games/elite)*** (1984),
by [David Braben](/vault/people/david-braben) and [Ian Bell](/vault/people/ian-bell), came
out under the Acornsoft label and did more for the BBC Micro than any hardware revision.

## Fast facts

- **Parent:** [Acorn Computers](/vault/companies/acorn-computers), Cambridge.
- **Platforms:** [BBC Micro](/vault/systems/bbc-micro), Acorn Electron.
- **Best known for:** *Elite* (1984).
- **Also published:** *Planetoid*, *Meteors*, *Snapper*, *Aviator*, *Revs*, *Sphinx Adventure* — first-party conversions of the arcade canon, plus originals.
- **Prices, per *Acorn User*'s charts:** *Elite* at **£14.95** cassette, **£17.95** on disc.

## What Elite was, as the period judged it

*ACE*'s all-time-greats feature of March 1991 gives the fullest contemporary assessment, and
its first clause is the commercial claim:

> ***Elite*** — Acornsoft (BBC B). **Probably responsible for selling many BBCs**, this
> archetypical space fighting-and-trading game **has been much imitated but (many would
> argue) never surpassed**. The intergalactic action, depicted in **wireframe 3D**, provided
> the immediate pull-you-in thrills, but the **trading at space stations, plus possible
> mission assignments, added the depth** needed to make this an **all-time Great**.

"Probably responsible for selling many BBCs" is the part worth holding onto. Acorn's machine
was bought for education and specified for it; the thing that moved units was a first-party
game about smuggling.

The month before, *ACE*'s readers had voted it **second** in an all-time list — the magazine's
comment on the entry ran to four words: "**we couldn't agree more**" — above *Alien 8*, *Atic Atac*,
*Kick Off* and *Stunt Car Racer*.

## The technical claim, stated carefully

*Elite* is routinely called the first free-roaming 3D game, and a 2002 retrospective puts the
claim in the form that survives scrutiny: it was **probably the first vector-graphics 3D game
in which the player could move entirely freely in three dimensions** — where *Battlezone* and
*Tempest*, for all their vector graphics, confined movement to two.

The same piece records the detail that made the title mean something: **Elite** was the
highest attainable rank, universally coveted, and reaching it required destroying around
**6,400** opponents.

⚠ Both points come from a retrospective rather than contemporary reporting, and the second is
a single source. Treat the rank threshold as reported rather than verified.

## The argument Elite was used to make

The most interesting Acornsoft citation in this library is not a review. It is a reader
writing to *ACE* in September 1989 about what the industry had become, using *Elite* as the
measure of what it had lost:

> I have a horrible suspicion that **unless a game can be coded** [in a] **compiled language
> like 'C' it doesn't get past the accountant**. Games no longer seem to sell by repute
> [without] **very expensive promotion and release on the first few available formats**. **On
> this basis the original Acornsoft version of *Elite* would have died at birth.**

Five years after release, *Elite* had become the standard example of a game that the
publishing model of 1989 could no longer have produced: hand-written assembly, on a minority
machine, from two students, selling on word of mouth.

That is an argument this Vault makes repeatedly from the other direction — see
[the bedroom coder](/vault/phenomena/bedroom-coder) for *Sinclair User* reporting the same
door closing in 1985, four years earlier.

## Aviator, and who wrote it

The catalogue below lists *Aviator* without an author. It was **Geoff Crammond** — later of
*Stunt Car Racer* and *Formula One Grand Prix* — and it was his first real simulator. He
told *Retro Gamer*:

> I bought one of the first BBC Micros. Months earlier I had an idea about **writing some sort
> of flight simulator in 3D just for my own enjoyment**… I developed *Aviator* **expecting it
> to be published by Acornsoft** rather than just doing it as a hobby — in the end **it took
> me a year to complete**. It was **my first experience of writing real-time simulator code**,
> and I also had to learn about the principles of flight.

Two things worth drawing out. He wrote it *speculatively*, expecting Acornsoft to take it —
the label was a credible destination for an unsolicited year of work, which is what a
first-party publisher on a minority platform could be. And the man who spent the next fifteen
years defining the racing simulator learned real-time simulation on a BBC Micro, for Acorn's
own label.

⚠ Crammond had already been published before *Aviator*: he took an earlier game to a
publisher who released it as *Super Invaders*.

## The rest of the catalogue

Acornsoft's other output is first-party work in the ordinary sense: arcade conversions and
originals commissioned to give the machine a library. *ACE* singles out ***Planetoid*** as
"**the definitive *Defender***" — high praise for a clone, and a fair indication that
Acorn's own label was competent well beyond *Elite*.

⚠ **Coverage note.** This library's *contemporary* Acorn holdings are thin — *Acorn User*,
*Acorn Programs*, *Micro User* and *Electron User* — so first-run reviews of much of the
Acornsoft catalogue are not available here. **Retrospective** coverage is now much better:
*Retro Gamer* runs an "Acorn Archives" strand and carries interviews with the people
involved. Treat the 1980s reviewing record as partial and the later reconstruction as
reasonably solid.
