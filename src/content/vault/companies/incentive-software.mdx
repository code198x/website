## Overview

Most British publishers of the eighties are remembered for their catalogue. Incentive is remembered for a **piece of technology**: *Freescape*, an engine that put solid, filled-polygon, first-person 3D worlds onto machines with no hardware capable of drawing them.

## Fast facts

- **Based:** Reading, Berkshire.
- **Press evidence in this library:** reviewed in *CRASH*, *ZZAP!64*, *Amstrad Action* and *Amiga Power* from **1987 to 1991**, at **£14.95–£29.99** — priced above the going rate, which the technology was expected to justify. ⚠ This library's run begins in 1987; the company was trading from 1983, so its first 4 years are not represented here and nothing in this line should be read as a start date.
- **Known for:** *Driller*, *Dark Side*, *Total Eclipse*, *Castle Master*, and the *3D Construction Kit*.
- **Before Freescape:** *Confuzion* (1985), a sliding-tile puzzle game — the company existed for years before the technology it is remembered for.


## It began as a postcard shop
*CRASH* profiled [Ian Andrew](/vault/people/ian-andrew) in May 1985 and the
origin is not the one the Freescape reputation suggests. Andrew was a
**deltiologist** — a postcard dealer — who started with two filing cabinets in
his bedroom and grew into shop premises in Reading. He bought one of the first
ZX81s off an advert in the *Daily Mail*, wrote *Mined-Out*, sold the postcard
business, and ran Incentive out of the shop.

An advert in the local paper for a machine-code programmer brought Ian Morgan
in, and *Splat!* became the first Incentive game. By 1985 there were three
full-time staff besides Andrew, and the shopfront was doing work no office
would have:

> There's a very informal atmosphere in the shop, and it has been a definite
> advantage having a shopfront. Programmers tend to wander in and hang around
> drinking coffee and playing our arcade machine, which means that the place is
> a melting pot of ideas.

That is not a figure of speech. *Confuzion* reached the Spectrum because
**Brendan Kelly** popped into the shop — the same Brendan Kelly who went on to
co-write [GAC](/vault/tools/graphic-adventure-creator) with Sean Ellis.

## They did this twice
The construction-kit instinct described below was not a one-off idea that
arrived with Freescape. Incentive had already done it, five years earlier and in
a different genre.

| Year | Product | What they sold |
|---|---|---|
| 1986 | [Graphic Adventure Creator](/vault/tools/graphic-adventure-creator) | An adventure authoring system with a picture editor built in — £22.95 |
| 1991 | [3D Construction Kit](/vault/tools/3d-construction-kit) | The Freescape engine itself, published by Domark — £24.99 |

Both reviewed near the top of the scale. GAC took 5/5 from *Sinclair User* and
9/10 from *Your Sinclair*; 3D Construction Kit took 92% and 91% from the same
two magazines.

The pattern is the company's actual signature: **build the thing, then sell the
means of building it**. It is rarer than it sounds, and it is why both tools are
still discussed while most of the catalogue is not.

## Freescape

Before [*Driller*](/vault/games/driller) in 1987, 3D on an 8-bit home computer
meant wireframe outlines or a fixed sequence of pre-drawn views.
[Freescape](/vault/techniques/freescape) gave you something closer to a place:
**solid shaded objects, drawn from any position you walked to**, in a world you
could move around freely instead of stepping through.

It was slow — noticeably, sometimes painfully so, with the screen redrawing in
visible stages as you turned. The trade was accepted because nothing else on the
machine offered the thing at all. What the engine bought was *coherence*: the
world stayed consistent as you moved, which is the property that makes a space
feel real rather than illustrated.

The press agreed at the time and kept agreeing. *Driller* took **97%** from
*CRASH* in December 1987 and 94% from *The Games Machine*; *Dark Side* 95%;
*Total Eclipse* 93%; *Castle Master* 85%.

## The construction kit

The second act is the more unusual one. Instead of keeping the engine in-house, Incentive packaged it and sold it as the **3D Construction Kit**, letting players build and share their own Freescape worlds.

This puts Incentive in a small and interesting category — publishers who **handed their core technology to their audience**. It is the same instinct that produced *The Quill* for adventures or, much later, level editors as standard equipment: an acknowledgement that the tool might be worth more than any one game made with it, and that the people buying it were also makers.

## Why it mattered

Incentive is the answer to "what did 3D look like before hardware could do it", and the answer is: an enormous amount of ingenuity spent on a machine that was never designed for the job. That is the era's characteristic move, and Freescape is one of its clearest cases.
