## Overview

Most British publishers of the eighties are remembered for their catalogue. Incentive is remembered for a **piece of technology**: *Freescape*, an engine that put solid, filled-polygon, first-person 3D worlds onto machines with no hardware capable of drawing them.

## Fast facts

- **Based:** Reading, Berkshire.
- **Seen in the press:** reviewed in *CRASH*, *ZZAP!64*, *Amstrad Action* and *Amiga Power* from **1987 to 1991**, at **£14.95–£29.99** — priced above the going rate, which the technology was expected to justify.
- **Known for:** *Driller*, *Dark Side*, *Total Eclipse*, *Castle Master*, and the *3D Construction Kit*.

## Freescape

Before *Driller* in 1987, 3D on an 8-bit home computer meant wireframe outlines or a fixed sequence of pre-drawn views. Freescape gave you something closer to a place: **solid shaded objects, drawn from any position you walked to**, in a world you could move around freely instead of stepping through.

It was slow — noticeably, sometimes painfully so, with the screen redrawing in visible stages as you turned. The trade was accepted because nothing else on the machine offered the thing at all. What the engine bought was *coherence*: the world stayed consistent as you moved, which is the property that makes a space feel real, not illustrated.

## The construction kit

The second act is the more unusual one. Instead of keeping the engine in-house, Incentive packaged it and sold it as the **3D Construction Kit**, letting players build and share their own Freescape worlds.

This puts Incentive in a small and interesting category — publishers who **handed their core technology to their audience**. It is the same instinct that produced *The Quill* for adventures or, much later, level editors as standard equipment: an acknowledgement that the tool might be worth more than any one game made with it, and that the people buying it were also makers.

## Why it mattered

Incentive is the answer to "what did 3D look like before hardware could do it", and the answer is: an enormous amount of ingenuity spent on a machine that was never designed for the job. That is the era's characteristic move, and Freescape is one of its clearest cases.
