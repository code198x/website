Incentive Software started in a Reading shop that had sold postcards, and within four years it was selling a 3D world you could walk round on a 48K Spectrum. [Ian Andrew](/vault/people/ian-andrew) founded it in 1983 to sell games with prizes attached. It published a much-praised adventure-writing tool, the [Graphic Adventure Creator](/vault/tools/graphic-adventure-creator), and built [Freescape](/vault/techniques/freescape), the system behind [*Driller*](/vault/games/driller), which *CRASH*'s readers voted the best Spectrum game of 1987. Then it sold Freescape itself to the public, and took the technology into virtual reality under a new name, Superscape.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** August 1983, by Ian Andrew and Ian Morgan.
- **Based:** a shop in Reading, Berkshire; by 1987, Calleva Park, Aldermaston.
- **Known for:** *Splat!*, the Ket Trilogy, the Graphic Adventure Creator, the Freescape games (*Driller*, *Dark Side*, *Total Eclipse*, *Castle Master*) and the [3D Construction Kit](/vault/tools/3d-construction-kit).
- **Later names:** its sister company Dimension International developed the Superscape 3D system and was renamed Superscape, which Glu Mobile bought in 2008.

## Games with prizes

Andrew had made his name with *Mined-Out*, a minefield game published by [Quicksilva](/vault/companies/quicksilva). In August 1983 *Personal Computer News* reported that he had "set up his own company; the first product is called Splat". He ran it with Ian Morgan, a machine-code programmer who had answered an advert in the Reading local paper. Andrew designed *Splat!* and wrote its BASIC parts, and Morgan wrote the machine code. It was a maze game in which the maze scrolled by itself in any direction, and *CRASH* rated it 82%.

The name was the sales pitch. "Incentive was chosen as the company name because it was decided to offer an added incentive to people who bought our games," Andrew told *CRASH*. *Splat!* offered £500 for the highest score by 14 January 1984, checked by a code the game produced. The winner was 17-year-old James Tant of Wokingham, with 112,930 points. The shop took the game's name and became the Splat Shop.

Several early Incentive games came from people who walked in. Rob Carter, an economist, wrote *1984*, a political simulation. Richard McCormack wrote *Mountains of Ket*, which grew into the Ket Trilogy with *Temple of Vran* and a final part; Incentive offered a video recorder to the first person to complete all three. John Hunt wrote *Millionaire*, a game about getting rich in the software business, and sent it to Incentive because he liked *1984*. Incentive also bought the home computer rights to the arcade game *Moon Cresta* from its Japanese maker, Nichibutsu, and *CRASH* made the Spectrum version a Smash.

## The shop as a studio

*CRASH*'s May 1985 profile of Andrew found a company with two full-time staff besides him and no staff programmers: all the code came from freelances paid royalties. Andrew said the shopfront made up for that:

> There's a very informal atmosphere in the shop, and it has been a definite advantage having a shopfront. Programmers tend to wander in and hang around drinking coffee and playing our arcade machine, which means that the place is a melting pot of ideas.

His example was *Confuzion*, a puzzle game in which you slide pallets of fuse wire around to guide a spark to a row of bombs. Paul Shirley designed it on the Amstrad, and the Spectrum version was written by Brendan Kelly, who had popped into the shop one day. Kelly went on to write the Spectrum version of the Graphic Adventure Creator.

## The Graphic Adventure Creator

Sean Ellis wrote the Graphic Adventure Creator (GAC) on the Amstrad CPC, and Incentive released it there in 1985, with Spectrum, Commodore 64 and BBC Micro versions in 1986, at £22.95 on cassette. It combined an adventure writer, a parser that understood whole sentences and a drawing program in one package. *CRASH* made it a Smash and *ZZAP!64* gave it a Gold Medal, both in September 1986. Incentive asked no royalty on games written with it, and set up a label, Medallion, to publish adventures its customers wrote.

## Freescape

In 1985 Ian Andrew's brother Chris had the idea for a system that could draw solid 3D objects on an 8-bit machine. Incentive formed an in-house team, Major Developments (Chris Andrew, Stephen Northcott and Paul Gregory), and work began in September 1986. The result was [Freescape](/vault/techniques/freescape): objects drawn as solid shapes in true perspective, which the player could walk round, look up at and see from behind.

*Driller*, the first Freescape game, came out for Christmas 1987 at £14.95 on cassette and £17.95 on disk, in a large box with a novella and a card model of its moon. *CRASH* gave it 97%, and in the 1987 *CRASH* Readers' Awards it won Best Game, Most Imaginative Game, Best Graphics and Best Game Packaging. Three more Freescape games followed:

| Game | Year | *CRASH* | Setting |
|---|---|---|---|
| [*Driller*](/vault/games/driller) | 1987 | 97% | a gas-filled moon, Mitral |
| *Dark Side* | 1988 | 93% | Evath's other moon, Tricuspid |
| *Total Eclipse* | 1988 | 93% | a pyramid in Egypt |
| *Castle Master* | 1990 | 85% | a haunted castle |

The games came out on the Spectrum, Amstrad CPC and Commodore 64, and later the Atari ST, Amiga and PC. Sean Ellis, GAC's author, wrote the 16-bit versions of *Driller* and *Dark Side*. By January 1989 Incentive had signed a deal giving the American publisher [Epyx](/vault/companies/epyx) the exclusive right to distribute the Freescape games in the USA, and *CRASH* reported half a million dollars of advance orders there for *Driller*. By 1990 Incentive's advertising listed a C&VG Golden Joystick, a *Commodore Computing International* Oskar and a Computer Industry Award for "8 Bit Programmers of the Year" among its honours.

## Domark and the 3D Construction Kit

*Castle Master* was the first Freescape game published by [Domark](/vault/companies/domark). Its 1990 advertisements read "Programmed by Incentive Software © 1990 New Dimension International Ltd. Published by Domark Ltd". Domark also published the next product, in 1991: the Freescape engine itself, as the [3D Construction Kit](/vault/tools/3d-construction-kit), at £24.99. Instead of "typing in lists of coordinates (a very tricky business)", as *CRASH* described how the games had been made, buyers built worlds from shapes with menus and a pointer, and saved them as stand-alone programs.

It was the second time Incentive had sold its own tools. GAC had put adventure-writing in its customers' hands six years earlier.

## Superscape and virtual reality

After converting *Dark Side*, Sean Ellis began a new 3D engine. It became Superscape, and Incentive's sister company, Dimension International, sold it for virtual reality and business use. In 1991 Dimension provided the real-time 3D for *Cyberzone*, a virtual reality game show from Broadsword, the makers of *Knightmare*, and announced a VR Toolkit at the London Virtual Reality Conference. A Superscape system cost £20,000, including a fast PC. A 1990 job advert from "Ian Andrew, Incentive Software, New Dimension International Ltd." described the company as "now into our 8th year" and its "Real Time 3 Dimensional Software FREESCAPE and SUPERSCAPE Systems" as "renowned for their quality and innovation".

By 1994 Dimension International had been renamed Superscape, and it planned to float on the stock market that April to raise £3–4 million; Ian Andrew owned 56% of it. Superscape's 3D benchmark program, 3DBench, was widely used in the mid-1990s to measure PC graphics speed. In the 2000s the company turned to 3D for mobile phones, and *Edge* described it in 2003 as a technology company that made tools for mobile developers. It was bought by Glu Mobile in March 2008 for about $36 million.
