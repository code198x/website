In the summer of 1997 *Edge* went looking for the company that had made *Myst* and found it in a pine forest on the edge of Spokane, in the far east of Washington state. Cyan, the magazine noted, had "only ever released one game". That game had sold more than three million copies, and the brothers who ran the studio, Rand and Robyn Miller, were spending the money on its sequel rather than take an advance from their publisher. Richard Vander Wende, a former Disney artist who had become the brothers' right-hand man, explained why they worked so far from the rest of the industry: "Up here we can just focus on work, eliminating the outside influences which are so detrimental."

Cyan has stayed there, and stayed independent, for nearly four decades; *Inverse* called it in 2023 "the oldest surviving independent game studio in America".

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1987, by the brothers Rand and Robyn Miller.
- **Based:** near Spokane, Washington; *Computer Gaming World* gave its address as Mead in 2001, and it is still there.
- **Names:** Cyan, Inc.; Cyan Worlds by 2001, the name on *Uru* and *Myst V*.
- **Children's games:** *The Manhole* (1988), *Cosmic Osmo* (1989) and, by 1992, *Spelunx and the Caves of Mr. Seudo*.
- **Myst series:** [Myst](/vault/games/myst) (1993), *Riven* (1997), *Uru: Ages Beyond Myst* (2003), *Myst V: End of Ages* (2005). *Myst III* and *Myst IV* were made by other studios.
- **Since 2005:** *Obduction* (2016), *Myst* in virtual reality (2020), *Firmament* (2023), and a remake of *Riven* (2024).

## HyperCard and The Manhole

Neither brother set out to make games for adults. "Rand had an idea for a children's book on a computer," Robyn told *Retro Gamer*, and Rand sent him a copy of HyperCard, Apple's program for linking cards of pictures and text, which Robyn called "like a predecessor to the web". Robyn's first drawing was a manhole on a white background. "What became immediately obvious was that an entire world existed below that manhole, and I wanted to visit it." The book idea quickly turned into what he called "a more nonlinear world", and that became *The Manhole* (1988).

*Cosmic Osmo* followed, and in February 1990 *The Games Machine* reported that [Activision](/vault/companies/activision) had released both on CD-ROM for the Macintosh, "enormous adventure games on a scale that has never before been possible". *Computer Gaming World* was using *Cosmic Osmo* as the yardstick for a family game by January 1990. [Brøderbund](/vault/companies/broderbund) published the third, *Spelunx and the Caves of Mr. Seudo*, which *Computer Gaming World* reviewed in May 1992 as entertaining and educational. Of *Cosmic Osmo*, Rand told *PC Gamer*: "We put things into this program that the adults would pick up on and the kids would enjoy."

## Myst

The money for something bigger came from Japan. The Millers had offered a game like *Myst* to Activision and been told to stick to children's games; then, in Robyn's words, "it was [Sunsoft](/vault/companies/sunsoft) who approached us asking us if we'd do a game for an older audience". *Inverse*'s oral history of the studio puts Sunsoft's investment, in 1991, at $250,000.

*Myst* shipped on Macintosh CD-ROM in 1993, published by Brøderbund. It was built in HyperCard, like the children's games: a stack of about 2,500 rendered pictures and QuickTime films showing an island and four other worlds, called Ages, reached through books. The [Myst](/vault/games/myst) entry tells its story. Its sales are reported differently by different magazines: over three million by 1997 (*Edge*) and seven million by 1999 (*PC Zone*). *Computer Gaming World* said it remained the best-selling PC game until *The Sims* overtook it in 2002.

## Riven

*Riven* came four years after *Myst*. When *Edge* visited in 1997, the team was modelling and animating it in Softimage on Silicon Graphics workstations, and pushing them hard. "We created an island, but something wasn't right with it, so we called Softimage tech support," Vander Wende said. "They asked how many polygons were involved and when we said about a million and a half, the tech guy nearly had a heart attack." It filled five CDs. The Millers drew on "African, Arabic and Celtic cultures" for its look, and tried to fix a weakness they saw in *Myst*: some players never got off the first island, so *Riven* gave them "a lot of territory to explore before they encounter that initial hurdle". Brøderbund's games label Red Orb published it at the end of 1997.

Robyn Miller left Cyan after *Riven*. The next two games in the series were made elsewhere and published by [Ubisoft](/vault/companies/ubisoft), which had acquired *Myst* from Brøderbund's later owners: *Myst III: Exile* (2001), developed by Presto Studios, and *Myst IV: Revelation* (2004). By then *Australian PC User* could say that Cyan "no longer plays much of a role in the series' development".

## Uru

Cyan spent those years on something far more ambitious. In October 2001 *Computer Gaming World* revealed a massively multiplayer game code-named *Mudpie*, set in the D'ni underground city from the *Myst* books. Rand Miller had been working on it for two years with a team of about 50, had bought Headspin Technology of Chapel Hill, North Carolina, for its 3D engine, and had put $5 million of his own money into it. "If this is truly to become the vision that we want," he said, "it will be the most expensive computer game ever."

It became *Uru: Ages Beyond Myst*, published by Ubisoft in 2003. The single-player game came out, but the online service was cancelled in 2004 after a beta test. "Another six months would have been interesting for *Uru*, but we couldn't afford it," Rand told *Edge*. Cyan then made *Myst V: End of Ages* (2005), in real-time 3D, as the series' final chapter; Rand said it was "the fastest *Myst* we've ever done", at about a year.

The online game had a longer afterlife. It returned on the GameTap service as *Myst Online: Uru Live* in 2007, closed in 2008, and was relaunched free by Cyan in 2010. Cyan then released the source code of its client and engine, Plasma, to the fans.

## Near closure and after

In September 2005, with *Myst V* finished and no publisher signed for its next project, Cyan let its staff go. *Inverse* puts it at all but two of 40 people. "When it came to closing a deal before we ran out of money, it didn't happen," Rand told *Macworld* in Australia. Then another deal fell into place the following week, and "just about everyone came back, almost all of the key people".

The studio went back to its players for money. A Kickstarter campaign in October 2013 raised more than $1.3 million for *Obduction*, a new world in the *Myst* mould, released in August 2016. *Firmament* followed in May 2023, also funded through Kickstarter. Cyan also returned to its own history: a new *Myst* for virtual-reality headsets and flat screens in 2020, and on 25 June 2024 a remake of *Riven*, built from the ground up, with members of the original team working on it.

## Released source code

In 2011 Cyan Worlds and the fan site OpenUru.org released the client and 3ds Max plug-in of the CyanWorlds.com Engine (Plasma), which ran *Myst Online: Uru Live*, under the GNU General Public License version 3. Fan developers maintain it on GitHub.
