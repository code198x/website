In 1972, as *Video Games* magazine told the story ten years later, a young engineer from California flew to the company's headquarters in Franklin Park, Illinois, to show off two electronic paddles knocking a ball across a television screen. Midway's managers were not interested. The engineer was Nolan Bushnell, and the game was *Pong*. A few years later Midway passed on Larry Rosenthal's *Space Wars*, which became a hit for Cinematronics. Then, in 1980, it took on a Japanese maze game its parent company's president thought was "silly", and *[Pac-Man](/vault/games/pac-man)* made it, in the same magazine's words, "king of the coin-op hill".

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1958, by Henry "Hank" Ross and Marcine "Iggy" Wolverton, as Midway Manufacturing.
- **Owners:** Bally Manufacturing from 1969; WMS Industries, the parent of [Williams Electronics](/vault/companies/williams-electronics), from 1988; an independent public company, Midway Games, from 1998.
- **Licensed hits:** *[Space Invaders](/vault/games/space-invaders)* (from [Taito](/vault/companies/taito)), *[Galaxian](/vault/games/galaxian)*, *[Pac-Man](/vault/games/pac-man)* and *[Galaga](/vault/games/galaga)* (from [Namco](/vault/companies/namco)).
- **Own games:** *Gun Fight* and *Sea Wolf* in the 1970s, *Ms. Pac-Man*, *Tron*, [*Mortal Kombat*](/vault/games/mortal-kombat), [*NBA Jam*](/vault/games/nba-jam).
- **End:** bankruptcy in February 2009; *Mortal Kombat* and most of the company went to Warner Bros. that July.

## Pinball and pitch-and-bat

Ross and Wolverton started out with *Red Ball*, which Ross called a "quasi-legitimate" bingo-pinball game, and followed it with pinball and "pitch & bat" baseball games in pinball cabinets. The pinball-style *Winner* (1964) set off an animated car race in the backglass when the ball hit a target. By 1969 the company was in financial trouble, and its founders sold it to Bally, the slot-machine maker.

Ross admitted that Midway was "lowest on the totem pole" when video games arrived in 1972. "Everyone else was ahead of us in terms of electronics," he said. Three games put it on its feet in the mid-1970s, each selling more than 5,000 machines: a video game also called *Winner*, a copy of *Pong* that Midway licensed from Atari; *Gun Fight*, its first licence from Japan, from Taito; and *Sea Wolf*, designed by Bally's Nutting Associates. From 1974 Midway made importing Japanese games a policy. The company's name, *Video Games* noted, came from the naval battle in the Pacific in the Second World War.

## Space Invaders and Pac-Man

The policy paid off in 1978, when Taito told Midway about the game that was causing a sensation in Japan. Midway, Bally's coin-operated game division, bought the American rights to *[Space Invaders](/vault/games/space-invaders)* and began its own version in August 1978, with an upright cabinet that reflected the invaders onto a painted lunar landscape. It sold a then record 72,000 machines. *Space Invaders Part II* followed in America as *Space Invaders Deluxe*, and Namco's *[Galaxian](/vault/games/galaxian)* sold another 45,000.

*Pac-Man* nearly did not happen. Stan Jarocki, Midway's vice-president of marketing, came back from Japan "positively electrified" by Namco's *Puck-Man* and urged Bally's president, Robert Mullane, to buy the rights. "He turned it down, plain and simple," Jarocki said. Midway's own governing committee overruled him. Renamed *Pac-Man*, the game reached America in 1980, and by the end of 1982 Midway had built 190,000 *Pac-Man* and *Ms. Pac-Man* machines between them. *Ms. Pac-Man* itself began as *Crazy Otto*, a modification by the independent General Computer Corporation, which Midway agreed to take on in October 1981. Nearly 100 companies held licences for some 500 Pac-Man products, and Midway expected $8–10 million from licensing in 1981 alone. By then it was expected to earn nearly half of Bally's projected $1 billion revenue for 1982.

The money went into new games. Midway's president, Dave Marofske, put profits back into research and development: Nutting Associates answered with *Gorf* and *Wizard of Wor*, Bally's Arcade Engineering with *Omega Race*, and Midway's own team was growing towards more than 100 engineers and designers. When Disney wanted an arcade game of its film *Tron*, Midway's designers built it in three months from a script and some sketches; George Gomez designed a glowing see-through joystick grip for it, which he said was the first ever made. In December 1982 *Video Games* reported that *Tron* had overtaken *Zaxxon* as the highest-earning coin-op in the country. Bally also licensed Midway's games to CBS for the Atari VCS and to Commodore for the [VIC-20](/vault/systems/commodore-vic-20). In America Midway also sold other companies' games under its name, among them Data East's *[BurgerTime](/vault/companies/data-east)* as "Bally Midway". By the end of 1982 its trademark notices read "Bally Midway Manufacturing Co."

## Williams, Mortal Kombat and NBA Jam

In 1988 Bally sold its amusement game division to Williams, which had made *[Defender](/vault/games/defender)* and *[Robotron: 2084](/vault/games/robotron-2084)*. The Midway name survived. In 1993 an advertisement for a PC version of the 1981 *Eight Ball Deluxe* pinball table described "Midway Manufacturing Company (makers of BALLY pinball games)" as "a subsidiary of WMS Industries".

[*Mortal Kombat*](/vault/games/mortal-kombat), created by [Ed Boon](/vault/people/ed-boon) and [John Tobias](/vault/people/john-tobias), reached British arcades by the end of 1992. Its fighters were digitised sprites. *Computer and Video Games* found some "pretty good (not to mention violent) moves" but was unimpressed overall, calling it a "bold attempt to steal *Streetfighter*'s crown" that fell short, and scoring it 72%. American politicians paid it more attention. When the US Congress held hearings on video game violence, "Congress focused on the violence in *Mortal Kombat*, and on *Night Trap*", as Sierra On-Line's Ken Williams put it in a guest editorial for *Sega Visions*, and the industry answered with its own ratings board (see the [Mortal Kombat controversy](/vault/culture/mortal-kombat-controversy) and the [ESRB](/vault/culture/esrb)). The home versions of those years came from [Acclaim](/vault/companies/acclaim), not Midway. [*NBA Jam*](/vault/games/nba-jam) (1993), basketball with digitised players two to a side, was the other big arcade game of the period, and was updated as a *Tournament Edition*.

## Midway Games

In 1996 WMS bought Atari Games, the arcade company behind *Primal Rage* and *Area 51*. The same year WMS sold shares in Midway Games to the public, and in 1998 it spun off the rest, making Midway an independent company. It now published its own console games, and by 1999 its home division had a European headquarters in London, Midway Games Ltd, publishing for the Nintendo 64, PlayStation and Dreamcast. Arcades were shrinking, and in June 2001 Midway announced that it was closing its arcade division.

The media owner Sumner Redstone spent more than $200 million of his own money on a controlling stake, *Edge* reported in 2005, and his daughter Shari became Midway's vice chairman. [John Romero](/vault/people/john-romero) was creative director at Midway's San Diego studio in 2005, making *Gauntlet: Seven Sorrows*. The losses went on. In December 2008 Redstone sold his stake, and on 12 February 2009 Midway filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection. That July the court approved the sale of most of its assets, including *Mortal Kombat*, to Warner Bros. The Chicago studio became [NetherRealm Studios](/vault/companies/netherrealm-studios), which went on making *Mortal Kombat* games for Warner Bros.; the rest of the company was wound up.

The people carried on elsewhere too. Jarvis had already left to found Raw Thrills in 2000, and Steve Ranck, who made *Hydro Thunder* at Midway, later founded Specular Interactive, whose arcade games came out through Raw Thrills.
