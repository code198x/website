In most role-playing games of the early 1990s the player's party did as it was told. In Silmarils' *Ishar* it argued back. A stranger met on the road could join only if the rest of the party voted to let them in, characters formed friendships, and a player who murdered an unwanted companion might find the victim's friend turning on the killer. Silmarils, a small studio east of Paris run by the brothers Louis-Marie and André Rocques, made three *Ishar* games, and then *Robinson's Requiem*, a survival game in which the hero had to treat his own wounds and, if a leg turned gangrenous, cut it off.

## Fast facts

- **Registered:** 16 October 1987, at Lognes, east of Paris.
- **Run by:** Louis-Marie Rocques and André Rocques, credited as authors on most of its games.
- **Known for:** *Crystals of Arborea* (1991 in Britain), *Storm Master* (1992), the *Ishar* trilogy (1992–1994), *Transarctica* (1993), *Robinson's Requiem* (1994).
- **Platforms:** Amstrad CPC, Atari ST and Falcon, Amiga, PC.
- **In Britain:** marketed by [Daze](/vault/companies/daze) Marketing of London.
- **Closed:** 2003.

## Beginnings

Louis-Marie Rocques had made a name before Silmarils existed. He created *L'Aigle d'Or* (1984), a graphic adventure for the Oric published by Loriciels, which *Retro Gamer* later called the best example of its kind on the machine. Silmarils itself was registered in October 1987. Its first game to reach British readers was *Manhattan Dealers*, a beat-'em-up that *The Games Machine* gave away as a competition prize in November 1988. On the Amstrad CPC it released *Windsurf Willy*, *Targhan*, *Xyphoes Fantasy* and *Bunny Bricks*.

## Ishar

The road to *Ishar* began with *Crystals of Arborea*, an earlier role-playing game set in the same land, reviewed in Britain in 1991. *Amiga Format* was blunt about it in 1992: "Remember a game called *Crystals of Arborea*? Probably not; it wasn't very good." *Ishar: Legend of the Fortress* (1992) was its sequel. Arborea has become Kendoria, and an evil ruler named Krogh has built a fortress called Ishar. The player starts alone with a sword and recruits up to four companions on the way.

The party was where Silmarils broke with the American games it was compared to. *Amiga Format* explained that a new recruit joined "only after a vote from the rest of your team", that each character had "their own psychology, so friendships may form between them", and that a player who wanted rid of a companion had "two options: dismissal or murder". Kill a popular one and "the dead one's friend ... may very well attack the murderer". *Amiga Power* gave *Ishar* 78%. When the American magazine *Amiga World* reviewed *Ishar II: Messengers of Doom* in December 1993, it described the party as "a democracy" in which "the other members have a vote", and called it, as far as the reviewer could tell, "the first RPG in which characters have personal likes and dislikes".

American reviewers placed *Ishar* beside SSI's *Eye of the Beholder*. Henning Vahlenkamp, reviewing the AGA version for *Amazing Computing* in April 1994, wrote that it was "a role-playing game (RPG) by Silmarils, a French developer, in a similar vein as *Eye of the Beholder* (EOB). While *Ishar* may not break any new ground in the idea department, its implementation helps it stand out in a crowded RPG field." Unusually, most of it took place outdoors. Vahlenkamp admired "lush and detailed" graphics and the way "objects in the distance have a wonderful hazy, unfocused quality which disappears as you move toward them", the box claiming over 100,000 views, though "things do seem a bit static". The game had no character generation: "you simply start out with one predefined character".

*Ishar 2* (1993) let players bring their party over from the first game. *Ishar 3: The Seven Gates of Infinity* followed in 1994. *Amiga Computing*, previewing it that April, said Silmarils had risen to "a position of remarkably high stature" in role-playing over two years, "much of the success ... down to the *Ishar* titles".

Silmarils also supported Atari's Falcon, which few publishers did. Louis-Marie Rocques told *ST Format* in 1993 that converting the PC *Ishar* to the Falcon had taken a month, and that the Falcon *Ishar 2* would use the machine's true-colour mode and its DSP chip.

## Storm Master and Transarctica

Not everything was a dungeon. *Storm Master* (1992) was a god game with a Gothic feel, mixing arcade and strategy sections with "religious wind ceremony" scenes, in which the aim was to dominate a land by sending out armies; *Amiga Format* gave it 90%. *Transarctica* (1993) was stranger. In its back-story, an attempt to reverse the greenhouse effect by causing a nuclear winter has worked far too well, and Europe lies under ice. The player runs an armoured, coal-fired train across a map of Europe, the Far East and North Africa, adding carriages and fighting the Viking Union, which controls the world's transport, with cannons, infantry and even mammoths, while searching for a way to bring back the sun. Louis-Marie Rocques, the project leader, told *Amiga Power* that the game's old Russian style came from the Trans-Siberian express: "the huge coal-fired trains themselves represent a strong symbolism, when placed against the ice-locked world".

## Robinson's Requiem

*Robinson's Requiem* (1994) took Silmarils' interest in simulation further than any of its role-playing games. The hero is stranded on a prison planet with no hit points or health bar. Instead a monitoring system called Sesame reports his blood pressure and pulse rate. The merest sip of the planet's water could prove fatal, rain without a hat gave him a cold, and *ST Format* listed the treatments the game expected: bandages and disinfectant for cuts, stitches for large wounds, a splint for a fracture, quinine for malaria. Gangrene "requires amputation. Sorry." The reviewer explained: "your leg goes gangrenous, you're the one who has to lop it off." *Amiga Format* noted that the manual included a survival handbook explaining "how to amputate your own gangrenous limbs".

Not everyone was won over. *Amiga Power* found the graphics "slow, badly-drawn and unimaginative" and much of the game a matter of "charts and electrocardiograms". *ST Format* thought the slow pace "annoying" but concluded that it was "not only a rare game but a rewarding one too". *Deus* (1996), which *PC Zone* called "a sequel of sorts to *Robinson's Requiem*", was by its account "one part combat, two parts exploration, three parts survival".

## Later years

Silmarils moved to the PC as the Amiga and ST markets faded. *Edge* listed it in 1995 among French developers as an "adventure game specialist responsible for titles such as *Robinson's Requiem*", with offices in Nancy as well as the Paris area. *Asghan: The Dragon Slayer* (1999), a third-person action game, struck *PC Zone* as "a distinctly average affair". The company's head office closed in April 2003; according to French Wikipedia, it had gone into judicial liquidation. Its games are still sold: GOG offers an *Ishar Compilation*, with *Crystals of Arborea* and the three *Ishar* games, published by Dotemu.
