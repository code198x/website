## Overview

Silmarils made role-playing games in France and sold them across Europe through the early nineties. The *Ishar* trilogy, *Transarctica* and *Robinson's Requiem* were all reviewed in the British Amiga press, and they read as unmistakably **not British and not American**.

## Fast facts

- **Press evidence in this library:** *Amiga Power*, **1992–1994** — *Ishar*, *Ishar 2*, *Ishar 3*, *Transarctica*, *Robinson's Requiem*, *Bunny Bricks*. ⚠ This library's run begins in 1992; the company was trading from 1987, so its first 5 years are not represented here and nothing in this line should be read as a start date.
- **Distributed in Britain by:** [Daze](/vault/companies/daze) and others, not by a British publisher of its own.
- **Best known for:** the *Ishar* trilogy — first-person party-based RPGs with hand-painted backdrops.

## What made them French

Three habits set the *Ishar* games apart from the Anglo-American RPGs of the same years.

**The party has opinions.** Characters could refuse to accept a new recruit, or vote them out, and a badly chosen companion could turn on the group. That places social friction inside a genre that elsewhere treated a party as an inventory of statistics.

**The world does not forgive.** Money runs out, food matters, mistakes are permanent, and the game is content to let a run become unwinnable without saying so. *Robinson's Requiem* took this furthest: a survival game where injuries had to be treated specifically and correctly, and where you could amputate your own gangrenous leg.

**The art leads.** The *Ishar* backdrops are painted illustrations, not tiled textures — closer to European comics than to the graph-paper dungeons of the American tradition, and typical of a French scene that treated a game's look as authored, not generated.

## Sold through whoever would carry it

Silmarils had no British publishing arm. Its games reached the UK through small distributors — [Daze](/vault/companies/daze) among them — which is how most continental European software arrived after the 8-bit era.

That arrangement had a cost. A French studio's British reputation depended on whoever happened to be importing it that year, on how well the translation had been done, and on whether the magazines were sent a copy. Games could be reviewed a year late, or not at all, and the developer had no way to correct it from Paris.

## Why it mattered

Silmarils is the entry for **the French RPG**, a tradition that runs parallel to the American one and shares almost none of its assumptions. It sits alongside [Infogrames](/vault/companies/infogrames) and [Delphine](/vault/companies/delphine-software) in a French scene that consistently produced games which were harsher, stranger and better looking than the market average.

The difficulty is the part worth defending. These are games that expect to defeat you and do not apologise, at a moment when the mainstream was moving the other way. That is a design position, not a failure of polish, and it is one that later European studios returned to repeatedly.
