Bemani is the name of a Konami division and of the arcade genre it built. It began with one unexpected hit, the DJ game *[Beatmania](/vault/games/beatmania)*, which went into Japanese arcades in December 1997. Within two years the same division had put a dance floor, a guitar, a drum kit and a bank of giant buttons into arcade cabinets, all played by keeping time with the music. By the spring of 1999 music games were the leading genre at Japan's arcade trade show, and Konami had named them after its first success. *Edge* later called the division "the unseen pioneer of one of the world's most successful game genres". The Western plastic-guitar boom of the 2000s came from another company, but the idea it sold was Konami's first.

## Fast facts

- **Parent:** [Konami](/vault/companies/konami).
- **First game:** *Beatmania*, Japanese arcades, December 1997.
- **Name:** shortened from *Beatmania*. *Edge* reported that the division was first called the Games & Music Division, and that Konami coined and copyrighted "bemani" to claim the genre.
- **Games before 2000:** *Beatmania* (1997), *Pop'n Music* (1998), *[Dance Dance Revolution](/vault/games/dance-dance-revolution)* (1998), *Guitar Freaks*, *Drummania* and *Beatmania IIDX* (1999).
- **Biggest export:** *Dance Dance Revolution*, sold in Europe as *Dancing Stage*.
- **Today:** the arcade series are still updated in Japan; *Beatmania IIDX 33* came out in September 2025.

## One game, then a family

*Beatmania* gave the player five keys and a record turntable, and asked them to play along as notes fell down the screen. Konami had not expected much of it. Hiroshi Takyasu, who ran Konami's sound development department, told *Edge*: "I didn't think Beat-Mania would become as popular as it is now." Crowds gathered to watch good players, and Konami followed the same plan with other instruments. For the music itself, *Edge* reported, Konami spent 1997 acquiring licences for "top J-pop and dance music" so that its arcade games would sound familiar to passers-by.

The second wave came in 1998. *Pop'n Music*, aimed at younger players, had big buttons and, in *Edge*'s words, "lightweight visuals". *Dance Dance Revolution*, made by a separate Konami research and development team in Kobe, replaced the keys with arrow panels on the floor and was, *Edge* reported, "a massive hit with JAMMA attendees" that autumn. At the AOU arcade show in Tokyo in February 1999, music games were the leading genre, "inspired by the success of Konami's Beat-Mania series", and Konami showed two more: *Drummania*, with a drum kit, and "the rock-inspired and self-explanatory" *Guitar Freaks*. An "elaborate version" linked the two cabinets for "a semi-band experience", years before Western band games. *Edge* later explained that the pair shared a soundtrack, so one player could take the guitar part of a song and another the drums. At the same show Konami introduced *Beatmania IIDX*, with seven keys instead of five, in a deluxe cabinet with a plasma screen.

That was when the name appeared. *Edge*'s show report said that Konami was "only beginning to fulfil the potential of the genre it now calls Bemani". In June 1999 *Computer and Video Games* explained it to British readers: "Beat Mania is the first of a series of games from Konami, known collectively as BEMANI. And get this - you can link them all up for some of the weirdest gigs of all time!" *Edge* reported in 2003 that Konami had "coined and copyrighted" the word "in an attempt to exert complete ownership over the genre", and in 2010 that Konami's Games & Music Division had been "renamed the Bemani division in a nod to its breakthrough game".

## Boom years

The games were very profitable. In December 2000 *Newsweek* reported that Konami had sold 25,000 *Beatmania* machines in three years, where an arcade game that sold 1,000 counted as a success. *Edge* wrote in 2003 that Konami's net income had risen by 260 per cent in the financial year of 1999, and that the company "directly attributed" the rise to rhythm action. Home versions followed on the PlayStation and PlayStation 2, often with their own controllers, and extra discs of new songs. *Edge* counted more than 40 versions of *Beatmania* alone by 2003, "from PSone to WonderSwan". At the Tokyo Game Show in 2000, it reported, *Metal Gear Solid 2* was "relegated to a single 17-inch monitor deep within the recesses of Konami's booth" to make room for "the company's large BeMani roster", which met a more rapturous reception.

British magazines began to use "Bemani" for the whole genre, and for any game that borrowed its scrolling prompts. The games themselves travelled less well. *Beatmania* reached British arcades and, in 2000, the PlayStation, but *Edge* wrote that British players "either found it too hard, too expensive or just felt stupid bashing away at a plastic turntable". *Dance Dance Revolution*, as *Dancing Stage*, was the exception. *Edge* called it in 2010 "the only Bemani machine to make a significant impact outside of Japan - it's perhaps as famous abroad as it is at home".

## Slowdown

In Japan the boom slowed early in the 2000s. *Edge*'s report from a Tokyo games show in the autumn of 2001 complained of "a substantial Bemani roster that did little except highlight the creative stagnancy currently afflicting the genre", and its report on the AOU show in 2002 found evidence "that the soaraway success of the BeMani genre is beginning to slow". In 2003 it described Konami as "quickly but quietly putting each of its golden child bemani franchises to sleep", something "no one would have predicted three years ago".

When *Edge* looked back in 2010, Konami would not take part. The magazine reported that "despite repeated requests for comment", the company "refused to offer a voice for this article", and that Konami's own Western band game, *Rock Revolution*, "wasn't even made by the Bemani team". It judged that Konami's music games had gone for "rapid expansion rather than further innovation", with new mixes and harder songs for experts, and that *Jubeat*, played by tapping 16 touch-sensitive screens, was "Konami's only new rhythm-action machine in eight years". Still, it concluded: "The scrolling note charts, coloured prompts and peripherals that define modern rhythm-gaming are all Konami creations."

Western developers agreed about the influence, if not always the games. Alex Rigopulos of [Harmonix](/vault/companies/harmonix), whose *[Guitar Hero](/vault/games/guitar-hero)* and *[Rock Band](/vault/games/rock-band)* made music games a mass market in America, told *Edge* that seeing them succeed in Japan was "inspirational", but that "we found the Bemani family of games in particular to be sorely deficient in a number of key respects: the gameplay was shallow, the visual design was uninspired, the music selection and sound quality was poor". He added: "We saw this as an opportunity to take music games to the next level."

## Still running

The Bemani series never left Japanese arcades. *Guitar Freaks* and *Drummania* were brought together under the name *GITADORA* in 2013, and new series such as *Sound Voltex* (2012) joined the older ones. *Dance Dance Revolution World* reached arcades in June 2024, and Konami Amusement released *Beatmania IIDX 33 Sparkle Shower*, the latest version of the seven-key game shown at the 1999 AOU show, on 17 September 2025. The [music games](/vault/genres/music-games) entry follows the wider genre from *[PaRappa the Rapper](/vault/games/parappa-the-rapper)*, the PlayStation game *Beatmania* borrowed from, to its Western boom and collapse.
