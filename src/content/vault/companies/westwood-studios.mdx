Visitors to Westwood Studios in 1993 found light tables for animators, storyboards pinned to the walls, a sound room padded with eggshell foam, an art museum and a break room full of free coin-op machines. *[Computer Gaming World](/vault/magazines/computer-gaming-world)* thought anyone told about the tour would ask what films the company had made. It was a games developer in an industrial park near Las Vegas airport, and a few years earlier it had been known mainly for missing its deadlines. Over the next decade it made *[Dune II](/vault/games/dune-ii)*, the game whose pattern [real-time strategy](/vault/genres/rts-genre) followed, and *[Command & Conquer](/vault/games/command-and-conquer)*, which took that pattern to a mass audience, before [Electronic Arts](/vault/companies/electronic-arts) bought the studio and then closed it.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1985, in Las Vegas, as Westwood Associates, by Brett Sperry and [Louis Castle](/vault/people/louis-castle).
- **Owners:** independent until 1992; [Virgin Games](/vault/companies/virgin-games) (later Virgin Interactive) 1992–1998; Electronic Arts from 1998.
- **Best-known games:** *Eye of the Beholder* (for SSI), *The Legend of Kyrandia* (1992), *[Dune II](/vault/games/dune-ii)* (1992), *Lands of Lore* (1993), *[Command & Conquer](/vault/games/command-and-conquer)* (1995), *[Red Alert](/vault/games/red-alert)* (1996), *Blade Runner* (1997).
- **Closed:** 2003, when EA merged it into EA Los Angeles.

## Westwood Associates

Sperry and Castle started the company in 1985. For its first years it worked for other publishers, and it did not always deliver on time. *Computer Gaming World* recalled in 1993 that towards the end of the decade Westwood had been "notorious for missing milestones", and that the magazine's editor, Johnny Wilson, used to greet Castle with "Missed many milestones lately, Louis?"

Much of the early work was converting other companies' games to new machines. When [Infocom](/vault/companies/infocom) advertised Westwood's *BattleTech: The Crescent Hawk's Inception* early in 1989, it introduced the developer as "Westwood Associates, creators of Phantasie III, Questron II and Summer Games", titles from SSI and [Epyx](/vault/companies/epyx). Westwood's own designs followed: *Mars Saga*, a science-fiction role-playing game for the [Commodore 64](/vault/systems/commodore-64), published by Electronic Arts at $34.95, and *Hillsfar* for SSI.

## Eye of the Beholder

The game that changed Westwood's reputation was *Eye of the Beholder*, a role-playing game for SSI's *Advanced Dungeons & Dragons* range. *Computer Gaming World* announced it in October 1990 as a "point-of-view fantasy role-playing game" with a three-dimensional engine, VGA graphics and a point-and-click interface, the first in SSI's line to use the second-edition rules. *Retro Gamer* later described it as blending "the 3D corridor-based exploring of *[Dungeon Master](/vault/games/dungeon-master)* with the official *Dungeons & Dragons* licence", and like *Dungeon Master* it ran in real time. Westwood followed it with *The Legend of Darkmoon* in 1991.

Real time mattered to what came next. Looking back in 2008, *[Retro Gamer](/vault/magazines/retro-gamer)* reported that *Eye of the Beholder* had shown Sperry and Castle that "real-time decision making was a potentially very exciting strategy-gaming element", and they wanted to take the idea further.

## Virgin and Dune II

By 1992 the milestone jokes had stopped. *Computer Gaming World* found that publishers now promised a project would be on time *because* Westwood was doing it. That year [Virgin Games](/vault/companies/virgin-games) bought the company. German magazine *PC Games* described Westwood, "recently taken over by Virgin Games", as the Las Vegas team behind *BattleTech*, *Hillsfar* and the *Eye of the Beholder* series, now trying its hand at adventures. *The Legend of Kyrandia*, a fairy-tale graphic adventure, was its first game for Virgin.

*[Dune II](/vault/games/dune-ii)* followed at the end of 1992. Virgin held the *Dune* licence and wanted a strategy game; the team at Westwood, lead programmer Joe Bostic recalled, had been playing *Herzog Zwei*, *[Populous](/vault/games/populous)*, *Military Madness* and *[Civilization](/vault/games/civilization)*. *Dune II* was not the first strategy game played in real time. SSI's *Cytron Masters*, Imagine's *Stonkers* and Technosoft's *Herzog Zwei* had armies that moved without turns years earlier, and which of them came first is still argued (the [real-time strategy](/vault/genres/rts-genre) entry tells that story). What *Dune II* did was put harvesting, base building and unit production together, controlled with the mouse, in the form that Blizzard's *[Warcraft](/vault/games/warcraft)* borrowed in 1994 and Westwood's own *Command & Conquer* made famous.

In August 1993 *Computer Gaming World* counted about 50 people at Westwood, up to 20 of them artists. Castle, vice-president of creative development, oversaw the art, where classically trained animators from Hanna-Barbera, Filmation and Disney drew storyboards and pencil tests. Frank Klepacki, "the composer of 90% of Westwood's musical scores", wrote on a synthesiser keyboard in his own office. The studio also made cartridge games for Nintendo's machines, and in November 1993 *Computer Gaming World*'s Scorpia reviewed its new role-playing game, *Lands of Lore*, under the headline "Westwood's New RPG Breaks With Tradition In Character".

## Command & Conquer

Sperry had named the next game before it existed. In January 1993 he told *[The One](/vault/magazines/the-one)* that *Dune 2* ran on "Westwood's Command and Conquer engine". *[Command & Conquer](/vault/games/command-and-conquer)* came out on two CDs in September 1995: a near-future war over the alien mineral Tiberium, with filmed briefings between missions and the actor and director Joe Kucan as Kane, the leader of the Brotherhood of Nod. *[Edge](/vault/magazines/edge)* grumbled that it was "an only slightly improved version" of *Dune 2* and gave it eight out of ten anyway.

Players did not mind. By December 1996 *Edge* reported "well over one million copies" sold and called Westwood, now a team of about 90, "the most successful videogame company in the US today". It visited as *[Command & Conquer: Red Alert](/vault/games/red-alert)* neared completion, with SVGA graphics, network play and a green-screen studio that *Edge* called the biggest in Nevada. By 1997 *Computer Gaming World* was reporting a flood of "real-time C&C clones".

Westwood did not only make strategy games. *Blade Runner* (1997), a Windows adventure set in the world of Ridley Scott's film, advertised "over 70 real-time virtual actors pursuing their own agendas". *Computer Gaming World*'s Thierry Nguyen thought it did for "new-school adventuring" what [LucasArts](/vault/companies/lucasarts)' *The Curse of [Monkey Island](/vault/games/monkey-island)* did for the old school.

## Electronic Arts

Inside Virgin, *Computer Gaming World* wrote in 1998, "the relationship soon became a case of the tail wagging the dog": Westwood's games made money while Virgin lost it, and Sperry had taken control of Virgin's own development. When Virgin's owners, Viacom and Spelling Entertainment, sold up, Electronic Arts bought Westwood in August 1998 for $122.5 million, together with Virgin's internal development. Sperry and Castle signed five-year contracts with EA. Early in 1999 Castle told *PC Player* that Virgin's American headquarters at Irvine, California, had become an official Westwood branch, finishing Virgin's remaining projects under the Westwood label.

Under EA, Westwood released the true *Command & Conquer* sequel, *Tiberian Sun* (1999), *Red Alert 2* (2000), the first-person *Command & Conquer: Renegade* (2002) and an online role-playing game in space, *Earth & Beyond*. *Command & Conquer: Generals* (2003) came from the former Westwood team in California, by then renamed EA Pacific. In January 2003 EA announced that it would close both Westwood and EA Pacific and merge them into EA Los Angeles. The Las Vegas studio closed at the end of March. A reader writing to *Edge* that spring blamed EA's "franchise is king" mentality; looking back in 2010, *Edge* itself described how studios like [Bullfrog](/vault/companies/bullfrog) and Westwood "were crudely assimilated into the group, often with disastrous results".

## Later history

*Command & Conquer* carried on at EA without Westwood, from *Command & Conquer 3: Tiberium Wars* in 2007 to *Command & Conquer 4* in 2010. Some of Westwood's programmers, among them Bostic and Mike Legg, set up Petroglyph Games in 2003; *Edge* and *Computer Gaming World* introduced it as the Westwood veterans making LucasArts' *Star Wars: Empire at War*. Petroglyph came back to Westwood's games in 2020, when it developed the *Command & Conquer Remastered Collection* for EA, with Klepacki returning for the music. EA released the source code of the remastered games that year, and in February 2025 published the original source code of *Command & Conquer*, *Red Alert*, *Renegade* and *Generals* under the GNU General Public License.
