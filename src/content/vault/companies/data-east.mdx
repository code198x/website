Data East was never one of Japan's giants. *Wireframe* called it "something of an underdog" even in its heyday, a company much smaller than Konami, Sega or Capcom. But it had a knack for arcade games that stuck in the memory, often with a streak of oddness: a chef chased by hot dogs in *BurgerTime*, a fire-breathing Russian strongman in *Karnov*, and a pair of street fighters rescuing the President from ninjas in *Bad Dudes Vs. DragonNinja*. Its *Karate Champ* of 1984 was one of the first one-on-one fighting games, and its American arm went to court to defend it.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 20 April 1976, in Tokyo, by Tetsuo Fukuda. Its American subsidiary, Data East USA, was set up in California in 1979.
- **Known for:** *BurgerTime* (1982), *Karate Champ* (1984), *Karnov*, *Bad Dudes Vs. DragonNinja* and *RoboCop* (1988), *Midnight Resistance* and *Sly Spy* (1989), *Joe & Mac* (1991), *Windjammers* (1994).
- **Also:** the DECO Cassette System for arcade operators, pinball machines from 1987, and home publishing in America.
- **Ended:** declared bankrupt by a Tokyo court on 25 June 2003.

## Cassettes and burgers

Data East began, according to later accounts, as an electronics firm, and its first big idea was hardware. The DECO Cassette System, launched in 1980, let an arcade operator change the game in a standard cabinet by swapping an audio cassette instead of buying a new machine. *Wireframe* judged it a sound idea that "failed to gain long-lasting traction": the tapes were unreliable and easy to pirate. Its early games, the magazine added, were often clones of other companies' hits.

*BurgerTime* (1982) was the first that people remembered. The player is a chef, Peter Pepper, who builds giant hamburgers by running along each bun, patty and garnish until it drops a level towards the plates at the bottom, chased all the while by "angry frankfurters, rotten eggs, and killer pickle slices". A shake of pepper stuns them for a moment. In America it came out under Bally Midway's name as well as Data East's: *Electronic Games* reviewed it in July 1983 as "Bally Midway's tongue-in-cheek climbing game", praised the "waddling swagger of the hot dogs", and advised: "Eat before you play!"

## Karate Champ

In February 1985 Bill Kunkel of *Electronic Games* reviewed a new Data East coin-op that followed its *Tag Team Wrestling*. *Karate Champ* had two joysticks, one to move the fighter and one to attack, and the instructions on screen listed "some 24 possible maneuvers": punches, kicks, leaps, crouches, spinning reverse kicks and a somersault over the opponent's head. A fighter in white faced one in red. Blows scored half a point or a full point "much the same as in actual karate competition", and between rounds came bonus challenges in which the player broke a stack of boards, fended off objects thrown at him and, Kunkel wrote, "I kid you not", faced a charging bull. He found the graphics a little bland but the game "a fascinating exception" to arcade machines that looked better than they played.

Later accounts credit its development to Technos. According to a US court, Data East began selling it in Japan in July 1984 and an updated version from that September, which went on to America and Europe; a Commodore 64 version followed in the United States in October 1985. *Wireframe* called it Data East's most significant contribution of the era: "it sparked an entire genre of one-on-one fighting games."

## The Epyx case

British home computer games soon took up the idea. When *ZZAP!64* first saw Melbourne House's [*The Way of the Exploding Fist*](/vault/games/way-of-the-exploding-fist) in 1985, it was told the game was based on *Karate Champ*. It was a different British game, though, that Data East took to court.

In 1986 [Epyx](/vault/companies/epyx) began selling [System 3](/vault/companies/system-3)'s [*International Karate*](/vault/games/international-karate) in America as *World Karate Championship*, and Data East USA sued it for copyright, trademark and trade dress infringement. Judge William Ingram of the district court in Northern California dismissed the trademark claims but found the games "substantially similar", and on 10 June 1987 he ordered Epyx to stop selling *World Karate Championship* and to recall all copies of it and of *International Karate*. On 30 November 1988 the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals reversed him. Everything the two games shared, from the fourteen moves and the referee's speech balloons to the 30-second rounds, came from the sport of karate or from the limits of the Commodore 64, and could not be protected; the parts Data East did own, the scoreboard and backgrounds, were not similar enough. "When idea and expression coincide," the court quoted, "there will be protection against nothing other than identical copying."

The ruling came back to Data East in the 1990s. When Capcom sued it over the likeness of *Fighter's History* to *Street Fighter II*, the case was dismissed; *Retro Gamer* later put that result down to the precedent Data East had set against Epyx.

## Arcades and licences

Data East's late-1980s arcade games were its best known in Europe, where many reached home computers through other publishers. *Karnov* starred the bald, fire-breathing Russian; *Wireframe* reported the story, from a 2002 developer interview, that the hero was drawn to poke fun at one of the company's bosses, who was not amused. *Bad Dudes Vs. DragonNinja* opened with the President kidnapped by ninjas. *Atomic Runner Chelnov* (1988), about a Russian miner caught in a nuclear accident, was criticised by at least one Japanese outlet for making light of the Chernobyl disaster.

*RoboCop* (1988) was one of Data East's bigger arcade hits. At the PC Show in 1988 the coin-op stood on [Ocean](/vault/companies/ocean-software)'s stand while Ocean's programmers converted it. Data East had little to do with the home versions, *Wireframe* noted, and they became far more popular than the arcade original. Ocean's American office was later run by Ray Musci, formerly Data East's chief salesman in America.

Data East also put its name on other companies' games. It released Irem's *[Kung-Fu Master](/vault/games/kung-fu-master)*, and later took the licence for FASA's *Shadowrun*, which [Beam Software](/vault/companies/beam-software) developed for the Super NES in 1993. It made pinball machines from 1987, many on big licences such as *Guns N' Roses*, *The Simpsons* and *Jurassic Park*, before selling the pinball business to Sega in the mid-1990s.

## Bankruptcy

The 1990s went badly. Arcades were shrinking, Data East USA was wound up in the second half of the decade, and in 1999 the Japanese company filed for reorganisation. It kept going for a few more years by licensing its old games and, according to later accounts, by selling negative ion generators. In April 2003 it filed for bankruptcy, and a Tokyo district court declared it bankrupt on 25 June 2003.

The games outlived the company. In February 2004 the mobile games company G-Mode bought most of them, and it re-releases them under the Data East name. *Windjammers* (1994), a Neo Geo game of flying discs, was re-released in the West in 2017, and by 2019 a sequel was on the way, made without Data East.
