In the summer of 1999 *Computer Gaming World* previewed a game from a Finnish studio it had to introduce to its readers. "Remedy isn't very well known," it wrote: the company's "biggest hit to date" was a driving game, *Death Rally*, and that had been "a lackluster seller". The new game was *Max Payne*, a noir thriller about a New York cop whose gunfights played out in "John Woo-style slo-mo". When it came out in 2001 it sold, by *Wireframe*'s count, more than 7.5 million copies over the following decade, and *Edge* later credited Remedy with popularising bullet-time. The studio behind it had started in 1995 in a basement in Espoo, and *Edge* counted it among the companies founded by members of the demo group [Future Crew](/vault/groups/future-crew).

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1995, in Espoo, Finland.
- **Founders:** Samuli Syvähuoko, Markus Mäki, Sami Nopanen, John Kavaleff and Sami Vanhatalo.
- **First game:** *Death Rally* (1996), a shareware combat racer for MS-DOS.
- **Best known for:** *Max Payne* (2001) and *Max Payne 2* (2003), *Alan Wake* (2010), *Quantum Break* (2016), *Control* (2019) and *Alan Wake 2* (2023).
- **Technology:** its Max-FX engine ran *Max Payne* and the early 3DMark benchmarks of [Futuremark](/vault/companies/futuremark), a company that grew out of Remedy in 1997.
- **Listed:** on Nasdaq First North Finland from May 2017, and on the main list of Nasdaq Helsinki from May 2022.

## From demos to a studio

By the mid-1990s Future Crew's members were finishing school and needed paid work. Samuli Syvähuoko, who had joined the group in 1991 under the handle Gore, told *Wireframe* in 2022 that publishers were already calling: "our technical skills at the time were so high we were constantly being contacted by game publishers around the world asking us to start making games." He had been reluctant to start a company, *Wireframe* reports, because of his own shareware outlook, but in 1995 he formed Remedy with Markus Mäki, Sami Nopanen, John Kavaleff and Sami Vanhatalo. The magazine names two other Finnish studios founded by demo groups before it: Bloodhouse, from Bloodsuckers, and Terramarque, from CNCD.

The first office was a basement. "The company was founded in '95, just four kilometres down the road, in a basement!" the chief executive, Matias Myllyrinne, told *Edge* in 2011, from a much larger Espoo headquarters with a motion-capture room, a sauna and shelves of prop guns.

## Death Rally

Remedy's first game, *Death Rally*, came out for MS-DOS in 1996 and was sold as shareware, with Apogee as its publisher. It was a racing game with guns. A German shareware advertisement summed up the loop: race armed cars, then spend the winnings on stronger engines, armour and weapons. *GamePro* previewed it for readers who liked *Road Rash*, promising 18 tracks, one to four players, mines to lay and spectators to run over, and Duke Nukem as one of the rival drivers. Its music was by Purple Motion of Future Crew, as an Italian magazine pointed out in 1997.

It was, in *Wireframe*'s words, "a modest hit". Remedy kept it alive long after MS-DOS had gone. In 2009 the studio released a version that ran on modern PCs for free, after years of mail from fans, and that led to a remake for iOS with the Finnish studio Mountain Sheep, Remedy's first game on Apple's phones. In 2020, for the game's 25th anniversary, Remedy gave the original away on Steam.

## Final Reality and Futuremark

In 1997 Remedy showed something that was not a game. At Assembly '97 in Finland it premiered *Final Reality*, a benchmark that ran 3D scenes on a PC's graphics card and scored the result. German magazines testing cards in 1998 called it the benchmark of VNU's test labs and reported the results in "Realitymarks". *Edge* wrote in 2011 that Futuremark was "born out of Remedy in 1997 to develop the studio's Final Reality 3D benchmarking tool". The [Futuremark](/vault/companies/futuremark) entry tells the rest of that story.

## Max Payne

*Max Payne* took far longer than planned. Remedy had begun discussing a hard-boiled detective story in late 1996, *Wireframe* reports, and expected development to take a year and a half; it took four and a half. "We were complete amateurs in project management," Syvähuoko said, but the studio had "the business-savviness to start talking to Apogee Software early on", better known by then as [3D Realms](/vault/companies/3d-realms), "and they kept funding us even though we were constantly late with our schedules."

The previews showed what the time was going on. *Computer Gaming World* described radiosity lighting, a skeletal animation system that made "capes and coats flap in the wind", and a team of artists sent to New York with cameras to photograph the streets for textures. *Edge* in 2000 noted that the developer was "particularly keen to show off its own Max-FX technology", and that Remedy would release its level editor and effects tools with the game so that players could build their own.

The game came out for Windows in 2001, published by Gathering of Developers. The American advertisement called its slow-motion "bullet-time gameplay" "a first in gaming". Bullet-time let the player slow the action at will, dive across a room and pick off enemies one by one. *Computer Gaming World*'s review called it "the gaming world's first mindless blockbuster" and was hard on the writing, which it found "laughably ridiculous" in its attempts at noir, but it could not stay cross: "The action was too hot, the slo-mo bullet-time too cool. It really never got old." The story was told in comic-book panels made from photographs, and Remedy's writer, Sam Lake, stood in as the face of Max himself.

*Max Payne* sold about 82,000 copies in its first month, *Wireframe* reports, and more than 7.5 million by 2011 once it had reached the PlayStation 2 and Xbox. In May 2002 Take-Two bought the *Max Payne* brand from Remedy and Apogee for $10 million and about 970,000 shares, and commissioned a sequel from Remedy straight away. *Max Payne 2: The Fall of Max Payne* came out in 2003 for PC, PlayStation 2 and Xbox. The series then passed to Rockstar, which made *Max Payne 3* in-house.

### Late Goodbye

*Max Payne 2* ends with a song. Sam Lake had been friends since childhood with Marko Saaresto, who had just formed the band Poets of the Fall, and Lake gave him a poem to turn into lyrics. The result, "Late Goodbye", plays over the end credits. "They had an even bigger role in *Alan Wake*," Lake told *PC Gamer* in 2018: there the band also played a fictional group, Old Gods of Asgard. In *Alan Wake's American Nightmare* the hero's agent is on tour with them.

## Alan Wake, Quantum Break and Control

Remedy's next game was *Alan Wake*, published by [Microsoft](/vault/companies/microsoft) in 2010 for the Xbox 360, with a PC version as well. Its hero is a novelist in a small American town, fighting enemies wrapped in darkness that his torch has to burn away before a bullet can hurt them. *Edge*'s 2011 survey of the Finnish industry praised its "deft talent for toying with players' expectations", and a Remedy recruitment advertisement in the same issue claimed close to 10 million copies sold across the studio's games. A smaller spin-off, *Alan Wake's American Nightmare*, followed in 2012.

When Microsoft unveiled the Xbox One in 2013, it showed a teaser for Remedy's *Quantum Break*. *Edge*'s caption suspected that Phil Spencer's introduction "referenced Max Payne's invention of bullet time with good reason": time was the new game's subject. It came out in 2016 for Xbox One and PC, with a live-action television series woven into the game, whose episodes changed with the player's choices.

*Control* (2019) put the player in the Oldest House, a brutalist office building full of possessed security guards, with a gun and telekinetic powers. Looking back over the console generation in 2020, *Wireframe* called it "one of the most engrossing action games of the past few years", and credited its destruction physics and its architecture.

## Since 2017

Remedy's shares began trading on Nasdaq First North Finland on 29 May 2017 and moved to the main list of Nasdaq Helsinki on 4 May 2022. *Alan Wake 2* came out in October 2023, a survival-horror sequel for PC through the Epic Games Store, PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X|S. Remedy reported in February 2025 that it had sold more than two million copies and had recouped its development and marketing costs, so that the studio had begun to earn royalties.

The next launch went badly. *FBC: Firebreak*, a multiplayer shooter set in the world of *Control*, came out in June 2025. In October Remedy issued a profit warning citing its weak sales, and two weeks later its chief executive of nine years, Tero Virtala, stepped down. The co-founder Markus Mäki, the company's chairman, took over as interim chief executive until Jean-Charles Gaudechon, formerly of Electronic Arts and CCP Games, took the post on 1 March 2026.

Remedy set the worldwide release of *Control Resonant*, the sequel to *Control*, for 24 September 2026. A remake of the first two *Max Payne* games, made with Rockstar, is also in production.
