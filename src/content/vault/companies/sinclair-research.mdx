Sinclair Research was the Cambridge computer company of **[Sir Clive Sinclair](/vault/people/clive-sinclair)**, which he built from 1979 out of the wreckage of his earlier company, Sinclair Radionics. The registered company is older: it was incorporated in 1973 as Westminster Mail Order Limited, carried five names, and outlived the computer business by almost forty years before it was dissolved in 2025. In three years it produced the three machines that made its name: the **ZX80** (1980, £99.95 built), the **ZX81** (1981, £49.95 kit / £69.95 built) and the **ZX Spectrum** (1982, £125 / £175). Together they put programmable computers into hundreds of thousands of British homes.

The Spectrum was the success. By Easter 1983 the company was selling 12,000 a week, and it still took over 40 per cent of the British market in 1986. The stumble was the **[Sinclair QL](/vault/systems/sinclair-ql)** (1984), a 68008-based business computer announced before it worked and delivered four months late in a state *Sinclair User* called bug-ridden. On **7 April 1986** Sinclair sold the computer business, and the right to the Sinclair name on computers, to Alan Sugar's Amstrad for £5 million. Amstrad continued the Spectrum line with the +2 and +3. The company itself carried on under Sir Clive, making other things.

## Before Sinclair Research

Sinclair Research was Clive Sinclair's **third** attempt at a high-technology
company, and the story starts with the failure of the second.

**Sinclair Radionics**, formed in 1961, made amplifiers, pocket televisions and
then calculators. **Chris Curry** joined in 1966; **Nigel Searle** in 1973. The
**Black Watch**, a digital wristwatch, lost the company £355,000 in 1976, and
that year the **National Enterprise Board**, a government investment body, took
a 43% stake for £650,000. It put in more money in 1977, the year the calculator
market collapsed. Radionics also designed a microcomputer that it sold on to
**Newbury Laboratories**; it surfaced two years later as the **NewBrain**.

The board wanted to concentrate on measuring instruments; Sinclair wanted to keep
building his pocket television. In 1979 he split from the NEB and set up
**Science of Cambridge** with Chris Curry, which sold the **MK14** kit micro. Curry
then left and set up **[Acorn](/vault/companies/acorn-computers)**, which went on
to win the contract to build the BBC Micro. Science of Cambridge was renamed
twice, first to Sinclair Computers and then to **Sinclair Research**.

Acorn is sometimes described as founded by Sinclair Research alumni. The order
runs the other way: Curry left the predecessor company, and Acorn was trading
before the Sinclair Research name existed.

## Five names, one company

The period accounts date Science of Cambridge differently: *Sinclair User* puts it
in 1979, *CRASH* at the tail of the Radionics collapse. The public register shows
that the name went onto a company that already existed.

Company **01135105** was incorporated on **19 September 1973** as **Westminster Mail Order
Limited**, and carried five names before it was dissolved:

| Registered as | From |
|---|---|
| Westminster Mail Order Limited | 1973 (incorporated 19 September) |
| Sinclair Instruments Limited | 1976 |
| **Science of Cambridge Limited** | **1977** |
| Sinclair Computers Limited | 1980 |
| **Sinclair Research Limited** | **1981** |

So the company that sold the ZX Spectrum was eight years old when it launched the
machine, and was first registered to sell things by post. The register gives every
change of name the same day, 31 December, so only the years are shown here.

The company also outlived the machines by four decades. The computer business went
to Amstrad in 1986; company 01135105 was not dissolved until **14 October 2025**.

## The cost-reduction playbook

Sinclair Research's signature was getting as much as possible out of the cheapest possible parts. The repeated tricks:

- **Membrane and rubber keyboards** instead of mechanical typewriter keys. Rick Dickinson's membrane keyboard for the ZX81 was, *Sinclair User* said in 1982, "largely responsible for the low retail price of the product"; the Spectrum's keys were moulded rubber.
- **Custom ULA chips** that did the work of many. In the ZX81 one Ferranti chip replaced 18 of the [ZX80](/vault/systems/sinclair-zx80)'s, leaving four chips in the whole computer. The Spectrum's [ULA](/vault/hardware/ula) handled video, memory timing, the keyboard and tape input and output.
- **The family television as the display**, through an RF modulator, so no monitor to buy.
- **Cassette storage** rather than disk: most households already had a tape recorder.
- **Mail order.** The ZX80, ZX81 and Spectrum all started life as mail-order products. The coupons in *Computer & Video Games* in November 1981 give the address as **6 Kings Parade, Cambridge, CB2 1SN**, FREEPOST, no stamp needed. The ZX81 later reached the high street through WH Smith. Mail order also means the customer pays before the machine ships, which mattered when deliveries slipped.

## The other pattern: late, quirky, returned

The cost-reduction story is the one usually told. Running alongside it, from the
Spectrum onward, is a second pattern that the trade press named at the time.

*Sinclair User*, reviewing the company's history in June 1986, put it plainly
about the Spectrum's own launch: mail order only, "a disastrous operation which
left early purchasers waiting two months for delivery. The early machines were
operationally quirky and many of them were returned by customers. **The pattern of
late deliveries and unreliability had begun to emerge.**" The same paragraph
concedes what kept the company alive anyway: "notwithstanding, the machine was
outstanding value".

The peripherals fared worse.

- **Microdrives** (£49.95, 1983), one of Sir Clive's pet projects, were "badly
  savaged by the press": they initially took *longer* to load than cassette tape,
  the tape loops stretched and corrupted what was stored on them, and the drives
  were unreliable.
- **ZX Interface 2**, with its ROM cartridge slot
  and two joystick ports, was withdrawn within two years, and only a handful of
  ROM games were ever produced for it.

## The QL

The **[Sinclair QL](/vault/systems/sinclair-ql)** is usually summarised as an
ambitious 68008 machine let down by Microdrives. The contemporary account is
harsher, and it is about a launch date rather than a design. What follows is what
the QL did to the company; the machine itself has
[its own entry](/vault/systems/sinclair-ql).

Sinclair announced the QL in **January 1984** at £399. *Sinclair User*: "in a
desperate hurry to beat the launch of **Apple's Macintosh**, the QL was rushed out
in an only partly finished form."

What followed, in order:

1. **The machine shown to journalists at the launch "was hardly a QL at all".**
   The operating system on display, written by **GST**, was replaced soon after by
   a Sinclair-commissioned system called **QDOS**, which *Sinclair User* said was
   "widely held to be inferior".
2. **Machines arrived four months after the launch**, bug-ridden, and with "half
   the operating system left hanging out of the back" on an external board.
3. **By May, Sinclair Research was holding £5 million against 13,000 orders, of
   which only a few hundred had been delivered.** The company offered apologetic
   incentives, a free RS-232 lead among them.
4. **A reliable working QL did not appear until late summer 1984**, by which point
   Sinclair had been **criticised by the Advertising Standards Authority** and,
   in *Sinclair User*'s phrase, "hounded by customers".

## 1985: the year it came apart

- **October 1984 to January 1985.** Distributors had "wildly overstocked" the
  Spectrum Plus for Christmas, and when the home-computer market nose-dived in
  January, Sinclair Research "became desperate for money, just when nobody wanted
  to re-order". The planned **stock-market flotation was cancelled**. In 1984 a
  10% float had valued the company at over £130 million.
- **January.** The **C5** electric tricycle, launched by a separate company,
  Sinclair Vehicles, "did nothing to bolster Sir Clive's reputation"; *Sinclair
  User* judged that it "branded him in the media as a kind of 'mad boffin'".
  Production was scaled down and stopped.
- **Summer.** **Robert Maxwell announced a takeover** of Sinclair Research. In
  September *Acorn User* noted that Sir Clive had just turned up as a cartoon
  character in the "Jane" strip of Maxwell's own *Daily Mirror*, a strip the
  paper said had been commissioned ten weeks earlier, and called the timing "pure
  coincidence". By autumn Maxwell's board had rejected the deal and Sinclair was
  on his own again.
- **October.** A financial rescue package satisfied the creditors, who by then
  included **Thorn EMI, Timex, Barclays Bank and AIB**.
- **Christmas.** Most of the debt was repaid through a **£10 million bulk purchase
  of Spectrum Pluses by Dixons**, so a company that had started out selling by post
  was saved by a single retailer's order.
- **February 1986.** At the launch of the 128K Spectrum, Sinclair predicted his
  company would again be "the jewel in the British Computing crown".

Less than eight weeks later he sold it.

## Cambridge, and the people who left

Sinclair Research was based at **6 Kings Parade, Cambridge**, in the city centre
near the university. Industrial designer **[Rick Dickinson](/vault/people/rick-dickinson)**
designed the ZX81, the Spectrum and the QL. The software came from outside:
Sinclair BASIC was written by the Cambridge firm Nine Tiles, and the Spectrum's ROM
by [Steven Vickers](/vault/people/steven-vickers) and John Grant on contract from it.

The Spectrum's designers did not stay. **Richard Altwasser**, who designed its
hardware, left Sinclair in 1982, and **Steven Vickers** left Nine Tiles, to found
**Jupiter Cantab** together, and
their advertising said so in as many words: "leading computer designers Richard
Altwasser and Steven Vickers... after playing the major role in creating the ZX
Spectrum they formed Jupiter Cantab to develop their latest brainchild the
**[Jupiter Ace](/vault/systems/jupiter-ace)**".

The Ace shipped at £89.95 by mail order, with **Forth** rather than BASIC in ROM.
*ACE* magazine, looking back in 1990, remembered it as "the only home computer to
sport Forth as its inbuilt language. This proved to be its downfall." Altwasser
had left the company by 1983, and by the end of that year Jupiter Cantab was in
liquidation, having sold about five or six thousand Aces.

*ACE* filed Jupiter Cantab among the Cambridge companies as "the ill-fated
splinter group from Sinclair Research".

## 7 April 1986

*CRASH*'s John Minson went to the press conference and caught the tone in a
sentence: "If it had happened a week earlier we'd all have thought it was an April
Fool, but at the hastily called press conference at **noon on 7th April** there was
no laughter — just the smiling face of Amstrad's Alan Sugar and the slightly more
restrained features of Sir Clive Sinclair."

**£5 million**, for the worldwide rights to sell and manufacture existing and
future Sinclair computers and the Sinclair brand name for computer products.
Amstrad also bought the existing stock. What it got:

- Control of the **Spectrum 48, Plus and 128**, and first option on **Pandora**,
  the portable then in development.
- A warehouse. *Sinclair User* reported that Amstrad sold over 20,000 rubber-key
  16K and 48K Spectrums, along with most of the remaining QL stock, to an overseas
  distributor in a deal "worth more than £2 million".
- **The QL, which it did not want.** Amstrad announced within weeks that it would
  not pursue the machine, and tried to raise a quarter of a million pounds by
  selling the remaining 19,000 units and throwing in the manufacturing rights free.
  Interested parties included Timex and the QL user group *Quanta*; none of them
  came up with the cash.
- **Loki**, a super-Spectrum "well advanced at the time of the Amstrad take-over",
  named for the Norse god. Sir Clive told *Sinclair User* that summer that it was
  abandoned "so far as we are concerned", and no Loki machine was ever released.

For scale: Amstrad's profits for the last six months of 1985 came in above **£27
million**, so the purchase cost it roughly one month's profit.

The gap between the two men was not only financial. *Your Sinclair* noted the
sight of "the man who did more than anyone to fulfil his vision of a computer in
every home, sitting next to Amstrad boss Alan Sugar, who's called the Spectrum
**a pregnant calculator**". Amstrad's stated plans were to move Sinclair machines
firmly into entertainment, keep them software-compatible, and, in *Your
Sinclair*'s summary, "study why Sinclair products have gained a reputation for
poor quality".

## Legacy

The ZX Spectrum is Sinclair Research's best-known legacy, still commanding over
40 per cent of British machines sold as late as 1986, on *Sinclair User*'s
figures. It also created an industry around itself. *Sinclair User* counted the "cottage
industries" that sprang up to supply the ZX80 and ZX81 with software, add-ons and
publications, and *CRASH* pointed out that dk'tronics and Quicksilva, both still
active in the Spectrum market in 1985, began by making memory expansions for the
ZX80.

*Sinclair User*'s own summary, written eight weeks after the sale, makes the
argument in terms of what the machines cost rather than what they did: "Without
his sub-£100 micros the Commodore 64 might still be £350 and Amstrad certainly
wouldn't ever have become involved in home computers."

The Spectrum was not the best-made computer of its time, and Sinclair's own trade
press said so at length. It was **cheap enough to be in the house**, and being in
the house is what produced a generation who could program.

After the sale Sinclair Research went on without computers. In 1992 it announced
Sir Clive's **Zike** electric bicycle, at £499. Sir Clive died in 2021, and the
company outlived him by four years.
