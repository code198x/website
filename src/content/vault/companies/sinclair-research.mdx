## Overview

Sinclair Research was the Cambridge-based computer company founded by **[Sir Clive Sinclair](/vault/people/clive-sinclair)** in 1979, out of the wreckage of his second company, Sinclair Radionics, and its immediate successor Science of Cambridge. Over the next seven years it produced the three machines that defined British home computing: the **ZX80** (1980, £99.95), the **ZX81** (1981, £49.95 kit / £69.95 built), and the **ZX Spectrum** (1982, £125 / £175). These successive cost-reductions in 8-bit home computing brought programming within reach of British families and seeded the bedroom-coder generation that would go on to dominate UK software development through the 1980s and beyond.

The company's success was the ZX Spectrum, which sold over five million units across its commercial lifetime and was the platform for thousands of British game releases. The company's stumble was the **[Sinclair QL](/vault/systems/sinclair-ql)** (1984), a 68008-based business computer announced before it worked and delivered four months late in a state its own trade press called bug-ridden. On **7 April 1986** Sinclair sold the computer business to Alan Sugar's Amstrad for £5 million. Amstrad continued the Spectrum line with the +2, +3 and +2A; Clive Sinclair kept the name for other ventures.

## Before Sinclair Research

Sinclair Research was Clive Sinclair's **third** attempt at a high-technology
company, and the entry point to the story is the failure of the second.

**Sinclair Radionics**, formed in 1961, made amplifiers, pocket televisions and
then calculators. **Chris Curry** joined in 1966; **Nigel Searle** in 1973. The
calculator market collapsed in 1977, and before that the **Black Watch** — a
digital wristwatch — had cost the company £355,000. The **National Enterprise
Board** took a 43% stake for £650,000 in 1976 and injected a further £19 million
the following year. Radionics also designed a microcomputer that it sold on to
**Newbury Laboratories**; it surfaced two years later as the **NewBrain**.

In 1979 Sinclair split from the NEB and set up **Science of Cambridge** with Chris
Curry, which sold the **MK14** kit micro. Curry then left, and with Hermann Hauser
founded **[Acorn](/vault/companies/acorn-computers)** — the company that would win the schools with the BBC Micro
while Sinclair won the homes. Science of Cambridge was renamed twice, first to
Sinclair Computers and then to **Sinclair Research**.

⚠ [Acorn](/vault/companies/acorn-computers) is sometimes described as founded by Sinclair
Research alumni. The sequence runs the other way: Curry left the **predecessor** company, and
Acorn was already trading before the Sinclair Research name existed. Period accounts also
disagree on when Science of Cambridge was set up — *Sinclair User* puts it at 1979, *CRASH* at
the tail of the Radionics collapse.

## The cost-reduction playbook

Sinclair Research's signature was extracting maximum capability from minimum-cost components. The repeated tricks:

- **Membrane / rubber keyboards** instead of mechanical typewriter keys. Cheap to manufacture, divisive in feel — the Spectrum's rubber keys became iconic but were widely criticised at launch.
- **Custom ULA chips** consolidating multiple functions onto one gate array. The Spectrum's [ULA](/vault/hardware/ula) combined video generation, memory timing, keyboard scanning, and tape I/O onto a single Ferranti-manufactured chip, dramatically reducing component count.
- **TV-as-display** assumption — output via RF modulator to the household television; no dedicated monitor required, no dedicated monitor cost.
- **Cassette storage** rather than disk — every household had a tape recorder; no expensive floppy drive required.
- **Direct mail order** rather than retail — Sinclair Research bypassed retail margins for much of the company's early life, selling through magazine advertisements. The coupons in *Computer & Video Games* from November 1981 give the address as **6 Kings Parade, Cambridge, CB2 1SN**, FREEPOST, no stamp needed. That model is inseparable from what went wrong next: mail order takes the money before it ships the machine.

The result: each successive machine was cheaper than its predecessor in real terms, and each captured a new segment of the British (and European) consumer market.

## The other pattern: late, quirky, returned

The cost-reduction story is the one usually told. Running alongside it, from the
Spectrum onward, is a second pattern that the trade press named at the time.

*Sinclair User*, reviewing the company's history in June 1986, put it plainly
about the Spectrum's own launch: mail order only, "a disastrous operation which
left early purchasers waiting two months for delivery. The early machines were
operationally quirky and many of them were returned by customers. **The pattern of
late deliveries and unreliability had begun to emerge.**" The same paragraph
concedes what kept the company alive anyway — "notwithstanding, the machine was
outstanding value".

The peripherals fared worse.

- **Microdrives** (£49.95, 1983), one of Sinclair's pet projects, were "badly
  savaged by the press": they initially took *longer* to load than cassette tape,
  the tape loops stretched and corrupted what was stored on them, and the drives
  were unreliable.
- **ZX Interface 2**, with its ROM cartridge slot
  and two joystick ports, was withdrawn within two years, and only a handful of
  ROM games were ever produced for it.

## The QL

The **[Sinclair QL](/vault/systems/sinclair-ql)** is usually summarised as an
ambitious 68008 machine let down by Microdrives. The contemporary account is much
harder than that, and it is a story about a launch date rather than a design.
What follows is what the QL did to the company; the machine itself has
[its own entry](/vault/systems/sinclair-ql).

Sinclair announced the QL in **January 1984** at £399. *Sinclair User*: "in a
desperate hurry to beat the launch of **Apple's Macintosh**, the QL was rushed out
in an only partly finished form."

What followed, in order:

1. **The machine shown to journalists at the launch "was hardly a QL at all".**
   The operating system on display, written by **GST**, was replaced soon after by
   a Sinclair-commissioned system called **QDOS** — which *Sinclair User* reckoned
   inferior to the one it displaced.
2. **Machines arrived four months after the launch**, bug-ridden, and with — the
   detail that says everything — "half the operating system left hanging out of
   the back" on an external board.
3. **By May, Sinclair Research was holding £5 million against 13,000 orders, of
   which only a few hundred had been delivered.** The company offered apologetic
   incentives, a free RS-232 lead among them.
4. **A reliable working QL did not appear until late summer 1984**, by which point
   Sinclair had been **criticised by the Advertising Standards Authority** and,
   in *Sinclair User*'s phrase, "hounded by customers".

The figure worth sitting with is the third one. £5 million taken for machines not
yet built is, to the pound, what Sinclair Research was sold for two years later.

## 1985: the year it came apart

- **January.** The home-computer market nose-dived. Sinclair Research, geared for
  growth, "became desperate for money, just when nobody wanted to re-order". The
  planned **stock-market flotation was cancelled** — a year earlier, a 10% float
  had valued the company at over £130 million.
- **January.** The **C5** launched and was savaged. It "did nothing to bolster Sir
  Clive's reputation"; *Sinclair User* judged that it "branded him in the media as
  a kind of 'mad boffin'". Sinclair Vehicles scaled down and stopped.
- **Summer.** **Robert Maxwell announced a takeover** of Sinclair Research, widely
  reported at the time as a rescue — *Acorn User* was joking about it in print,
  Sinclair having simultaneously turned up as a cartoon character in Maxwell's own
  *Daily Mirror*. By autumn Maxwell's board had rejected the deal and Sinclair was
  on his own again.
- **October.** A financial rescue package satisfied the creditors, who by then
  included **Thorn EMI, Timex, Barclays Bank and AIB**.
- **Christmas.** Most of the debt was repaid through a **£10 million bulk purchase
  of Spectrum Pluses by Dixons** — the company that had spent its early life
  avoiding retail was saved by a single retailer's order.
- **February 1986.** At the launch of the 128K Spectrum, Sinclair predicted his
  company would again be "the jewel in the British Computing crown".

Less than eight weeks later he sold it.

## Cambridge, and the people who left

Sinclair Research was based at **6 Kings Parade, Cambridge** — central, and
university-adjacent — and drew on the city's electronics talent. Industrial
designer **Rick Dickinson** shaped the visual identity of the whole ZX line: the
wedge of the ZX81, the Spectrum's rubber keys and rainbow flash, the QL's severe
black. The software came largely from outside: Sinclair BASIC on the ZX80 and
ZX81, and then on the Spectrum, was contracted out.

The Spectrum's own designers did not stay. **Richard Altwasser** and **Steven
Vickers** left in 1982 to found **Jupiter Cantab**, and their advertising said so
in as many words: "leading computer designers Richard Altwasser and Steven
Vickers... after playing the major role in creating the ZX Spectrum they formed
Jupiter Cantab to develop their latest brainchild the **[Jupiter Ace](/vault/systems/jupiter-ace)**".

The Ace shipped at £89.95 by mail order, with **Forth** rather than BASIC in ROM —
a genuinely better idea for a small machine, and, as *ACE* magazine noted looking
back, "this proved to be its downfall". Jupiter Cantab could not fund the promised
add-ons; Altwasser had left the company by 1983. The Spectrum's architects built a
rival to it and the rival died first.

*ACE* filed Jupiter Cantab among the Cambridge companies as "the ill-fated
splinter group from Sinclair Research", which is the right description of what it
was and a fair description of the risk of working there.

## 7 April 1986

*CRASH*'s John Minson went to the press conference and caught the tone in a
sentence: "If it had happened a week earlier we'd all have thought it was an April
Fool, but at the hastily called press conference at **noon on 7th April** there was
no laughter — just the smiling face of Amstrad's Alan Sugar and the slightly more
restrained features of Sir Clive Sinclair."

**£5 million in cash**, for the rights to market and manufacture the Sinclair
computers, the Sinclair name, and the existing stock. What Amstrad got with it:

- Control of the **Spectrum 48, Plus and 128**, and first option on **Pandora**,
  the portable then in development.
- A warehouse. *Sinclair User* reported that Amstrad shifted over 20,000 rubber-key
  16K and 48K Spectrums, along with most of the remaining QL stock, to an overseas
  distributor in a deal "worth more than £2 million" — recovering a good share of
  the purchase price from inventory alone.
- **The QL, which it did not want.** Amstrad announced within weeks that it would
  not pursue the machine, and tried to raise a quarter of a million pounds by
  selling the remaining 19,000 units and throwing in the manufacturing rights free.
  Interested parties included Timex and the QL user group *Quanta*; none of them
  came up with the cash.
- **Loki**, a super-Spectrum "well advanced at the time of the Amstrad take-over",
  named for the Norse god. It was never built.

For scale: Amstrad's profits for the last six months of 1985 came in above **£27
million**. The company that defined British home computing cost its buyer under two
months' earnings.

The gap between the two men was not only financial. *Your Sinclair* noted the
sight of "the man who did more than anyone to fulfil his vision of a computer in
every home, sitting next to Amstrad boss Alan Sugar, who's called the Spectrum
**a pregnant calculator**". Amstrad's stated plans were to move Sinclair machines
firmly into entertainment, keep them software-compatible — and, in *Your
Sinclair*'s summary, to "study why Sinclair products have gained a reputation for
poor quality".

## Legacy

The ZX Spectrum is Sinclair Research's most-cited legacy — still commanding over
40 per cent of British machines sold as late as 1986, on *Sinclair User*'s
figures. The cultural effect is harder to count but larger. The British games
industry of the decade was substantially seeded by Spectrum-era programmers, and
a great many British developers of the 1990s and 2000s started on Sinclair
hardware.

*Sinclair User*'s own summary, written eight weeks after the sale, is the version
worth keeping, because it makes the argument in terms of what the machines cost
rather than what they did: "Without his sub-£100 micros the Commodore 64 might
still be £350 and Amstrad certainly wouldn't ever have become involved in home
computers."

That is the claim this project cares about. The Spectrum's importance is not that
it was good — plenty of contemporaries were better made, and Sinclair's own trade
press said so at length. It is that it was **cheap enough to be in the house**,
and that being in the house is what produced a generation who could program.

The Sinclair Research name effectively ended with the sale. Clive Sinclair kept
it for other ventures — pocket televisions, the Zike electric bicycle — and went
on inventing until his death in 2021. The **C5** is often filed with those, but it
belongs to the story above: it launched in January 1985, while Sinclair Research
was still trading, and the damage it did to his reputation was part of what made
the following year survivable only on someone else's terms.
