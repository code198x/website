## Overview

Ultimate Play the Game — usually just *Ultimate* — was the software label founded by brothers **Tim and Chris Stamper** in Ashby-de-la-Zouch, Leicestershire, in 1982. Over six years they released roughly twenty titles for the [ZX Spectrum](/vault/systems/zx-spectrum), most of them critical hits, several of them platform-defining. *Jetpac* (1983), *Atic Atac* (1983), *Sabre Wulf* (1984), and *Knight Lore* (1984) between them shaped the visual, mechanical, and commercial expectations of British 8-bit gaming. When the Stampers sold the Ultimate brand to US Gold in 1988 and renamed the development side **Rare**, they did so already mythical.

Two characteristics defined Ultimate at the time: technical ambition that consistently exceeded competitors, and a deliberate management of publicity. Games shipped in distinctive matt-black sleeves with no screenshots on the back and no advance previews. Each game was its own product, sold on the reputation of the games before it. In a market where rival publishers ran multi-page magazine ad campaigns for forgettable titles, Ultimate's reticence was its own marketing.

⚠ **"Reclusive" is overstated, and the period record says so.** The Stampers did give
interviews — *Arcade* records Tim and Chris Stamper telling **Roger Kean** the story of
*Knight Lore*'s delayed release. And Ultimate turned up to collect its prizes: at the second
*Computer & Video Games* Golden Joystick awards, reported in June 1985, **Louise Stamper
collected the awards on behalf of Ultimate** at a luncheon in London "and thanked the many
readers of C&VG who voted for them". The mystique was a policy about *games*, not a refusal
to appear in public.

Nor were they hard to find: the trade address was printed in their own advertising —
**Ultimate Play The Game, The Green, Ashby-de-la-Zouch, Leicestershire LE6 5JU**, telephone
0530 411485.

## The Stamper brothers

Tim Stamper (born 1958) and Chris Stamper (1959) trained in electronics and computer science respectively. Before Ultimate they ran **Ashby Computers and Graphics** (ACG), producing arcade conversion work and components. In 1982 they pivoted to home computer publishing and founded Ultimate. Tim handled most of the design and visual work; Chris was the primary programmer. A small additional team of artists and coders joined as the company grew, but the brothers remained the creative and technical core throughout.

The mystery cultivated around them was partly genuine privacy and partly brand strategy. The Stampers preferred working over interviews; the absence of personality-driven press meant the games carried more weight, and the lack of pre-release information meant every release felt like a discovery.

## The 16K cartridge games

The received catalogue starts at *Jetpac* and jumps to *Atic Atac*. Four games sit in
between, and they matter because of the constraint they were written under.

*Computer & Video Games*, looking back in 1996: "***JETPAC* and *PSST*, their first two
games, were crammed into just 16K** and also came out on the **Sinclair Interface II**" —
that is, on **ROM cartridge**, not cassette. "Then came ***COOKIE***, ***TRANS AM*** and the
awesome ***LUNAR JETMAN***."

Six games before *Atic Atac*, several of them in 16K, some of them shipping as cartridges
on a peripheral almost nobody remembers. The technical reputation the company traded on for
the rest of the decade was built in that space.

## The Spectrum catalogue

**Jetpac (1983)** — Ultimate's debut, a single-screen shooter with the player assembling a rocket while fending off aliens. Smooth animation and tight game-feel established the company's technical reputation in a single £5.50 cassette.

**[Atic Atac](/vault/games/atic-atac) (1983)** — Top-down haunted-castle adventure with three playable characters, a food meter, and 159 rooms across four floors. The game that founded the multi-room adventure template Ultimate would expand for the next year.

**[Sabre Wulf](/vault/games/sabre-wulf) (1984)** — 256-screen open jungle maze with smooth screen-to-screen transitions and the iconic green-skinned Sabreman. The first of the four-game Sabreman series.

**Underwurlde (1984)** — Sabreman sequel set in underground caverns; a flick-screen adventure where Sabreman ventures below the world.

**[Knight Lore](/vault/games/knight-lore) (1984)** — The isometric breakthrough. The game that introduced *Filmation* (Ultimate's isometric engine) and changed expectations of what the Spectrum could do.

**Alien 8 (1985)**, **Nightshade (1985)**, **Gunfright (1985)** — Filmation engine reused across new settings. Each was technically impressive; commercially they sold well but increasingly faced competitor isometric games.

**Pentagram (1986)** — The fourth Sabreman game; lower-profile than its predecessors and notable mostly for completing the series.

**Cyberun (1985)**, **Bubbler (1987)** — Late-era titles, increasingly facing the platform's decline.

## The Filmation engine

*Knight Lore* introduced an isometric 3D rendering system that Ultimate called **Filmation**. Solid 3D objects could occlude each other; the player character could walk behind columns and re-emerge; rooms had real spatial depth. On the Spectrum's 8-bit Z80 at 3.5 MHz, with no hardware sprite or scrolling support, *Knight Lore* drew and depth-sorted a full isometric scene in software. ⚠ This entry previously stated a figure of 25 frames per second; no source in this library supports it, and it is not credible for a full-room redraw, so it has been removed rather than replaced. The technical achievement was widely held to have advanced the platform by a year overnight; competitors scrambled to produce isometric games of their own, and the genre that emerged — *Batman* (1986), *Head Over Heels* (1987), *La Abadía del Crimen* (1987) — owed its existence to Ultimate's engine.

Ultimate reused Filmation across *Alien 8*, *Nightshade*, *Gunfright*, and *Pentagram*. The engine's existence was Ultimate's calling card for the rest of their Spectrum work.

*CRASH*'s own summary of the consequence is the sharpest line anyone wrote about it:
*Knight Lore* was "**the second most cloned piece of software after WordStar**".

### Held back on purpose

The detail that explains Ultimate's release strategy better than any amount of writing about
black sleeves: ***Knight Lore* was finished before the game that preceded it.**

*Arcade* records Tim and Chris Stamper revealing to Roger Kean that the game had been ready
for release months earlier — and that, **anxious to avoid rendering all following games
disappointments**, they held it back. *Sabre Wulf* shipped first and sold on its own terms;
*Knight Lore* followed and made everything on the shelf look old, including *Sabre Wulf*.

A company sitting on its best work so as not to devalue the release in front of it is not
being mysterious. It is managing a catalogue with more discipline than any of its
competitors managed.

## The end of Ultimate, the start of Rare

The Stampers sold the Ultimate brand and back catalogue to **US Gold**.

⚠ **The date is not verifiable from this library.** This entry has carried **1988**; no
period source held here states when the sale happened, and Ultimate-branded games were still
being listed with Ultimate as publisher through 1986. The date is left unstated rather than
swapped for another unsourced one. The Spectrum market was shrinking; the brothers wanted to develop for Nintendo's NES and the imminent SNES. They renamed their development arm **Rare Ltd.** and pivoted to Nintendo platforms. Rare produced *R.C. Pro-Am*, *Battletoads*, *Donkey Kong Country* (the SNES game that re-launched the platform commercially in 1994), *GoldenEye 007*, *Banjo-Kazooie*, *Perfect Dark*, and many more. Microsoft acquired Rare in 2002 for $375 million, and the studio continues as a first-party Xbox developer.

For the Stampers, Ultimate was the proving ground. The skills they showed on the Spectrum — squeezing more from limited hardware than anyone thought possible, building distinctive visual identities, secrecy as marketing — they carried into every Rare title that followed.

## Why Ultimate matters to Code Like It's 198x

For Shadowkeep specifically, Ultimate is the direct ancestor. Atic Atac defines the multi-room adventure template; Sabre Wulf shows the engine's expansion to open-world scope; the technical ambition across the catalogue is the bar against which Code198x's assembly tutorials measure themselves. The brief calls Shadowkeep a *remix unit* of Atic Atac — same tropes, one new central mechanic. Without Ultimate, Shadowkeep doesn't exist.
