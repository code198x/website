**Future Publishing** began in 1985 as one man and one magazine in a small Somerset town. Chris Anderson published the first issue of *[Amstrad Action](/vault/magazines/amstrad-action)* from Somerton in October that year. Six years later, from offices in Bath, his company told readers of *[Amiga Format](/vault/magazines/amiga-format)* that it was "a company founded just six years ago, but which now sells more computer magazines than any other publisher in Britain". Its formula was one magazine for each machine, written by enthusiasts, usually with a disk or tape of programs fixed to the cover.

## From ZZAP!64 to Somerton

Anderson had spent the first half of 1985 launching someone else's magazine. *[CRASH](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine)* introduced him in March 1985 as [Newsfield](/vault/companies/newsfield) put together the team for *[ZZAP!64](/vault/magazines/zzap-64)*: "Heading up the editorial team is Chris Anderson, who was editor of *PCG* until he heard about *ZZAP! 64*." *ZZAP!64* issue 1, dated May 1985, names him as editor. Five months later *Amstrad Action* issue 1 appeared, edited by Peter Connor, "another veteran of PCG", with Future's address at Somerton, Somerset.

For its first two years Future was a one-magazine company. Its second title, *[ACE](/vault/magazines/ace-magazine)*, launched in October 1987 from 4 Queen Street, Bath, as a multi-format magazine "courtesy of the team who produced the legendary [*Personal Computer Games*](/vault/magazines/personal-computer-games)". Subscriptions and mail order stayed behind at The Old Barn in Somerton for several more years.

## A magazine for every machine

By December 1988 Future's order form listed *ACE*, *PC Plus*, *8000 Plus*, *Amstrad Action*, *ST Amiga Format* and *New Computer Express*. *ST/Amiga Format*, launched in July 1988 with Anderson as publisher, covered both of the new 16-bit computers in one magazine. A year later Future split it in two. The July 1989 issue announced that from the next month two magazines would take its place: *Amiga Format* would reach the shops on Thursday 13 July and *[ST Format](/vault/magazines/st-format)* a week later, each with its own 3.5-inch disk. The same news page noted that *ACE* was "no longer published by Future Publishing, having been taken over by the London-based EMAP".

More single-machine magazines followed:

- **[Commodore Format](/vault/magazines/commodore-format)**, for the Commodore 64, in October 1990.
- **[Your Sinclair](/vault/magazines/your-sinclair)**, bought from Dennis Publishing, from its April 1990 issue. The magazine told readers that Future's chairman had only been trying to buy a second-hand Sinclair C5 and dialled a wrong number.
- **[Amiga Shopper](/vault/magazines/amiga-shopper)** and **[Amiga Power](/vault/magazines/amiga-power)**, both in the spring of 1991. *Amiga Format*'s review of the year put their launch in April.
- **[Edge](/vault/magazines/edge)**, a multi-format games magazine, in October 1993.

By the summer of 1991 the house advertisement in *Amiga Format* listed twelve titles under the line "Home of Britain's finest computer magazines": *Amiga Format*, *ST Format*, *Amiga Power*, *Amiga Shopper*, *PC Answers*, *PC Plus*, *8000 Plus*, *Amstrad Action*, *Your Sinclair*, *Sega Power*, *New Computer Express* and *Commodore Format*. The same page set out the company's rules. Its editors worked "under two golden rules": "Understand your readers' needs" and "Satisfy them". Its reviews came with "a cast-iron policy of editorial independence", and buying one of its magazines was "like joining a nationwide user group".

## Disks and tapes on the cover

Most of Future's magazines came with programs on the cover. *Amiga Format* carried a disk from its first issue in 1989. That disk held "880K of programs", including a playable demo of *The New Zealand Story*. *ACE* promised the first five levels of *Bubble Bobble* on a cassette with its second issue, in 1987. *Amstrad Action* put a covertape on every issue from April 1991, and *Commodore Format* had a tape from its first issue. By mid-1996 *Amiga Format* also came in a CD-ROM edition.

The giveaways worried the software houses. In 1991 *Amiga Format* gave away complete games, among them *Interphase*. It later said that the industry's reaction was "concern: if AF gave you games worth £30 for nothing, who'd buy theirs? An agreement was reached and we were prevented from doing it again". Its timeline of that year summed it up as "No more free games from mags."

## Pearson and after

*Edge* reported in January 1995 that Anderson had sold Future to Pearson's New Entertainment division for £52.5 million (later accounts give £52.7 million); he bought it back in 1998. Period magazines confirm the ownership: in March 1995 the Italian magazine *Zeta* described Pearson as the group "che controlla anche la Future Publishing" ("which also controls Future Publishing"). In 1999 the company was floated on the London Stock Exchange. The last issue of *Amiga Format*, in May 2000, described the parent company as The Future Network, "a public company quoted on the London Stock Exchange (symbol: FNET)". It said the group published more than 115 magazines from offices in five countries and licensed 42 more in 30 countries.

Future also expanded abroad through purchases. In September 1999 the German monthly *[PC Player](/vault/magazines/pc-player)* reported, in a note written "praktisch direkt vor dem Belichten unserer Seitenfilme" (just before its page films went for exposure), that it now belonged to Future Verlag GmbH instead of the WEKA publishing group. For readers who had not heard of the buyer, it explained that Future published "alles in allem 108 Magazine" (108 magazines in all), including market leaders in Britain, Italy, France and the United States, such as the American *PC Gamer*.

The company still trades, now as Future plc.
