Intelligent Systems has made Nintendo games for more than forty years without ever becoming a household name. Its founders programmed *Duck Hunt* and *Metroid* before the company existed. It turned war into a board game with *Famicom Wars* in 1988, built a fantasy saga on the same grid with *Fire Emblem* in 1990, and made *Advance Wars*, the game *Edge* called "the best game available for the Game Boy Advance". Behind the games sits a second business that players never see: the development tools that other studios have used to write games for Nintendo's machines, from the Game Boy onwards.

## Fast facts

- **Origins:** from 1983, a department of Iwasaki Giken Kogyo developing games for [Nintendo](/vault/companies/nintendo).
- **Founded:** December 1986, in Kyoto, when that department became a company of its own.
- **Leaders:** Ryoichi Kitanishi, representative director from 1986; Toshiyuki Nakamura, president since 2010.
- **Strategy series:** *Famicom Wars* (1988) and its successors, including *[Advance Wars](/vault/games/advance-wars)* (2001); *[Fire Emblem](/vault/games/fire-emblem)* (1990 onwards).
- **With Nintendo teams:** *[Super Metroid](/vault/games/super-metroid)* (1994), the *Paper Mario* series (from 2000), *Mario Kart: Super Circuit* (2001), the *WarioWare* series (from 2003).
- **Tools:** development tools for the Game Boy (1989), Super Famicom (1990) and each Nintendo handheld since.
- **Size:** 223 staff in April 2026, 98 of them graphic designers and 71 programmers.

## Famicom games under contract

The company's own history begins in 1983, the year the Famicom went on sale. A department of Iwasaki Giken Kogyo, a company since dissolved, took on game programming for Nintendo, and the history lists the Famicom games its founding members worked on there: *[Mario Bros.](/vault/games/mario-bros)*, *Tennis*, *Wild Gunman*, *[Duck Hunt](/vault/games/duck-hunt)*, *Hogan's Alley*, *Donkey Kong 3*, *Devil World*, *Soccer*, *[Wrecking Crew](/vault/games/wrecking-crew)*, *Gyromite*, *Stack-Up* and *[Metroid](/vault/games/metroid)*. Many of these were [Nintendo R&D1](/vault/companies/nintendo-rd1) games, designed inside Nintendo: R&D1's Hiroji Kiyotake started work on *Duck Hunt*.

The company's website recalls how small it was. The author of its top message writes that the first game they programmed was *Tennis*, released soon after the Famicom itself, by a team of three university students who loved computers, working in one room of a small flat. With no division of labour and almost no development tools, they did the design and graphics as well as the program. In the company's words, its founders built all the tools and know-how they needed themselves, because none existed.

In December 1986 the department became independent as Intelligent Systems, with Ryoichi Kitanishi as representative director and two development labs. Western accounts, including a 2017 *Shacknews* history, name Tohru Narihiro as the founder, and say Nintendo contracted him to convert Famicom Disk System games to cartridges for the NES. The company's own history does not mention Narihiro. He appears in the credits of the first *Fire Emblem* under special thanks, alongside Kitanishi.

## War on a grid

*Famicom Wars* (1988) set red and blue armies against each other on a map of squares, turn by turn, with factories to build new units. *Fire Emblem* (1990) kept the grid and the turns and gave every unit a name, a story and one life. Its credits show how the work was shared: Nintendo's [Gunpei Yokoi](/vault/people/gunpei-yokoi) produced it and Nintendo's Keisuke Terasaki directed, while the scenario was by Shouzou Kaga of Intelligent Systems and most of the programmers and artists came from the company too. Yuka Tsujiyoko of Intelligent Systems wrote music with Nintendo's [Hirokazu Tanaka](/vault/people/hip-tanaka). *Nintendo Power* later wrote that Nintendo had "took a chance on an unusual game" that combined strategy and role-playing, and that it became "a cult classic".

Both series stayed in Japan for more than a decade. *Game Boy Wars* followed in 1991 and *Super Famicom Wars* in 1998, and the *Fire Emblem* games moved to the Super Famicom. Nintendo's director Kentaro Nishimura later told *Edge* that when he joined Nintendo he was told turn-based games "would never be successful abroad".

## Working alongside Nintendo

Through the 1990s Intelligent Systems also worked inside Nintendo's own projects. On *Super Metroid* (1994), made with R&D1, the Intelligent Systems staff were all programmers, and they worked with the game's main designer on what the Super Famicom could display. It made *Galactic Pinball* for the Virtual Boy and the puzzle game *Panel de Pon* for the Super Famicom in 1995. In 1998 *Edge* reported that a sequel to Square's *Super Mario RPG* was to be developed by Intelligent Systems for the 64DD disk drive; it reached the Nintendo 64 in 2000 as *Paper Mario*, the start of a series the company still makes.

## Advance Wars

*Advance Wars* began, Nishimura told *Edge*, because Nintendo was too busy with the Game Boy Advance's launch games and asked Intelligent Systems to share the work. Director Makoto Shimojo said the team aimed its bright, rounded look at young children, then found from the sales figures that it had mostly sold to teenage boys. It was the sixth game in the series and the first released outside Japan, and it came with a full tutorial, because the team had decided to make the game "really easy to play" even before an overseas release was planned. It came out in America in September 2001. Nintendo held back the British release until 2002, wary of a game with "war" in its title after the attacks of 11 September. Japan got it last, in November 2004, packaged with its sequel.

The reviews were among the best any handheld game had received. *Edge* called it "the closest the format has to perfection", and in 2002 made Nintendo its developer of the year, counting Intelligent Systems, for *Advance Wars* and *Mario Kart: Super Circuit*, as part of Nintendo's in-house talent. *Super Circuit* was the first *Mario Kart* not made by Nintendo alone. Looking back in 2013, *Edge* found something unexpected in all the company's strategy games: they have "great rhythm", and "none with as consummate a tempo as *Advance Wars*".

## Tools

The development-tool business runs alongside the games. The company's history lists tools for the Game Boy in 1989 and the Super Famicom in 1990, then for the Game Boy Color, Game Boy Advance, Nintendo DS, DSi, 3DS and New 3DS. In 1987 it set up a joint company with Nintendo, Tech, which it absorbed in 1997. Nintendo's own interview pages describe Intelligent Systems as a company that develops Nintendo games and "software development support tools" for its systems.

## Since 2003

*Fire Emblem* reached the West in 2003 with the Game Boy Advance game known there simply as *Fire Emblem*. From 2003 Intelligent Systems also worked on the *WarioWare* series of five-second microgames; *Edge* reported in 2005 that a 15-strong team from the company, led by art director Naoko Mori, collaborated with Nintendo's developers. Later games included the puzzle game *Pullblox*, the company's first 3DS game, and more *Paper Mario* games.

By the early 2010s *Fire Emblem* was in trouble. In a 2015 *Iwata Asks* interview, Nintendo's producer Hitoshi Yamagami said that the team intended *Fire Emblem: Awakening* (2012) "to be the last in the series", because Nintendo's head of sales had told them the series "isn't making the numbers". *Awakening* sold well enough to change his mind. *Awakening*'s director, Kohei Maeda of Intelligent Systems, told *Edge* that the series' newcomer mode, in which fallen characters return, was there because many beginners stayed away thinking the games difficult, "so that those kinds of people could play Fire Emblem too". The series has continued since with *Fire Emblem Heroes* for smartphones (2017), *Three Houses* (2019), *Engage* (2023) and *Fortune's Weave* for the Nintendo Switch 2 (2026), alongside a remake of *Paper Mario: The Thousand-Year Door* (2024). The company moved into a new building in Minami-ku, Kyoto, in 2013.
