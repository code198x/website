Before 1995 a games box usually showed a painting of the hero or a screenshot, under a logo in lettering borrowed from fantasy novels. Then Psygnosis's *[WipEout](/vault/games/wipeout)* arrived for the new PlayStation in a package that looked like a dance record: clean type, team logos for its racing craft, warning signs and arrows. The look came from The Designers Republic, a graphic design studio in Sheffield best known for record sleeves. Its founder, Ian Anderson, told *Edge* the point was to avoid "the usual Dungeons and Dragons lettering" and a screen grab, and "to come up with something different to anything that had gone before".

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 14 July 1986, in Sheffield, by Ian Anderson with Nick Phillips.
- **Known for:** record sleeves and identities for Pop Will Eat Itself, Warp Records and many bands.
- **Games:** *WipEout* (1995), *WipEout 2097* (1996) and *Wip3out* (1999) for Psygnosis; work for Gremlin's *Hardwar* and the packaging of *Mass Destruction*.
- **2009:** voluntary liquidation; Anderson bought back the name and assets and restarted the studio.

## Sleeves and posters

Anderson was born in Croydon in 1961 and came to Sheffield in 1979 to read philosophy at the university. He joined the city's music scene and managed a band, Person to Person, and *Retro Gamer* records that the studio began in 1986 to design posters for a band he was managing. *Eye* magazine dates the declaration of The Designers Republic to Bastille Day, 14 July 1986, made with Nick Phillips.

By 1996 *Edge* could call it "one of the UK's top graphic design companies". It had first come to prominence, the magazine said, by giving the band Pop Will Eat Itself "a stunningly original design identity", and it was designing for the dance labels Warp and React and for Supergrass, Pulp and Sleeper. It also ran a Sheffield nightclub, The Republic. *Retro Gamer* describes its style as combining "ironic messages and hyper-consumerism with minimalist and constructivist art influences", and *Creative Review* called it "much copied".

## WipEout

The link with *WipEout* came from inside Psygnosis. "We were contacted by people in the Psygnosis art department who were Designers Republic fans," Anderson told *Edge*. "They were aware of some quite obscure stuff we'd done." *Retro Gamer* says the studio was first hired only for the box. Then the artist Lee Carus, working late on the game's opening sequence, noticed a fax from the studio on a colleague's desk. "I thought to myself, 'That fax header is cool!'" he told *Edge*. He scanned it and mapped it onto the side of one of the racing craft, and the team agreed that it suited the game. From there, Anderson said, "we developed the logos, followed by weapon icons for use on-screen", as well as the packaging for the game and its music CD.

For Psygnosis this was new. "Wipeout was a real first," said Nicky Carus-Westcott, the game's chief artist and Psygnosis's link with the studio. "Before, every element of our games had been done in-house, including the music." *Edge* listed the studio's work beyond the packaging alongside the licensed dance soundtrack and the compilation album as "firsts for a videogame". The studio's designer on the game was Michael C. Place, whom *Edge* later called "Wipeout's designer at The Designers Republic".

The partnership lasted for the first three games. For *WipEout 2097* in 1996, *Computer and Video Games* reported, "once again Sheffield boys Designers Republic have been employed to reestablish Wipeout's cool branding"; the credits list them for "Additional Graphic Design and Styling" and the original packaging. For *Wip3out* in 1999, the team at Psygnosis's Leeds studio wanted to strip the look back. "Mike from The Designers Republic loves to keep things simple," one of the developers told *Retro Gamer*; "they took a step back and gave us pure design." *Edge* noted the studio's "stylised Japanese touches" on the game's single city. That December, Psygnosis won the design award at BAFTA's Interactive Entertainment Awards for the game, "as aided by Designers' Republic", in *Edge*'s words. *Retro Gamer* says it was the last *WipEout* the studio designed.

## Other games

*WipEout*'s success brought other offers. In 1997 *Computer and Video Games* reported that "following the success of WipEout, Gremlin have also employed the Designers Republic to do the artwork and in-game logos" for *Hardwar*, a trading and combat game set on an alien planet, for which a deal with the studio's long-standing client Warp Records was about to be signed for the music. The same spring, *PC Zone* printed a Designers Republic logo for the tank game *Mass Destruction*, whose credits name the studio for its logo and packaging. Its look travelled further than its contracts: in 1996 *Edge* noticed that Ocean's advertisements for *Raiden* and *Zero Divide* shared imagery with the studio's work, though Ocean said the pictures came from Japan.

## After WipEout

Michael C. Place left in 2000 and later set up his own studio, Build, in London. In 2012 Build designed the logo, packaging and in-game typeface of *[Lumines](/vault/games/lumines)*: *Electronic Symphony*, which *Edge* reported as the designers' "second-ever game series" after *WipEout*.

The Designers Republic closed for business in January 2009, after 23 years. *Eye* reported that it went into voluntary liquidation, and *The Drum* listed the reasons Anderson gave: lost clients, unpaid fees, failed pitches and a large tax bill. Anderson bought back the name and assets and restarted it as a smaller studio, with Coca-Cola among its first clients. It was still working in the 2020s. Thames & Hudson published a survey of its work, *A to Z of The Designers Republic*, and in August 2024 the Saatchi Gallery in London held a talk with Anderson to mark it.
