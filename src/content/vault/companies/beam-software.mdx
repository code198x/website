**Beam Software** was an Australian game developer founded in 1980 in Melbourne. Creators of The Hobbit's revolutionary English parser and numerous licensed games, Beam became one of the southern hemisphere's most prolific studios before being absorbed into Krome Studios in 2006.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1980
- **Location:** Melbourne, Australia
- **Known for:** [The Hobbit](/vault/games/the-hobbit), [licensed games](/vault/culture/licensed-games)

## Two companies, not one

The single most common error about Beam is treating it and
[Melbourne House](/vault/companies/melbourne-house) as the same firm under two names. They
were not. *ACE* set the arrangement out in February 1990:

> The original Melbourne House was set up as a **UK software publishing base in 1982** for
> Australia-based **Fred and Naomi Milgrom**. Product was sourced mainly from the
> **Milgroms' Beam Software.**

So: a **development house in Melbourne**, and a **publishing operation in London** that
existed to sell what the development house made. Both belonged to the same owners, which is
why the names are used interchangeably — but they had separate fates, and only one of them
was sold.

*ACE* continues: "Melbourne House became part of **[Mastertronic](/vault/companies/mastertronic)**
and today still exists as a **label within the Virgin Mastertronic group**." The 1989
*Amiga* trade guide confirms the shape from the other side of the world, listing the company
in its vendor directory as "**Melbourne House — Distributed by Mastertronic
International**".

Beam Software was not part of that sale. *The Australian Commodore and Amiga Review*
records the Australian half of the same moment: "**in Australia Melbourne House disappeared
and became SLM Publishing**" — while, as its own 1988 interview puts it, "**Beam Software
is still a strong firm and is still churning out the classics**".

The date is **February 1987**, and the source that settles it is *ACE*'s own review of the
year, printed in February 1988 while the events were twelve months old at most:

> **Mastertronic swallowed up Melbourne House in February; eight months later, Virgin bought
> substantial minority interest in Mastertronic.**

⚠ Note that *ACE* contradicts itself. The February 1990 "where are they now?" survey quoted
above says the sale happened "in 1986". Prefer the 1988 review: it is contemporaneous, it is
specific to the month, and it places the sale correctly relative to Virgin's stake in
Mastertronic. The Australian timeline's apparent 1988 dating is unusable: that page runs interleaved columns, and the item cannot reliably be attached to a month.

Alfred Milgrom later explained the reasoning to *Edge*: the company did not have the depth
to run publishing and development at once, so it **sold the publishing side and kept Beam**.
That is the transaction in one sentence, and it is why Beam outlived the label by more than
twenty years.

## The size of it

"Australian developer" undersells what Beam had become by the middle of the decade. *CRASH*
reckoned that by 1987 the studio employed **25 programmers, working in groups of four or
five, each group taking a game from start to finish** — which made it, in the magazine's
estimate, **the biggest programming team outside Japan and the United States**.

That is the context for everything else here: not a bedroom operation that got lucky with a
Tolkien licence, but the largest development house in the industry outside the two countries
that dominated it, running a studio structure that most British publishers of the period did
not have.

## The Nintendo turn

The *Shadowrun* line in the games table above is not an outlier — it is the whole business by
that point. *ACE*, February 1990, in a "where are they now?" survey:

> In Australia, **Beam Software is now a Nintendo-approved developer and works virtually
> exclusively on Nintendo games for the Japanese and American market.**

A studio that made its name on British 8-bit text adventures spent the turn of the decade
building for the NES and SNES, for territories on the other side of the Pacific. The 1988
interview already shows the pressure: asked about writing parsers, the team notes that the
American market "is almost not the same language they're talking over there; **they have
different grammar and they will say things very differently**".

## Inside the adventure system, 1988

*The Australian Commodore and Amiga Review* visited the studio in April 1988 while
*Shadows of Mordor* was finishing, and named the team: **John Harwood** (project leader),
**Paul Kidd** (storyline developer) and **Norton Truter** (programmer). Harwood explains
that he "**inherited the development of Shadows of Mordor when Phillip Mitchell left, who
mainly developed the adventure system**" — placing Mitchell, the programmer of *The
Hobbit*, as the author of the engine that outlived his own tenure.

Their working method was unusually blunt about its own failures. On the first *Lord of the
Rings*:

> There were a lot of complaints about the first Lord of the Rings game, particularly part 1,
> in that it was different from Tolkien… so we went through it ruthlessly and eliminated many
> things that were really quite interesting… I had the habit of **collecting all the hate
> mail that we get**, and so collected together the complaints that we could do something
> about.

## "I loathe adventure games"

The same interview produced the line that followed Beam around. It belongs to **Paul Kidd**,
the storyline developer, answering whether you need to be good at adventure games to make
one:

> **I loathe adventure games. I hate wrestling with parsers, they bore me to tears.** I don't
> see why anyone would voluntarily sit there and constantly try and tell the machine what
> they know it is going to accept but they can't find the words for it. So, I try and avoid
> that as much as possible!

The magazine's own retrospective, a year later, recorded the fallout: the interview "**gained
them criticism in UK magazines**", because quotes like that "**didn't sound too good from
producers of top selling adventure games**".

It is worth reading as design philosophy rather than heresy. The man writing the stories
hated the interface his own house was famous for, and said so — which is a fair account of
why Beam's parser work kept pushing at the limits of verb-noun input in the first place.

## Legacy

Beam Software proved that world-class game development could happen outside traditional centres. The Hobbit's parser technology was years ahead of competitors, and the studio's thirty-year run produced numerous memorable titles.
