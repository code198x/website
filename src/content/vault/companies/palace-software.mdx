## Overview

Palace Software was the games arm of **Palace Pictures**, the London independent that distributed *The Evil Dead* in Britain and produced *Mona Lisa* and *The Company of Wolves*. That parentage shows: Palace's games were sold with a **film company's instincts about publicity**.

## Fast facts

- **Parent:** Palace Pictures.
- **Seen in the press:** reviewed in *CRASH*, *ZZAP!64*, *Amstrad Action* and *Amiga Power* between **1985 and 1992**, at **£8.99–£25.99**.
- **Known for:** *Barbarian: The Ultimate Warrior*, *Cauldron* and *Cauldron II*, *Rimrunner*, *Antiriad*.

## Cauldron

Before the notoriety there was *Cauldron* (1985), a witch-themed platform and flying game with a distinctive look, and *Cauldron II: The Pumpkin Strikes Back*, which reversed the premise and put you in control of a bouncing pumpkin. Both were well regarded and are the reason Palace had a reputation before *Barbarian* gave it a different one.

## Barbarian, and the poster

*Barbarian: The Ultimate Warrior* (1987) was a one-on-one sword-fighting game with a decapitation move — enough to attract attention on its own in a market where fighting games were rare and gore rarer.

What made it a national talking point was the **advertising**. Palace shot its packaging and press campaign with the model **Maria Whittaker**, then closely associated with *The Sun*'s Page 3, in a fur bikini beside a barbarian. It was a film-marketing tactic applied to a computer game, and it worked exactly as intended: coverage, complaints, and a title everybody had heard of.

Read from here it is a straightforward piece of eighties tabloid sexism, and it drew objections at the time too — including from within the games press. It is also, unavoidably, one of the most recognisable images British games advertising produced, which is why the game is remembered and its many contemporaries are not.

## Why it mattered

Palace is the sharpest example of **film-industry marketing arriving in games**. The techniques were novel in this market — a star face, a poster campaign, deliberate provocation — and the results demonstrated that a game's visibility could be manufactured, not earned by reviews.

That lesson did not stay in 1987. Much of what the games industry now does routinely in marketing was, at this point, a distributor of art-house films trying something.
