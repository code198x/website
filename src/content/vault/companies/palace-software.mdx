## Overview

Palace Software is usually introduced as the games division of **Palace Pictures**, the
London independent behind *The Company of Wolves* and *Mona Lisa*. That is the wrong end
of the company. In December 1987 **Pete Stone**, who ran it, told *Amstrad User* where
the group came from:

> The very first part of the Palace group was **The Video Palace**, which I originally set
> up. This was **London's first video mega-store**.

Berwick Street, Soho. Stone had come "from the record industry", and games reached him
across a shop counter.

## Fast facts

- **Based:** London — offices above the **Scala cinema**, Pentonville Road.
- **Run by:** **Pete Stone**.
- **Named in the press as:** **Steve Brown**, designer (*Cauldron*, *Cauldron II*,
  *Barbarian*, *Barbarian II*, *Rimrunner*); **[Dan Malone](/vault/people/dan-malone)**, artist
  (*The Sacred Armour of Antiriad*); **Richard Leinfellner**, first programmer;
  **Richard Joseph**, musician; **Dave Chapman**, who programmed *Barbarian II* on Amiga.
- **Press evidence in this library:** *CRASH*, *ZZAP!64*, *Amstrad Action*, *ACE* and
  *Amiga Power* between **1985 and 1992**, at **£8.99–£25.99**.
- **Confirmed *CRASH* scores:** *Cauldron* **91%** (issue 18, Jul 1985, p34),
  *Cauldron II* **91%** (issue 31, Aug 1986, p113), *Barbarian* **85%** (issue 41,
  Jun 1987).
- **Second label:** **Outlaw Productions**, launched late 1987.

## The shop floor

Stone dates his involvement to "the retail end, right back at the start of computer
games, in **'82–'83**", and his description of the trade at that moment is the best
one-paragraph picture of the British software industry before it had an industry:

> We were actually buying programs from the programmers in those days. They'd come in with
> **cardboard boxes and carrier bags full of cassettes**, often with just typed or even
> handwritten labels. And we were selling them by the lorry load.

The first Palace programmer came off that same shop floor. **Richard Leinfellner** "used
to hang around the store, playing with the computers, while he was still at college.
Eventually we gave him a job because he was one of the few people in the shop who knew
what he was talking about."

## The Evil Dead, and where the instinct came from

Palace's first game was an adaptation of ***The Evil Dead***,
a film Palace Pictures had just put into British cinemas and onto video. Stone is blunt
about the game — "By today's standards it was rather awful, but it was good experience
because we saw the direction that we needed to follow" — and completely unembarrassed
about what happened around it.

By the time the game shipped, *The Evil Dead* was at the centre of the **video nasties**
panic and "being confiscated all over the country". Stone:

> there's an old saying that **no publicity is bad publicity**, and the outrage helped
> ensure good sales for the game.

That is worth pinning down, because it moves the story of Palace's marketing back three
years and out of the *Barbarian* section where it usually sits. The company learned that
a moral panic sells copies in **1984**, from a horror film, in the middle of the same
scare that produced the **Video Recordings Act 1984** — the Act
[CRL](/vault/companies/crl-group) would later invoke to get its games certificated.
Palace and CRL took opposite lessons from the same events: CRL submitted itself for
classification, Palace did not.

## Four jobs, not one

The other thing Stone was doing early is more quietly influential. Asked how Palace made
games, he describes a division of labour that reads as unremarkable now and was not then:

> We realised that the best way to create a successful program, at least for us, was as a
> **team effort**. Principally we wanted **an artist, a programmer and a musician**.

A fourth role surfaced during *Cauldron*: gameplay, as a job someone owns. The company had
to go looking for the artist, since nobody was advertising for one, and found **Steve
Brown** — who came in to do graphics and ended up running the game. Stone: "Steve had come
in to handle graphics but was taking over the making of *Cauldron*, throwing in more and
more good ideas until it became **his game** in the end."

*Cauldron* (1985) started as a Halloween-movie licence, was abandoned as a bad scenario,
and became witches and pumpkins instead. It took **91%** in *CRASH*. *Cauldron II: The
Pumpkin Strikes Back* inverted the premise, put the player in the pumpkin, and took **91%**
again — and was "based almost solely on Steve's ideas".

Then a second artist, **[Dan Malone](/vault/people/dan-malone)**, arrived on *The Sacred Armour of Antiriad*, and
Palace ran what Stone calls "a sort of **production line**": Brown and Malone generating
games, a team of programmers porting them across machines. Brown began *Barbarian* while
*Antiriad* was still in development.

By the end of 1987 Stone would not describe a house style at all — only authors: "It's
more a case of there being **a Steve Brown game, a Dan Malone game**."

## Barbarian, and the cover

*Barbarian: The Ultimate Warrior* (1987) is a one-on-one sword fight with a decapitation
move. *CRASH* gave it **85%**.

What everyone remembers is the packaging: the Page 3 model **Maria Whittaker** in a fur
bikini beside a barbarian. The received version is that a film company applied film
marketing to a game. Palace's own account, given to *Amstrad User* while the row was still
running, is smaller and stranger than that. It was **the designer's idea, as part of the
game**:

> It was **Steve Brown's idea** originally. When he conceives a game he conceives it in
> total, including the package. He wanted a sword fighting game based round the swords and
> sorcery theme, and he wanted **a cover in the style of the fantasy artist Boris**. So he
> hit on the idea of using Maria Whittaker.

Brown built the set himself and made the costume, which did not survive contact with the
shoot: "the bra and knickers kept falling apart during the photo shoot and he had to get a
**pair of pliers** to fit the metal clasps back together again."

Two things followed that the entry for a marketing triumph usually leaves out.

**It was banned from a high street.** "**Boots** told us that we couldn't have a semi-naked
woman, but that we could have a **semi-naked man**." Palace reissued the inlay.

**It was banned from a country.** West Germany's Bundesprüfstelle für jugendgefährdende
Schriften put *Barbarian* on its list of prohibited publications — the same list that
caught MicroProse's *Silent Service* — which meant no advertising, and sale only through
premises licensed to exclude minors. *Barbarian II* was still being introduced in 1989 as
"the game which got banned in Germany".

Stone's defence, in his own words:

> People who complained that it was a sexist cover seem to forget that it was an idea set
> in a certain genre, which is all about men with huge muscles and women with rather large
> bosoms. **We were slightly upset that people didn't see the funny side of things.**

He also pointed out, correctly and beside the point, that "there's a male as well as a
female on that cover".

Read now it is straightforward eighties tabloid sexism, and it read that way to plenty of
people at the time. *ACE*'s letters pages carried the argument through **February, March
and April 1988** — readers arguing about exploitation, about whether Palace expected women
to buy the game, about whether the objection was prudishness. That is a games magazine
audience conducting a public argument about advertising and women, in 1988, because a
publisher started it.

## Where Palace stood on censorship

Worth recording, because it is the exact counterpart to
[CRL](/vault/companies/crl-group)'s position and was printed on the same pages.

Asked by *ACE* in February 1988 whether the Video Recordings Act applied to his games,
Stone said he "honestly [didn't] feel that *Barbarian* is a case where the Act applies" —
and drew the line somewhere else entirely:

> [If I] believed that *Barbarian* incite[d] violence, I wouldn't release it. But I don't
> believe it does.

Clement Chambers of CRL was arguing that games were legally video works and had to be
submitted. Stone was arguing that the question was whether a game caused harm, and that
his did not. Neither man was talking about the other's test.

Stone held the same line on piracy after *Barbarian II* was cracked, telling *Amiga
Action* in 1989 that hackers "seem to think that software houses are the fat cats who just
sit there absorbing all the money. It's simply not true."

## Outlaw Productions

In late 1987 Palace launched a second label, **Outlaw Productions**, to publish work by
outside teams — Stone's reasoning being that "there are a lot of very good programming
teams around" with four or five years' experience behind them.

Its first release came from **Sensible Software**, fresh from *Wizball* and *Parallax*:
the **Shoot 'Em Up Construction Kit**. A publisher that had spent three years insisting
games needed an artist, a programmer, a musician and a gameplay designer put out a product
that let a fourteen-year-old skip all four.

## Sold, 1991

⚠ Palace did not just stop; it was sold. *Amstrad Action* reported the end in **May 1991**:
Palace Software had been bought by the French company **Leisure Holding**, which also owned
Titus — "which is good news, because the company is coming back to the CPC market."

Head of the Palace group **Chris Craib** on the sale: "Having nurtured the software
division over the years we will be sad to see it go. However we were impressed by Leisure
Holding's organisation, plans and enthusiasm and **they made us a good offer**."

That matters for reading anything published under the Palace name afterwards. From 1991
the label belonged to a French group, so everything described above is a property of the
**1984–1991** company and not of the name.

## Why it mattered

The standard reading — film company brings film marketing to games — is tidy and mostly
wrong. Palace came out of **video retail**, its boss came out of **records**, and its most
notorious image was conceived by **the game's designer** as part of the game, not by a
marketing department.

What Palace genuinely introduced was less photogenic and more durable: **the idea that a
game is made by named specialists**. An artist, a programmer, a musician, and someone whose
job is gameplay — assembled deliberately in 1984, at a moment when the industry's own
retailers were still buying finished cassettes out of carrier bags. By 1987 Palace could no
longer describe its own house style except by naming which of its designers made a given
game, which is how every studio has described itself since.
