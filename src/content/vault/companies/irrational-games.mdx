Irrational Games began in 1997 in Ken Levine's living room in Cambridge, Massachusetts, with three former [Looking Glass](/vault/companies/looking-glass) developers and no start-up money. Ten years later its underwater shooter *[BioShock](/vault/games/bioshock)* went on to sell more than four million copies. In between, it was the kind of studio critics loved and players overlooked. "Everybody loved our games but nobody bought them," its animation director Shawn Robertson told *Edge* in 2013. What it kept throughout was a habit of building games around a place and an idea, and of letting the player work out the story from the world rather than from cutscenes.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1997, by [Ken Levine](/vault/people/ken-levine), Jonathan Chey and Rob Fermier, all from Looking Glass Studios.
- **Based:** Cambridge, then Boston, Massachusetts; later Quincy, just south of Boston. A second studio opened in Canberra, Australia, in 2000.
- **Owner:** independent until January 2006, then Take-Two Interactive, under its 2K Games label.
- **Names:** Irrational Games; 2K Boston and 2K Australia from 2007; Irrational Games again (Boston) from 2010; Ghost Story Games from 2017.
- **Games:** *[System Shock 2](/vault/games/system-shock-2)* (1999, with Looking Glass), *Freedom Force* (2002), *Tribes: Vengeance* (2004), *SWAT 4* (2005), *Freedom Force vs the Third Reich* (2005), *BioShock* (2007), *BioShock Infinite* (2013).
- **Wound down:** February 2014, from about a hundred staff to about fifteen.

## Three people and a borrowed engine

Levine, Chey and Fermier left Looking Glass on good terms. Their first job, the single-player part of a shooter called *FireTeam*, was cancelled after three weeks. "I'm living in my apartment in Cambridge without a dime to my name, we didn't have any start-up money," Levine told *Edge* in 2005. The next week Looking Glass's Paul Neurath rang and offered them the engine his studio was building for *[Thief](/vault/games/thief)*. With a contract artist, the three built a prototype of a science-fiction role-playing game called *Shock* in two months. Electronic Arts, which held the rights to the *[System Shock](/vault/games/system-shock)* name, signed it, and the game became *System Shock 2*.

It came out in August 1999, made, in Levine's words, "in about a year", with "no time to polish and balance". Critics gave it scores of 90 per cent and more; *Edge* gave it eight out of ten. It sold about 50,000 copies in its first six months. "I think we expected the critical reaction to be on par with the commercial reaction, which was tepid," Levine told *Edge* in 2013. Its best-known character, the rogue computer SHODAN, was not Irrational's creation: she came back from Looking Glass's first *System Shock* of 1994. The [System Shock 2](/vault/games/system-shock-2) entry tells that story in full.

## Boston and Canberra

After *System Shock 2* the studio nearly ended. "When we finished Shock 2, both my business partners said: 'This was great, we're exhausted, see you later.' And I was running the company by myself with no staff," Levine told *Edge*. Chey went home to Australia, and when the superhero game *Freedom Force* found funding, he set up a second Irrational studio in Canberra in the spring of 2000 to share the work. "And less than two years later, from what started with a guy in his living room, we had the game shipped," Levine said. The two studios worked across the time difference through a virtual private network, shared source control and weekly meetings run by Chey, whom Levine called "the best manager I've ever known".

Canberra went on to lead *Tribes: Vengeance* (2004), a new game in the *Tribes* series of science-fiction shooters, because, Levine said, "the Looking Glass experience pool doesn't help that much with the multiplayer-oriented games". Its engine, custom-built on an Unreal base, also ran Boston's police tactics game *SWAT 4* (2005). "The engine is a shared codebase — Vengeance and SWAT just branch off at a certain point, which is the great thing about working on multiple titles," Levine explained. A third game, the survival-horror *The Lost* for the PlayStation 2, was never released. By *Edge*'s 2013 count it was eight years after *System Shock 2* before the studio had "a title that was truly commercially successful".

## BioShock and Take-Two

That title grew slowly. By 2002 the only thing Irrational had for its next *Shock*-like game was a system of "protectors, harvesters and drones carrying a valuable resource". Pitches built on it were turned down by Codemasters, Atari and EA before 2K Games, part of Take-Two Interactive, signed the project in 2004. On 9 January 2006 Take-Two announced that it had bought Irrational, "the award-winning development studio behind such titles as System Shock 2 and Freedom Force", with studios in Boston and Canberra, and said that *BioShock* would be the first game from the deal. Levine told *Edge* in 2012 that the money changed what the studio could make: a role-playing game for a team of 30 became a shooter for a core team of 90, with the resources "to really establish an aesthetic as carefully as we did in that game".

In August 2007, days before *BioShock* came out, the two studios took the names 2K Boston and 2K Australia. *BioShock* sold more than four million copies. It also changed who wanted to work there. "It didn't change the old timers so much," Levine told *Edge*, "because we all have the 'fat kid' syndrome — like we're the unpopular kid in school." In January 2010 the Boston studio took back the Irrational Games name, and in 2013 *Edge* found about a hundred people there, in an open-plan office where artists, designers and programmers sat together so that an idea could be tried "in a day". Canberra, still 2K Australia, worked on other 2K games, among them *Borderlands: The Pre-Sequel*, until 2K closed it in April 2015.

*BioShock Infinite*, released in March 2013 after five years of work, moved the series from the undersea city of Rapture to Columbia, a city in the sky. Levine told *Edge* the team had turned down the idea of another *BioShock* until someone asked, "What if it wasn't in Rapture?" The end of its development was not easy; *Edge* reported "a mini staff exodus" late in 2012, which Levine described as "a mix of people leaving and people being asked to leave".

## Winding down, and Ghost Story

On 18 February 2014, less than a year after *Infinite*, Levine announced that he was "winding down Irrational Games as you know it". Most of the staff were laid off, with financial support, time in the studio to prepare portfolios and a recruiting day for other publishers. Levine kept "about fifteen members of the Irrational team" for "a smaller, more entrepreneurial endeavor at Take-Two", to make "narrative-driven games for the core gamer that are highly replayable", sold only as downloads. He handed the *BioShock* series to 2K, noting that it had taken more than half a billion dollars at retail.

The small team kept the Irrational name for three more years. In February 2017 it became Ghost Story Games, with twelve founders from Irrational and Levine as president and creative director. Its first game, *Judas*, was announced in December 2022; the [Ken Levine](/vault/people/ken-levine) entry follows that story.
