Before Mastertronic, as *ACE* later put it, Spectrum software "cost anything from £5 to £10". In the spring of 1984 Mastertronic launched a range of Spectrum and Commodore games at £1.99 each, and *Sinclair User* reported that it took more than 100,000 orders in its first week, from small independent shops as well as W H Smith. Asked how the established publishers felt about it, chairman Frank Herman said: "They hate us." Within a year Mastertronic claimed audited sales of two million games. By the end of 1987 it owned a full-price publisher, ran an arcade-machine business, and was selling Sega's consoles in Britain.

## Breaking the £2 barrier

*Sinclair User* called cut-price games "the latest upset in the industry". That spring Pulsonic had launched Spectrum games at £2.95; Mastertronic went "one better", "breaking the £2 barrier" with titles "at an unprecedented £1.99 each". Other publishers had said prices could not fall without killing profits. Herman disagreed: the secret, he said, lay in "direct mass marketing".

That meant selling where games had not been sold before. *ACE*, looking back in 1990, listed motorway service stations, newsagents, garage forecourts and supermarkets. By May 1985 Mastertronic had also set up Shuttlesoft to distribute budget software; *CRASH* reported that Firebird was its only supplier apart from Mastertronic itself, and that managing director [Martin Alper](/vault/people/martin-alper) had said Firebird was the only other budget company likely to stay in business.

*Computer & Video Games* reported in July 1985 that Mastertronic, "formed in April 1984", claimed to be the brand leader in budget software, and that "in its first 12 months of trading, Mastertronic says it has audited figures of sales for two million games". *The Games Machine* later said the company had been founded on All Fool's Day by three people; Herman, Alper and Alan Sharam are usually named as its founders.

## Where the games came from

Many of the first games came from two teenagers. Richard and David Darling had their own company, Galactic, and *Commodore Computing International* reported in December 1984 that more than half of Mastertronic's first 30 titles were theirs. In August 1984 Galactic and Mastertronic formed a joint company, Al Products; Richard, aged 16, told the magazine he attended "the monthly board meetings and weekly meetings". Their *Chiller* was Mastertronic's biggest early seller, over 100,000 copies on the Commodore 64 and 30,000 on the Spectrum by the summer of 1985. It also caused trouble. A music-rights agency said its tune copied Michael Jackson's "Thriller"; Herman denied that the game had been withdrawn, but agreed "to negotiate a royalty" and "to use different music in future production runs". *CCI* wondered, if *Chiller* had nothing to do with *Thriller*, why a Michael Jackson lookalike had been wandering around the press launch. The Darlings left in 1986 to found [Codemasters](/vault/companies/codemasters).

Most games arrived in the post. In August 1985 *Sinclair User* reported that Mastertronic received around 200 games a month; in May it had looked at 220. It could spend "ten minutes on each", and many came "without instructions or any form of documentation". Two that month were almost ready to publish.

## One company, many labels

By 1986 Mastertronic was selling under several names. Mastertronic Added Dimension (MAD) sold games at £2.99, among them the Magic Knight arcade adventures; *Your Sinclair* counted *Finders Keepers*, *Spellbound*, *Knight Tyme* and *Stormbringer* as the series. Entertainment USA carried American games, Bulldog was another £1.99 label, and Ricochet re-released older full-price games at £1.99, including Ultimate's *[Knight Lore](/vault/games/knight-lore)* and *Alien 8* at Christmas 1987.

The group spread beyond software too. In April 1987 *CRASH* listed "all the Mastertronic labels, Bulldog, the new Mastersounds record label and Mastervision videos, and the Arcadia arcade machine specialists", and wondered where the offices would find room for Melbourne House's staff. Arcadia made coin-op machines built on the Amiga.

## Buying Melbourne House

In early 1987 Mastertronic bought [Melbourne House](/vault/companies/melbourne-house), the full-price publisher of *The Hobbit* and *The Way of the Exploding Fist*. Geoff Heath, who had left Melbourne House for Mastertronic, promised *CRASH* that it would "retain its own identity" and that "it won't be producing budget titles, and Mastertronic won't be going full price". Alfred Milgrom, Melbourne House's founder, gave *Edge* a different account ten years later: "Mastertronic decided that Melbourne House should move directly to 16bit and ditch its 8bit heritage, and as a result we spent a whole year developing products that went nowhere. They never got published."

## Sega and Virgin

In 1987 Mastertronic began distributing Sega's Master System in Britain; Sega's advertisements in September 1987 said "Distributed in the UK by Mastertronic Ltd". In the same autumn Richard Branson's Virgin Group bought a 45% stake. *ACE* reported that Virgin explained the move by Mastertronic's expertise in fields where Virgin had little: budget software, arcade machines and distribution in the United States. *ACE*'s review of 1987 put the two deals together: "Mastertronic swallowed up Melbourne House in February; eight months later, Virgin bought substantial minority interest in Mastertronic." In 1988 it became part of the Virgin group, trading as Virgin Mastertronic.

Virgin Mastertronic was Sega's European console distributor. It told *ACE* in 1989 not to expect a PAL Mega Drive before Easter 1990, and it launched the console in Britain in 1990. Later accounts say that in 1991 Sega bought the business, which became part of Sega Europe, and that the remaining publishing side became Virgin Interactive Entertainment.
