Type `WAIT 6502,1` on a Commodore PET with the newer ROMs and the machine prints the word MICROSOFT! across the screen. The joke was left there by the company that wrote the PET's BASIC, and a 1981 book of PET games let its readers in on it: "The company that wrote the BASIC interpreter for your PET is MICROSOFT." By then the same company's BASIC was the first thing millions of people saw when they switched on a home computer: on the Commodore PET, VIC-20 and 64, the TRS-80, the Apple II Plus, Tandy's Color Computer, the Dragon and, from 1983, every MSX machine. Microsoft later made the PC's operating system, turned Windows into a games platform and became a console maker with the [Xbox](/vault/systems/microsoft-xbox), but its home-computer story is the BASIC.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1975 in Albuquerque, New Mexico, as Micro-Soft, by Bill Gates and Paul Allen.
- **First product:** Altair BASIC, for MITS's Altair 8800 kit, written by Gates, Allen and Monte Davidoff.
- **BASIC in ROM:** Commodore PET, VIC-20 and 64; TRS-80 Level II; Apple II Plus (Applesoft); Tandy Color Computer and Dragon 32; MSX.
- **Operating systems:** MS-DOS (1981, sold to IBM as PC-DOS); Windows (1985).
- **Consoles:** Xbox (2001), Xbox 360 (2005), Xbox One (2013), Xbox Series X and S (2020).
- **Studios bought:** Bungie (2000, independent again 2007), Rare (2002), Lionhead (2006), Mojang (2014), Obsidian and inXile (2018), ZeniMax Media, Bethesda's owner (2021), Activision Blizzard (2023).
- **Headquarters:** Albuquerque, then Bellevue, Washington, from 1979, and Redmond, Washington, from 1986.

## Altair BASIC and Micro-Soft

MITS sold its Altair 8800 in 1975 as a kit for hobbyists, for $395. Gates and Allen wrote a BASIC for it. In November 1975 *BYTE*, then in its third issue, printed a letter signed "William H. Gates, President, Micro-Soft, Albuquerque NM". Gates wrote that he had spent the last three years programming microcomputers, "most recently writing ALTAIR BASIC with Paul Allen and Monte Davidoff". He also explained how MITS priced it: software from MITS cost "about six times as much" if you had not bought the hardware from MITS, because as long as you had, "MITS is willing to just break even on the software".

Most hobbyists did not pay at all. In early 1976 Gates wrote "An Open Letter to Hobbyists", printed in the February 1976 *Computer Notes*, MITS's newsletter, and in the Homebrew Computer Club's newsletter, complaining that people were copying the BASIC instead of buying it. The letter was reprinted widely, and *BYTE* returned to it in September 1976 in an article on software ownership: "it now appears that more copies of Altair's BASIC have been pirated than have been legally sold." Gates was still making the same argument four years later. "Looking at the amount of software we offer, we are the most ripped-off company around," he told *80 Microcomputing* in 1980, and he warned that if there were not "enough honest people out there to buy the stuff", Microsoft would stop selling its cheaper packages.

## A BASIC for every machine

Micro-Soft's BASIC was soon the one other manufacturers came to. [Chuck Peddle](/vault/people/chuck-peddle) visited the company's Albuquerque office to ask for a version for Commodore's new PET. How that deal was paid for is told by Commodore's side. [Jack Tramiel](/vault/people/jack-tramiel) said in 2007 that Gates had asked for $3 a machine: "I told him that the highest price I'm willing to pay is $25,000, and about six weeks later he came and took the $25,000." Kit Spencer, who ran Commodore's British marketing, called it "an operating system that we put on 20 million computers and it didn't cost us anything in reality".

Commodore kept that BASIC for years. Sheldon Leemon's *Mapping the Commodore 64* described the 64's interpreter as "almost identical to the Microsoft BASIC interpreter found on the VIC-20", both "slightly modified versions of PET BASIC 2.0". Microsoft published the source of this BASIC in September 2025, under the MIT licence: version 1.1 of its 6502 BASIC, dated 1978, which assembles for the Commodore PET, the Apple II, the KIM-1 and Ohio Scientific machines. Microsoft's announcement confirms that Commodore licensed the BASIC for a flat fee of $25,000 in 1977, and that version 1.1, with garbage-collection fixes made by Commodore's John Feagans and Gates in 1978, shipped in the PET as BASIC V2, the BASIC that the VIC-20 and 64 also used.

That is why [BASIC V2](/vault/techniques/basic-v2) on the [Commodore 64](/vault/systems/commodore-64) has no commands for its sprites or sound chip: it was a BASIC written for machines that had neither.

The same code went elsewhere:

- **Tandy.** The [TRS-80](/vault/systems/trs-80)'s Level II BASIC was Microsoft's; a 1980 guide to its ROM names it as "Level II ROM BASIC written by Microsoft's Paul Allen and Bill Gates". Microsoft also wrote Color BASIC for the [Tandy Color Computer](/vault/systems/tandy-coco), which the Welsh [Dragon 32](/vault/systems/dragon-32) shared in a close variant.
- **Apple.** Wozniak's own Integer BASIC had no floating-point arithmetic, so Apple licensed Microsoft's. The *Applesoft II Reference Manual* of August 1978 carries two copyright lines, Apple's for 1978 and "Copyright 1977 Microsoft Inc." William Luebbert's guide to the Apple's memory, *What's Where in the Apple*, explained that it was "mostly a transfer to the Apple hardware/firmware environment of Microsoft's MITS BASIC". From 1979 the [Apple II](/vault/systems/apple-ii) Plus had it in ROM.

The shared ancestry helped programmers. A 1983 book on the Commodore 64 explained that Microsoft BASIC had become "closest to a 'standard' version of BASIC for tiny computers", though versions for the TRS-80, the Apple II and the Commodores still differed enough that a program could not always move between them unchanged. *COMPUTE!'s First Book of PET/CBM* noted that because "both APPLESOFT and PET BASIC were written by Microsoft", programs had "exactly the same format in memory".

Microsoft also sold hardware for the Apple II: the Z-80 SoftCard, a plug-in card with a Z80 processor that let the Apple run the CP/M operating system. *Computers That Made Britain* counts it among the best-selling expansions for the Apple II Plus.

## Games for the 8-bit machines

Microsoft published games from early on. *Microsoft Adventure* was its version of the original cave adventure, [*Colossal Cave Adventure*](/vault/games/colossal-cave-adventure), for the TRS-80 and Apple II. A December 1980 advertisement in *SoftSide* sold it "from the people who wrote BASIC for personal computers" as "the granddaddy of the computer Adventure games", at $29.95 on a 32K disk for either machine. *Microsoft Adventure* was also one of the games available for the IBM PC when that machine went on sale in 1981.

*[Microsoft Flight Simulator](/vault/games/microsoft-flight-simulator)* came from outside the company. subLOGIC wrote it and Microsoft published the IBM PC version; a mail-order advertisement in *BYTE* in May 1983 listed "MICROSOFT. Flight Simulator (by Sub-Logic)" at $50.

## MS-DOS and the IBM PC

In 1980 IBM was building a personal computer and wanted to buy its software rather than write it. According to *Computers That Made Britain*, IBM's Jack Sams went to Microsoft first. Microsoft had no operating system of its own, but Seattle Computer Products had one: Tim Paterson's 86-DOS, written for Intel's 8086 and modelled on Digital Research's CP/M. Paul Allen negotiated its purchase for $50,000, and the deal with IBM, signed in November 1980, left Microsoft free to license its DOS to other manufacturers. "The key to the structure of our deal was that IBM had no control over our licensing to other people," Gates said later.

IBM announced the Personal Computer on 12 August 1981. PC-DOS, IBM's name for Microsoft's DOS, cost $40; CP/M-86, when it arrived six months later, cost $240. Most of the companies that copied IBM's hardware licensed MS-DOS for their machines, and that is how the PC, and in time PC games, came to run on Microsoft's software.

Microsoft has since published the source of early DOS. It gave the code of MS-DOS 1.25 and 2.0 to the Computer History Museum in March 2014 and republished it on GitHub under the MIT licence in September 2018. In April 2024, with IBM, it added the source of MS-DOS 4.00 under the same licence.

## MSX

Microsoft's BASIC was also the centre of [MSX](/vault/systems/msx), the home computer standard announced in Tokyo on 16 June 1983 by ASCII, Microsoft and fourteen Japanese manufacturers. Kazuhiko Nishi of ASCII had earlier signed ASCII's deal with Microsoft over its BASIC in the Far East, and *COMPUTE!* called him "the prime force behind MSX development", as president of Microsoft's Far East division. Microsoft, the magazine said, "owns the rights to MSX and licenses the technology to the manufacturers". *Computer and Video Games* described MSX BASIC as Microsoft BASIC 4.5 "with considerable enhancements", and *COMPUTE!* added that MSX-DOS was "written by the author of MS-DOS" and could read disks formatted on an IBM PC.

## Windows and DirectX

Microsoft released Windows 1.0 in November 1985, as a graphical extension that ran on top of MS-DOS. For years PC games stayed on DOS, because Windows did not give programmers the direct control of the hardware that DOS games relied on. Microsoft's answer was the Windows Game SDK, released in September 1995 and soon renamed DirectX: a set of interfaces that gave games on Windows 95 fast access to graphics, sound and input hardware. Microsoft was publishing PC games too. *Edge* wrote in 1998 that its first games, such as the "dire" *Microsoft Soccer*, had cost it credibility, but that "with the likes of Age of Empires", Ensemble Studios' strategy game, "it has begun to address that particular problem". See [Age of Empires](/vault/games/age-of-empires).

## Xbox

In June 2000 Microsoft bought Bungie. *Edge* reported the doubts this raised among Bungie's Macintosh fans, and that Bungie had "moved to work on X-Box software"; Ed Fries was Microsoft's vice-president of games. Bungie's *[Halo](/vault/games/halo)* became the main launch game for the [Xbox](/vault/systems/microsoft-xbox), which Bill Gates had announced at the Game Developers Conference in 2000 and which went on sale in North America on 15 November 2001, built from PC parts with a hard disk and an Ethernet port in every box. In September 2002 Microsoft bought the British studio [Rare](/vault/companies/rare) for $375 million, and its online service, [Xbox Live](/vault/culture/xbox-live), opened in the US that November.

The Xbox 360 followed in November 2005. *Edge* described its British launch in December, where demand "far outstripped supply" and shops restocked within days only to sell out again. Microsoft bought [Lionhead](/vault/companies/lionhead) in 2006. In 2007 [Bungie](/vault/companies/bungie) became independent again, and Microsoft kept *Halo*.

The Xbox One, unveiled in 2013, had a harder start. *Edge*'s report on the reveal summed up the confusion over its online requirement: "Xbox One is always connected, except when it isn't". Microsoft's Phil Harrison said that a console left offline would stop playing games after a set time: "I believe it's 24 hours." Games were to be registered to an Xbox Live account. After a public backlash Microsoft dropped the online check and the limits on lending and reselling games in June 2013, before the console launched that November, and from June 2014 it sold the console without the Kinect camera it had first insisted on.

## The largest buyer of studios

From 2014 Microsoft bought studios on a larger scale. It bought [Mojang](/vault/companies/mojang), the maker of *[Minecraft](/vault/games/minecraft)*, for $2.5 billion in 2014, and launched Xbox Game Pass, a subscription to a library of games, in June 2017. In November 2018, at its X018 event, it announced that it had bought two role-playing studios with roots in [Interplay](/vault/companies/interplay): [Obsidian](/vault/companies/obsidian-entertainment) and inXile. The Xbox Series X and S went on sale on 10 November 2020. In March 2021 it completed the purchase of ZeniMax Media, the owner of [Bethesda](/vault/companies/bethesda), a deal announced at $7.5 billion.

The largest purchase was [Activision](/vault/companies/activision) Blizzard, which Microsoft agreed to buy for $68.7 billion in January 2022 and, after nearly two years of regulatory review, took over in October 2023. Cuts followed: 1,900 games jobs in January 2024, and in May 2024 the closure of four studios, among them Arkane Austin and Tango Gameworks, the Japanese studio behind *Hi-Fi Rush*. Krafton bought Tango that August and brought back about half of its staff.
