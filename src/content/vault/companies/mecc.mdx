Early in the 1970s Minnesota faced a problem that most American states met later: 433 public school districts and 30 public college campuses, each tempted to buy its own computers and write its own plan. The state legislature decided to coordinate them instead, and in 1973 the Minnesota Educational Computing Consortium was born. It began as a public service for Minnesota's schools. Within ten years it was selling educational programs to schools across the United States and in Britain, and one of them, *[The Oregon Trail](/vault/games/oregon-trail)*, became the program it is remembered for.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1973, by a joint powers agreement between Minnesota's four public education systems.
- **Base:** Lauderdale, then St. Paul, Minnesota.
- **Best-known program:** *[The Oregon Trail](/vault/games/oregon-trail)*; others included *Odell Lake*, *Number Munchers* and *Word Munchers*.
- **Became:** the Minnesota Educational Computing Corporation, a state-owned company, by 1986; sold to private investors in 1991.
- **Bought:** by SoftKey in 1995. SoftKey took over [The Learning Company](/vault/companies/the-learning-company) the same year and renamed itself after it, so MECC became part of The Learning Company.

## A consortium for a whole state

*The Atari Connection* explained the arrangement in September 1981. MECC was created "through a joint powers agreement" between the University of Minnesota, the State University System, the Community College System and the State Department of Education. "Minnesota is the only state," the magazine said, "where a single organization... supports computer education from elementary school through the university level."

Much of that support was people rather than programs. MECC's instructional coordinators spent each school year visiting schools, running workshops and classes, organising conferences, publishing newsletters and answering phone calls: in a typical year they visited more than 300 school districts and 30 college campuses. MECC also bought hardware for the state. In 1981 it introduced a statewide purchase contract for Atari computers, mainly the Atari 400, and began converting 75 of its programs to run on them, each package with a teacher's booklet of objectives, lesson plans and worksheets. According to later accounts, it had already chosen the [Apple II](/vault/systems/apple-ii) for Minnesota's schools in 1978.

## Selling to schools everywhere

By the early 1980s MECC's programs were travelling well beyond Minnesota. *COMPUTE!* called the consortium "renowned for its educational software for grades one through eight in a variety of math and science subjects" in April 1983. That August *Hardcore*, the journal of the British Apple Systems User Group, reported that the London borough of Havering had standardised on Apple computers and bought a licence to distribute MECC's software to all its schools, sending the programs down a telephone line. In September 1983 *Family Computing* visited an American elementary school that had joined MECC for access to "hundreds of grade- and subject-specific programs": its kindergarten teachers used a MECC concentration game to teach shapes and colours.

The prices were set for schools. In July 1984 MECC's music theory programs for the Apple II cost $46 to $49 each. In May 1988 *COMPUTE!*'s guide to summer software for children listed *The Oregon Trail* for grades 5 and up, on the Apple II only, at **$55**. In the same table *Math Blaster* cost $49.95 and The Learning Company's *Math Rabbit* $39.95.

## The Oregon Trail

*The Oregon Trail* was older than MECC. Don Rawitsch, Bill Heinemann and Paul Dillenberger wrote it in 1971, in HP Time-Shared BASIC, so that Rawitsch could teach his students about pioneer life in the 19th century. As *Retro Gamer* told the story, it became so popular that MECC hired Rawitsch to develop it further. He stayed: in 1987 *RUN* quoted Don Rawitsch as MECC's administrative vice-president. MECC's graphical Apple II version, *The Oregon Trail*, followed in 1985.

In March 1984 *Commander*, a magazine for Commodore owners, used an "Oregon Trail" program to show what an educational simulation was for: it "confronts the user with the choices that may have presented themselves to the pioneers as they travelled across the country."

## Into the home

By 1986 MECC had become the Minnesota Educational Computing Corporation, and it began to sell to families as well as schools. Commodore sold a "MECC Home Software Library" of six programs for the Commodore 64 and 128, at $19.95 each: "For years, children have used MECC's high-quality software in school," its advertisement said. "Now MECC's most popular packages are designed to continue education in the home!" Commodore also bundled *Odell Lake*, a simulation of a freshwater lake in which the player is a fish trying to survive, with the Commodore 64C.

Reviewing the library in *Commodore Power Play* in 1986, the teacher Jack Emberly singled out *Expeditions*, three historical journeys. One was the Oregon Trail of 1847, "a 2,000-mile journey from Independence, Missouri, to Oregon", in which your possessions and your life depended on your marksmanship and your decisions. "My intermediate students loved role-playing pioneers of days gone by," he wrote. "The biggest thrills were the hunting excursions."

## Sold, and sold again

Later accounts say that Minnesota sold MECC to private investors in 1991 and that its shares were floated in 1994. In October 1995 the software publisher SoftKey, in the middle of a hostile bid for The Learning Company, announced that it had agreed to buy MECC for $370 million in stock. SoftKey won The Learning Company and took its name, and MECC became part of the renamed company. That brought *The Oregon Trail*, *Number Munchers* and *Word Munchers* under the same owner as *Reader Rabbit*. When Mattel bought The Learning Company in 1999, MECC's offices were closed. *The Oregon Trail* outlived the company that made it famous; its later history is told in [its own entry](/vault/games/oregon-trail).
