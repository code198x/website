MECC began as a state-funded consortium bringing computing to Minnesota schools. They became famous for educational games that made learning genuinely engaging—most notably *The Oregon Trail*, which taught history through simulation and mortality. MECC proved that educational software could be both pedagogically sound and genuinely fun.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1973 as Minnesota Educational Computing Consortium.
- **Original purpose:** provide computing resources to Minnesota schools.
- **Key title:** *[The Oregon Trail](/vault/games/oregon-trail)* (1971, widespread 1985).
- **Other hits:** *Number Munchers*, *Word Munchers*, *Odell Lake*.
- **Privatised:** 1984; acquired by Softkey (1995), then The Learning Company.

## The Oregon Trail

Gaming's most famous educational title:
- **Origins:** created by student teachers Don Rawitsch, Bill Heinemann, and Paul Dillenberger.
- **Premise:** simulate 19th-century pioneer journey.
- **Learning:** resource management, historical context, consequences.
- **Cultural impact:** "You have died of dysentery" became gaming meme.

## What it cost, and who it was for

*COMPUTE!*'s educational software buyer's guide of May 1988 lists ***The Oregon Trail*** with
the details that matter to a school buying it:

| Title | Ages / grades | Platforms | Price | Publisher |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *The Oregon Trail* | Grades 5 & up | Apple II | **$55.00** | **MECC** |

**Fifty-five dollars, Apple II only, in 1988.** Set that beside the commercial games in the
same guide — *Reader Rabbit* at $39.95, *Math Blaster* at $49.95 — and against British
full-price cassettes at £9.95. Educational software was priced for institutional purchasing,
not for parents, and MECC's single-platform listing reflects a customer that had standardised
on one machine.

That is the commercial fact underneath the nostalgia. *The Oregon Trail* is remembered as a
game millions of children played; almost none of them or their families bought it.

## What the period described

*COMPUTE!*'s buyer's guide summarises the game without any of the mythology that has since
attached to it:

> *Oregon Trail* simulates a **2000-mile trip over the Oregon Trail in 1847**. The player has
> to buy supplies and deal with strangers, bad weather, and wild animals.

And *Commander*, in March 1984, describing what the simulation was for: the program
"**confronts the user with the choices that may have presented themselves to the pioneers**
as they travelled across the country."

⚠ Note the date the period sources give for the *setting*: **1847**. The software's own
dates are 1971, and widespread from 1985 — a different thing, and easily confused with the
year the game is set in.

## Educational philosophy

What MECC understood:
- **Engagement first:** games needed to be genuinely fun.
- **Failure as teacher:** dying taught decision-making.
- **Simulation:** experience history rather than memorise it.
- **Accessibility:** software designed for classroom deployment.
