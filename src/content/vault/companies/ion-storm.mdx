"Judging by the line-up of talent," *Edge* wrote in February 1997, "Ion Storm is all set to become the next id." [John Romero](/vault/people/john-romero), fresh from *[Doom](/vault/games/doom)* and *[Quake](/vault/games/quake)*, founded it in Dallas in 1996 with his old [id Software](/vault/companies/id-software) colleague Tom Hall. It had a ten-year publishing deal with [Eidos](/vault/companies/eidos) and offices at the top of a downtown skyscraper. Four years later its first big game, *Daikatana*, was a byword for hype and delay. Yet the same company, through a second studio in Austin, also made *[Deus Ex](/vault/games/deus-ex)*, which *Edge* called "the anti-Daikatana" and BAFTA named the PC game of 2000.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** Dallas, Texas, 1996, by John Romero and Tom Hall, with Todd Porter, Mike Wilson, Jerry O'Flaherty and Bob Wright among the founding team.
- **Publisher and owner:** Eidos Interactive, whose ten-year publishing deal *Edge* reported in February 1997, and which took majority control in 1999.
- **Studios:** Dallas (1996–2001) and Austin, run by [Warren Spector](/vault/people/warren-spector) (1997–2005).
- **Dallas games:** *Dominion: Storm Over Gift 3* (1998), *Daikatana* (2000), *Anachronox* (2001).
- **Austin games:** *[Deus Ex](/vault/games/deus-ex)* (2000), *Deus Ex: Invisible War* (2003), *Thief: Deadly Shadows* (2004).
- **Closed:** Eidos closed the Dallas studio in 2001 and the Austin studio in February 2005.

## A company of stars

Romero left id in August 1996 and started talking to Tom Hall about a new company months before he went. In January 1997 *Computer Gaming World* described "ION Storm" as "John Romero and Tom Hall's new venture", with strategy, action and role-playing games planned. Todd Porter, who had recently left the developer 7th Level, had joined them. The next month *Edge* reported Ion Storm's ten-year publishing deal with Eidos Interactive.

The founders presented themselves as a band. Ion Storm's first print advertisement, two pages in *Computer Gaming World* in December 1997, introduced six "misfit, zealot freaks" with photographs: Romero, Hall, Porter, the former id marketing man Mike Wilson, the artist Jerry O'Flaherty and Bob Wright. It promised that rules "must be broken" and the status quo "spanked". Four years later the magazine's Jeff Green looked back at the same advertisement and its motto: "Design is law."

The offices matched the pitch. When the German magazine *PC Player* visited in 1998 it found Ion Storm "ganz nobel weit oben, knapp unterm Dach" (very grandly, high up just under the roof) of the Chase Tower, a downtown Dallas skyscraper. Even the air-conditioning vents and partitions were designer pieces, and the floor held a motion-capture studio, a "Deathmatch Area" for testing multiplayer games and a screening room with a THX sound system.

## Dallas: three games in four years

Ion Storm's first release was not its own design. *Dominion: Storm Over Gift 3*, a real-time strategy game, had been started at 7th Level; Ion Storm bought it, and Porter, its lead designer, told *PC Player* that when the code arrived from 7th Level the game would not even install. It came out in 1998, published by Eidos at $59.95. *Computer Gaming World*'s review was headlined "Ion Storm's First Launch Lands With a Resounding Thud", and found it derivative of *Red Alert*, *Total Annihilation* and *StarCraft*.

*Daikatana*, Romero's own game, was a first-person shooter that travelled through four periods of history with two computer-controlled companions. Romero had promised it for Christmas 1997; it came out in mid-2000, rebuilt on the *Quake II* engine. Along the way a 1997 advertisement became notorious. Its slogan boasted, in crude terms, that Romero was about to humiliate the player. *APC* later called it the kind of marketing "that would make even the most confrontational of ad agencies cringe". When the game's demo disappointed players in 2000, *Computer Gaming World* reported that they were "referring back to the infamous" advertisement, and Romero later apologised for it. The reviews were poor; *PC Zone* opened with "Oh dear. Oh dear, oh dear, oh dear". The [John Romero](/vault/people/john-romero) entry tells the story in full.

Behind the delays, the company was losing people. Several developers who had worked on *Daikatana* left to found Third Law Interactive, which made *KISS Psycho Circus*. In the summer of 1999 *PC Zone* and *Computer Gaming World* both reported that Porter and O'Flaherty had gone too, apparently removed by Romero. Eidos's publishing director John Kavanagh, who had spent much of 1998 in Dallas overseeing Ion Storm, told *PC Zone* that the press should "focus on products, rather than the ridiculous rumours of boardroom antics". By 2001 an Eidos spokesman was describing Eidos as "the company that owns ION Storm".

Tom Hall's *Anachronox* was the last Dallas game. It was a role-playing game in the style of *Final Fantasy*, with turn-based battles and a washed-up detective hero, Sly Boots, built on a heavily reworked *Quake II* engine. It came out in the summer of 2001, and it divided the critics. *Edge* gave it four out of ten, and thought it had more in common with *Daikatana* than with *Deus Ex*. *Computer Gaming World* praised its characters and dialogue, and Jeff Green called it "easily the best console-style RPG ever made for the PC". By then, he wrote, Eidos had shut the Dallas studio down: the game was "released simultaneously with the company's closure". Romero and Hall left to found Monkeystone Games.

## Austin: Spector's studio

In the summer of 1997 Warren Spector, who had produced *[Ultima Underworld](/vault/games/ultima)* and *[System Shock](/vault/games/system-shock)*, joined Ion Storm and set up a branch in Austin. Its first game, *Deus Ex* (2000), mixed shooting, role-playing, stealth and hacking in a near-future conspiracy, and let the player solve most problems in several ways. In November 2000 BAFTA named it the PC game of the year. The [Deus Ex](/vault/games/deus-ex) entry covers it in detail.

Austin's later games were received less warmly. *Deus Ex: Invisible War* came out at the end of 2003 for PC and Xbox, and *Edge* gave it seven out of ten. The studio also took over *Thief 3*, which [Looking Glass](/vault/companies/looking-glass) had begun before it closed in 2000. Randy Smith, who had co-led the design at Looking Glass, became its project director at Ion Storm, and it was released in 2004 as *Thief: Deadly Shadows* for PC and Xbox. *Edge* gave it seven; *Computer Gaming World* liked its lighting and its new third-person view but thought it "rushed out the castle door". The [Thief](/vault/games/thief) entry tells the series' story.

Spector left Ion Storm late in 2004; Eidos said he would stay on as a consultant. In February 2005 Eidos closed the Austin studio. The German magazine *PC Games* put the closure down to disappointing sales of *Invisible War* and *Deadly Shadows*, and noted that the lead designers Harvey Smith and Randy Smith had already left in 2004.

## What came after

Ion Storm's games outlived it. Eidos, bought by Square Enix in 2009, revived both of the Austin studio's series at Eidos Montreal: *Deus Ex: Human Revolution* in 2011 and a new *Thief* in 2014. Harvey Smith, lead designer of *Deus Ex*, went on to make *[Dishonored](/vault/games/dishonored)* (2012) with [Arkane Studios](/vault/companies/arkane-studios), a stealth game that *Edge* traced to his experience with "both Deus Ex and Thief".
