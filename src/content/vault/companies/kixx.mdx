## Overview

Kixx was [US Gold](/vault/companies/us-gold)'s **budget label**, and it did not make games. It **re-released** them — taking titles whose full-price life was over and putting them back on shelves at a few pounds, in new packaging, often years later.

That sounds like a footnote. It was a substantial part of how the British software market worked.

## Fast facts

- **Parent:** US Gold.
- **Business:** budget re-releases of catalogue titles, plus compilations.
- **Press evidence in this library:** reviewed in *ZZAP!64* and *Amiga Power* from **1991 to 1995**, at **£3.99–£12.99** — the price range that defines the category. ⚠ This library's run begins in 1991; the company was trading from 1988, so its first 3 years are not represented here and nothing in this line should be read as a start date.
- **Sibling labels:** part of the same budget wave as Kixx XL, Hit Squad, GBH, Silverbird and Players.
- **Typical re-releases:** *Flimbo's Quest*, *Super Monaco GP* — full-price hits of two or three years earlier.


## Launch, as reported at the time
*CRASH* announced it in September 1988, and the announcement fixes both the date
and the price the entry previously had wrong:

> …a selective budget venture. Known as Kixx (not sure why) the new label is
> [U.S. Gold's]. Games retail at **£2.99** and should be ready to burn a hole in
> your pocket some time in the middle of **August**.

So: August 1988, at £2.99, and even the magazine could not work out the name.
Prices drifted upward later, which is where the £3.99 commonly quoted for Kixx
comes from — but the label did not start there.

The word doing the work in that announcement is **selective**. Kixx was not
dumping a back catalogue indiscriminately; it was choosing which of
[U.S. Gold](/vault/companies/us-gold)'s titles were worth a second release,
which is what separates a re-release imprint from a clearance sale.

## The economics of the second release

A budget label solves a problem specific to this industry: a game costs a great deal to make and almost nothing to copy, so a title that has stopped selling at £9.95 still has value if you can reach people who would never have paid £9.95.

The re-release model exploited that directly. Development was already paid for. Packaging got cheaper — a smaller box, a thinner manual, sometimes just a cassette in a case. And the audience was enormous: **children with pocket money**, for whom a £3.99 game was an achievable weekly purchase and a £9.95 one was a birthday.

The cultural effect was larger than the margins suggest. A generation's game collection was substantially budget re-releases, which means the games people remember playing are frequently **not the games that were reviewed that month** — they are the hits of two or three years earlier, arriving late and cheap.

## Why it mattered

Budget labels are the reason British 8-bit software stayed a mass-market product for as long as it did. They kept machines alive commercially past the point where full-price publishers had moved to the next platform, and they made a back catalogue continuously available at a time when nothing resembling digital distribution existed.

Kixx is a representative example, not an exceptional one — which is exactly why it is worth an entry. It shows the machinery.
