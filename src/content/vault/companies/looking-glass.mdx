"Who thought in the '90s that a low-profile PC developer on the unfashionable US east coast would one day seem the prototype for 21st-century game development?" *Edge* asked in 2004, four years after Looking Glass had closed. In a little over a decade the studio made *Ultima Underworld*, which let players walk, jump and swim through a solid 3D dungeon before *Wolfenstein 3D* came out, the space-station game *[System Shock](/vault/games/system-shock)*, and *[Thief](/vault/games/thief)*, a first-person game in which the best plan was usually not to be seen. Its designers built worlds that ran on consistent rules and let players decide how to use them, the approach later called the [immersive sim](/vault/genres/immersive-sim). Its alumni carried that approach to [Ion Storm](/vault/companies/ion-storm), [Irrational Games](/vault/companies/irrational-games) and beyond.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1990, by Paul Neurath, as Blue Sky Productions, in New Hampshire.
- **Renamed:** LookingGlass Technologies after a merger with Ned Lerner's company in 1992; trading as Looking Glass Studios by 1998.
- **Based:** Cambridge, Massachusetts.
- **Best-known games:** *Ultima Underworld* (1992), *Ultima Underworld II* (1993), *System Shock* (1994), *Flight Unlimited* (1995), *Terra Nova: Strike Force Centauri* (1996), *Thief: The Dark Project* (1998), *Thief II: The Metal Age* (2000).
- **Publishers:** [Origin Systems](/vault/companies/origin-systems) and [Electronic Arts](/vault/companies/electronic-arts), its own label from 1995, and [Eidos](/vault/companies/eidos).
- **Closed:** May 2000.

## Blue Sky

Paul Neurath had worked for Richard Garriott's [Origin Systems](/vault/companies/origin-systems); his last game there was *Space Rogue*, which mixed space flight with role-playing. In 1990 he started his own studio, Blue Sky Productions, first in New Hampshire, to make a role-playing game in a new way. *Edge* told the story in 2004. The artist Doug Wike joined, then a couple of programmers, and then Neurath hired young graduates of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, among them Dan Schmidt and Doug Church. Church's housemates, bandmates and frisbee team became the first testers. "Paul set an early tone that was about just finding ways to make interesting games," Church said.

A programmer called Chris Green gave the game an early lead with texture mapping, which painted detailed images across the walls and floors of a 3D world instead of flat colours. Origin offered Neurath the *[Ultima](/vault/games/ultima)* licence, and the game became *Ultima Underworld: The Stygian Abyss*. It was not easy. Origin went through two producers on the project in little over a year; the third, whom Neurath had asked for, was [Warren Spector](/vault/people/warren-spector). To finish the game, Spector and Neurath took the team out of the New Hampshire offices and into one room in Cambridge, Massachusetts, where most of them lived. "The entire team squeezed into one room, dragging beach chairs down there and propping their keyboards in their laps," Spector remembered. "We had to stuff towels under the doors to keep the wind from whistling through the place."

*Ultima Underworld* came out in the spring of 1992. *ACE*'s review, which named Blue Sky Productions as the developer, called it "the closest thing I've seen to Virtual Reality … on a home computer". It sold half a million copies, *Edge* reported.

## LookingGlass Technologies

The same year Blue Sky merged with the company of Neurath's old colleague Ned Lerner, whose Lerner Research had made the driving game *Car and Driver* for Electronic Arts, and took the name LookingGlass Technologies. The new company was incorporated in June 1992, and by the end of the year Origin's advertisements for *Ultima Underworld II: Labyrinth of Worlds* carried the line "A LookingGlass Technologies Design". In March 1993 the studio advertised in *Computer Gaming World* for game developers, describing itself as "a computer game design house that creates original role-playing games and simulations" whose titles included *Ultima Underworld* and *Car and Driver*.

*System Shock* (1994), again for Origin, moved the first-person role-playing game to a space station run by a hostile computer, SHODAN. In 1995 Looking Glass began publishing under its own label with *Flight Unlimited*, a flight simulator whose terrain came from real satellite images. *Edge* called it "one of those rare games that made you drop your coffee when you first saw it", and wrote that its physics model was so realistic that real aircraft's handling flaws "emerged quite naturally". *Terra Nova: Strike Force Centauri* (1996), a squad-based tactical game across three planets, was "critically adored but was ignored by the Quake-playing masses". There was also work for hire: *Edge* noted that the Mega Drive version of *John Madden Football '93* was "ironically one of Looking Glass Studios' best-selling games".

## Life at the studio

The staff were young, many straight from MIT, and worked in small teams in an open-plan office with, *Edge* reported, "virtually no bureaucracy". Seamus Blackley, who later became known as the prime mover behind Microsoft's Xbox, remembered a building that switched off its air conditioning and lifts at night, so in summer the team set up fans blowing in from the fire exits, "an amateur HVAC system": "Twenty young developers awake for a week at a time in a dark office in a nameless corporate park sweating into their keyboards, creating the future." Eric Brosius, who wrote music for *Terra Nova* before joining as a sound designer and later became audio director, found the place "teeming with fresh-out-of-MIT intellectuals" who "debated topics like 'simulation vs emulation' as if the honour of the Klingon race depended upon it". Spector put the difference plainly: "Origin was like going to college; Looking Glass was like grad school."

## Thief

The game that became *[Thief: The Dark Project](/vault/games/thief)* started in the spring of 1996 as *Dark Camelot*, an Arthurian story turned upside down, from design documents by [Ken Levine](/vault/people/ken-levine). Its development was hard. *Retro Gamer* counted two near cancellations and the departure of its producer, project director, lead programmer, renderer programmer and lead artist before release. The designer Randy Smith arrived just as the company looked about to close: he remembered people "walking into the office to take back the [rented] plants which the studio was no longer able to pay for". The rescue came from Intermetrics, a Massachusetts software company that bought the studio; by the end of 1998 *Thief*'s copyright line read "Intermetrics Entertainment Software, LLC dba Looking Glass Studios".

*Thief* came out in December 1998, published by Eidos, and made hiding the heart of the game. Guards heard footsteps and reacted to what they saw, and a light gem on screen showed how visible the player was. Eidos's *Thief Gold* followed in 1999, and *System Shock 2* (1999), built on the *Thief* engine and made with Irrational Games, a studio founded by former Looking Glass staff.

## The end

*Thief II: The Metal Age* came out early in 2000. In May Looking Glass closed. *PC Zone* reported that it had shut "due in part to poor financial results", that *System Shock 2* had "underachieved in terms of sales", and that a deal for Eidos to buy into the studio seemed to have fallen through. Randy Smith wrote that there was "a low chance that *Thief 3* will ever see the light of day". Electronic Arts cancelled *Jane's Attack Squadron*, a flight game Looking Glass had been making for it. *Edge* added other causes: "a couple of unlucky breaks, cashflow issues" and cancelled projects, including a *Star Trek: Voyager* game and *Deep Cover*, signed with Microsoft.

Spector, who had worked there full-time for about a year, rejected talk of villains. "Looking Glass died a death of a thousand cuts. There was no conspiracy, no bad guy," he told *Edge*. "There were some bad business decisions, some bad development decisions, a little too much emphasis on innovation and intelligence over commercial considerations and, ultimately, the natural dissolution of the Great Group."

## After Looking Glass

The studio's games and people carried on. At Ion Storm's Austin studio Spector released *[Deus Ex](/vault/games/deus-ex)* a month later, and Randy Smith and Terri Brosius took *Thief 3* there, where it came out in 2004 as *Thief: Deadly Shadows*. Irrational Games went on to *[BioShock](/vault/games/bioshock)* (2007). Neurath founded Floodgate Entertainment in 2000 and, later, OtherSide Entertainment, which released *Underworld Ascendant*, a successor to *Ultima Underworld*, in 2018. "Plenty are making tons of money exploiting ideas first conceived at Looking Glass," Spector told *Edge*. "It's almost like Looking Glass was the Xerox PARC of the game industry."
