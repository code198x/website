Hitmaker was the name Sega gave its AM3 arcade team in 2000, and the team's best-known game came out just before the change. *Crazy Taxi* (1999) gave players a cab, a hilly city modelled on San Francisco and a clock, and asked for each fare to be delivered as fast as possible, with punk rock on the soundtrack. In its first test version, the producer Hisao Oguchi told *Edge*, the taxi "simply picked up passengers", and "it was more 'cool taxi' than 'crazy taxi'. After the location test, we concentrated more on the 'crazy' aspect." *Crazy Taxi* and *Virtua Tennis*, both on Sega's [NAOMI](/vault/systems/sega-naomi) arcade board, came home to the [Dreamcast](/vault/systems/sega-dreamcast), and Oguchi went on to run Sega itself.

## Fast facts

- **Origins:** Sega's third amusement-machine research and development department, AM3 (AM R&D#3), whose arcade games included *Jurassic Park*, *Star Wars Arcade* and *[Sega Rally Championship](/vault/games/sega-rally)* (1995).
- **As Hitmaker:** a separate Sega subsidiary from 2000 to 2004, led by Hisao Oguchi.
- **Key people:** Hisao Oguchi (producer of *Crazy Taxi*, later president of Hitmaker and of Sega); Kenji Kanno (director of *Crazy Taxi* and producer of its sequels); Mie Kumagai (producer of *Virtua Tennis* for the Dreamcast).
- **Games:** *[Crazy Taxi](/vault/games/crazy-taxi)* (1999, as AM3), *Virtua Tennis* (arcade; Dreamcast 2000), *Virtua Athlete 2K* (2000), *Crazy Taxi 2* (2001), *Confidential Mission* (2001), *Virtua Tennis 2* (2001), *Crazy Taxi 3: High Roller* (2002), *Virtual-On Marz* (2003), and the arcade games *Air Trix*, *Derby Owners Club*, *The Maze of the Kings* and *World Club Champion Football*.
- **The end:** folded back into Sega in 2004.

## Crazy Taxi

*Edge* previewed *Crazy Taxi* in April 1999 as an AM3 arcade game for NAOMI. Kanno, its director, said the team had been influenced by car chases in old American television series. He compared it with AM1's 1997 free-roaming motorbike game *Harley-Davidson & L.A. Riders*, where the ride was "relaxing and enjoyable": "With this game we wanted something different — we wanted panic." New York was ruled out as the setting: "New York is very dark," said Oguchi. The music was punk: "We use Offspring and Bad Religion for the background music in the game," said Kanno, as if it had been a group decision. "It is his personal taste," a colleague laughed.

The game followed on the Dreamcast at the end of 1999, and on the PlayStation 2, published by Acclaim, in 2001. *Crazy Taxi 2* moved to New York and added a jump button; *Edge* found that Hitmaker had "adhered to the 'if it ain't broke, don't fix it' adage". *Crazy Taxi 3: High Roller* followed on Microsoft's Xbox in 2002.

## Virtua Tennis

*Virtua Tennis* began in the arcades on NAOMI. The Dreamcast version added the doubles matches players had asked for, and a one-player World Circuit of training games and tournaments; "After all, the videogame is born from tennis — Pong," its producer, Mie Kumagai of AM R&D#3, told *Edge*. *Edge* gave it eight out of ten in September 2000, finding it "the genre's most accomplished example to date", spoiled only by "almost unbeatable players in later stages" and matches of a single set. *Dreamcast Magazine* called *Virtua Tennis 2* (2001) "the best sports game the Dreamcast has ever seen".

## After the Dreamcast

Sega announced the end of Dreamcast production in January 2001, and Hitmaker turned to other formats and back to the arcade. Its games appeared on the PlayStation 2 (*Virtual-On Marz*) and the Xbox (*Crazy Taxi 3*), and its arcade cabinets got stranger: *Crack'n DJ*, a DJ mixing game, and *Air Trix*, which *Edge* described as the freestyle play of *Tony Hawk's* with the skateboard controller of Sega's earlier *Top Skater*. Asked by *Edge* in 2001 where games were going, Oguchi predicted they would be "played by a much bigger audience". In *World Club Champion Football*, an arcade football game, players moved real cards across the cabinet's table to position their team; by the end of 2004 *Edge* was noting that Sega's *Sangokushi Taisen* used the same system.

In 2003 Sega restructured; *Edge* thought the collapse of merger talks with Sammy had prompted it. The magazine reported that the company's president, Hideki Sato, would step down to become chairman, "with Hitmaker president Hisao Oguchi stepping up as his replacement". The next year Sega folded its studios back into the parent company, [AM2](/vault/companies/sega-am2) among them, and Hitmaker's name went with them. By December 2004 *Edge* was describing Sega's studios by their old department names again: *Sangokushi Taisen* came from "the former Wow studio (now re-established as AM1)".
