Square gambled on a single game more than once. The first time, in 1987, its designer Hironobu Sakaguchi treated a role-playing game called *[Final Fantasy](/vault/games/final-fantasy)* as his last try at a hit, and it made the company. The second time, Square took the series away from Nintendo to Sony's new PlayStation, and *[Final Fantasy VII](/vault/games/final-fantasy-vii)* (1997) carried the Japanese role-playing game round the world. By then *Edge* reckoned Square was spending around $80,000 on computers and software for each of its programmers and up to $230,000 for each artist. The third gamble, a computer-animated Hollywood film, came close to sinking it. Two years later Square merged with Enix, the publisher of its great rival *[Dragon Quest](/vault/games/dragon-quest)*, and the company it became, Square Enix, still makes Final Fantasy.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** September 1986, as Square Co., Ltd., in Tokyo. It had started in 1983 as the software division of another company, Den-Yu-Sha.
- **Breakthrough:** *[Final Fantasy](/vault/games/final-fantasy)* (Famicom, December 1987).
- **Other series:** SaGa (sold in the West as *The Final Fantasy Legend*), Mana, *[Chrono Trigger](/vault/games/chrono-trigger)*, *[Final Fantasy Tactics](/vault/games/final-fantasy-tactics)*, Kingdom Hearts.
- **Key people:** [Hironobu Sakaguchi](/vault/people/hironobu-sakaguchi) (designer, director and producer), [Nobuo Uematsu](/vault/people/nobuo-uematsu) (composer), Yoshitaka Amano and Tetsuya Nomura (character designers), Yoichi Wada (president at the merger).
- **Ended:** merged with [Enix](/vault/companies/enix) on 1 April 2003 to form Square Enix.

## From Den-Yu-Sha to Final Fantasy

Square began inside a company that had nothing to do with games. Den-Yu-Sha was an electric power company run by Kuniichi Miyamoto. His son Masafumi Miyamoto led its computer game division, set up in 1983, and in September 1986 it became an independent company, Square. Its first finished game was a text adventure, *The Death Trap* (1984), for NEC's PC-8801 computer. Sakaguchi, whom *Edge* later called "a founder member of Square", had made games for it that struggled to sell.

*Final Fantasy* was meant to change that or end his career. "Everyone's heard the story," *Retro Gamer* wrote in 2012: the game was Sakaguchi's "then-final attempt to produce a hit game", and if it failed "he'd quit the games industry and try something else". It borrowed its turn-based battles from Enix's *Dragon Quest*, which had come out the year before, and added a large open world map, a choice of character classes, and vehicles, ending with an airship that could fly anywhere. It came out in Japan on 18 December 1987 and became, in *Retro Gamer*'s words, "an instant hit, mainly in Japan", and the start of "Square's resuscitation as a company". (The popular story that Square was on the edge of bankruptcy goes further than these sources do.)

Square kept changing the formula. Each game had a new world and new characters. *Final Fantasy II* (1988) gave its heroes names and personalities and introduced the Chocobo, a big yellow bird, and a recurring character called Cid. *Final Fantasy III* (1990) let characters switch jobs during play. For the Game Boy, Square made the SaGa role-playing games, which it renamed *The Final Fantasy Legend* in the West to catch attention there, and *Final Fantasy Adventure* (1991), the first game of what became the Mana series. In March 1989 it set up an American subsidiary, Square Soft.

## The Super Famicom years

On the Super Famicom, Square made the series the reference point for Japanese role-playing games. *Final Fantasy IV* (1991) brought in the Active Time Battle system, in which each character's turn comes round in real time and the enemy does not wait for the player to choose. It was sold in America as *Final Fantasy II*, because it was only the second of the series to be released there. *Final Fantasy V* (1992) made the job system far more flexible; it was the last game in the series Sakaguchi directed before he moved up to producer. *Final Fantasy VI* (1994) had 14 playable characters, two of them secret, each with a back story, and reached America as *Final Fantasy III*. *Final Fantasy: Mystic Quest* (1993), a lighter game with more action and no numbered hit-point meter, was the first Final Fantasy released in Britain, although there the Final Fantasy name was dropped and it was sold as *Mystic Quest Legend*.

The Mana series moved to the Super Famicom too. *Secret of Mana*, reviewed on import by *Super Play* in January 1994, could be played by up to three people at the same time. In 1995 Square's team worked with *Dragon Quest*'s designer Yuji Horii and artist Akira Toriyama on *[Chrono Trigger](/vault/games/chrono-trigger)*; the [JRPG](/vault/genres/jrpg) entry tells that story.

How Square stood against Enix depends on when you ask. In 1996 *Edge* wrote that Square had "wrested the domination of the console RPG market from long-term rival Enix". Looking back in 2010, the same magazine judged that Enix had been "easily winning — on home turf, at least" until the two merged.

## Leaving Nintendo

By 1996 Square had committed *Final Fantasy VII* to Sony's PlayStation instead of Nintendo's new console. Sakaguchi explained it to *Edge* as a matter of space: "We need about 1,500Mb for FFVII, and we're already squeezing it into two disks, maybe even three," far more than the Nintendo 64's cartridges or its planned disk drive could hold. *Retro Gamer* later pointed out the irony: Nintendo had itself backed out of a joint CD-ROM project for the Super Famicom with Sony earlier in the decade.

*Edge*'s visit that year shows how much money Square now had. It was developing ten PlayStation games at once. It had hired former Namco and Sega staff to make its first fighting game, *Tobal No.1*, and packed a playable demo of *Final Fantasy VII* in with it, so that, *Edge* suggested, some Japanese players bought the fighting game just for the demo. More than 100 designers at its office in Meguro, Tokyo, were working on *Final Fantasy VII* alone, and Square told *Edge* it was probably number one in Japan for money spent on Silicon Graphics workstations. The magazine also passed on a claim "widely reported" at the time, that Square counted any game selling under a million copies in Japan as a flop.

*Final Fantasy VII* changed the look of the series. Its characters were 3D models walking over pre-rendered backgrounds, and it was the first game in which Tetsuya Nomura designed all the characters, replacing Yoshitaka Amano, who had drawn the characters for the earlier games; Amano still drew its logo. Nobuo Uematsu wrote the music. By late 2002 the game had sold 9.2 million copies, more than half of them outside Japan. In 1998 Square set up a joint venture with [Electronic Arts](/vault/companies/electronic-arts), Square Electronic Arts, which published its PlayStation games in North America, among them *[Final Fantasy Tactics](/vault/games/final-fantasy-tactics)* (1997) and *[Xenogears](/vault/games/xenogears)*.

## The film

The third gamble was a film. Square opened a studio in Hawaii, and [Henk Rogers](/vault/people/henk-rogers), who had a house in Hawaii, helped set it up as what *Edge* called a "Hawaiian honeypot, designed to lure SGI artists from colder climes". There Square Pictures made *Final Fantasy: The Spirits Within*, a fully computer-animated science fiction film directed by Sakaguchi and released by Columbia Pictures in July 2001. Its animators modelled 60,000 individual hairs on the head of its heroine, Aki Ross. The cost was rumoured at $100 million.

The film did badly in American cinemas, and the company's finances were already under strain. Three of Square's top executives had stepped down in February 2001 ahead of its first reported financial loss. Nintendo's president Hiroshi Yamauchi would not let Square develop for the Game Boy Advance, and Square, Enix and Namco had each taken five per cent holdings in the others. Later in 2001 Sony bought an 18.6 per cent stake in Square. *Edge* later summed it up: *Final Fantasy* had made Square a powerhouse, and the "catastrophic failure" of *The Spirits Within* "nearly crushed it again".

The games still sold. *Final Fantasy X* (2001), the first on the PlayStation 2, had sold 5.4 million copies by the end of 2002, and in 2002 Square released *Final Fantasy XI*, an online role-playing game, in Japan. *Kingdom Hearts* (2002), which mixed Final Fantasy characters with Disney's, began, according to *Retro Gamer*, with a conversation in a lift between Disney and Square executives, whose Japanese offices were in the same building.

## The merger with Enix

On 26 November 2002 Square and Enix announced that they would merge. The new company, Square Enix, started on 1 April 2003. Legally, Square's assets and liabilities were absorbed by Enix, but the new company was headed by Yoichi Wada, Square's president. The two companies presented the deal as a fit between Square's success in America and Europe and Enix's in Japan, and backed it with a table of their best sellers, headed by *Final Fantasy VII* with 9.2 million copies.

## Square Enix

Some of the people who had made Square left. Uematsu left Square Enix in 2004 to start his own studio. Sakaguchi set up his own company, Mistwalker, which by 2006 was making the role-playing games *Blue Dragon* and *Lost Odyssey* for Microsoft's Xbox 360, with Uematsu among its freelance collaborators.

Square Enix grew by buying other companies. It took control of [Taito](/vault/companies/taito), the maker of *[Space Invaders](/vault/games/space-invaders)*, in September 2005 and owned all of it by March 2006. In October 2008 it became a holding company, Square Enix Holdings. In April 2009 it bought the British publisher [Eidos](/vault/companies/eidos), gaining *Tomb Raider*, *Hitman* and Eidos's studios. It also returned to Nintendo's machines: *Final Fantasy: Crystal Chronicles* (2003) was a GameCube game.

The series itself had one of its worst moments online. *Final Fantasy XIV* launched in 2010 to what *Edge* called "a critical drubbing"; Square Enix shut it down, gave it a new director, Naoki Yoshida, and relaunched it in 2013 as *A Realm Reborn*. In 2022 Square Enix sold Crystal Dynamics, Eidos-Montréal and other Western studios to Embracer Group for $300 million, saying that the sale would let it invest in fields "including blockchain, AI, and the cloud". Final Fantasy, *Dragon Quest* and Kingdom Hearts stayed with Square Enix, which is still based in Tokyo.
