Impressions began as a chartered accountant's sideline and ended as Sierra's city-building studio. Its *Caesar* (1992) asked the player to build a Roman city that worked, with roads, water, housing and jobs, and then to defend it from barbarians. Under [Sierra](/vault/companies/sierra) the same engine of needs and supply moved to Egypt, Greece and China. One of the programmers who wrote *Caesar*, Simon Bradbury, later made *Stronghold*, whose remakes were still selling in 2025.

## An accountant's sideline

*Computer Gaming World* told the founding story in 1993. David Lester had qualified as a chartered accountant and was working at Arthur Young, the accountancy firm, with fast-growing businesses. With Edward Grabowski, a systems analyst he had worked with since 1984, he knew a group of struggling developers who could not find a publisher. Publishers, the magazine wrote, did not want "much of anything that didn't have a high-profile license". Lester put up money to publish some games himself and paid [Zeppelin Games](/vault/companies/zeppelin-games) to distribute them.

The company's own ten-year timeline, printed in the German *PC Player* in 1998, dates this to 1988 and names the first title as a Kenny Dalglish football game. Zeppelin launched *Kenny Dalglish Soccer Manager* on its new full-price label, Cognito, early in 1989; *ACE* reviewed the Atari ST version that March at £19.95. *CGW* reported "a huge hit and a number one sales position during the summer of 1989", but the arrangement with Zeppelin fell apart, and Impressions "emerged as a distinct brand name". Its 1990 follow-up, *Kenny Dalglish Soccer Match*, came out under the Impressions label and did not impress *CRASH*.

Looking back in 1998, Lester was frank about those years. "When Impressions was founded it was more my personal hobby," he told *PC Player*. "We worked with amateur programmers and did everything more for fun, to see whether we could get anything made." There was heavy financial pressure, he said, to release programs before they were really finished.

## Strategy and nothing else

*Rorke's Drift* (1990) set the direction. It recreated the defence of a British outpost in the Zulu war, where, as *ACE* put it, 101 able-bodied men and 36 wounded held off 4,000 warriors, and it represented each man in the battle individually. *Amiga Computing* reported that each man would have his own strengths and weaknesses and eighty frames of animation, and wondered why Impressions had "taken a break from football games" to make it. *ACE* called it "the sort of game you either love or you hate".

It sold, and in March 1991 Lester, then the managing director in London, announced that Impressions would "concentrate solely on strategy games". "The move is a direct result of market forces," he told *Amiga Computing*. "The response from consumers to our strategy games has been phenomenal, with far more praise and repeat sales than our arcade titles ever achieved." *Cohort: Fighting for Rome* followed in 1991, and after it came more strategy games for the Amiga, ST and PC: *Air Bucks*, *Cohort 2*, *Global Domination*, *D-Day: The Beginning of the End* and *Detroit*. *Amiga Power*'s review headers priced them between £29.99 and £35.

Not all of them were well received. *PC Player*'s 1998 timeline described *Cohort 2* (1993) as having gone down in history as one of the worst strategy games of all time, and Lester admitted that for a long time the company had made "the mistake of believing our products had to become ever more complicated".

## The move to America

The games found buyers in the United States, and American publishers offered licensing deals. Lester chose to move instead. According to *CGW*, Impressions set up its headquarters in Farmington, Connecticut, near the strategy publisher Omnitrend, whose games it also published; *PC Player*'s timeline dates the first American office to 1992. By 1994 the company's address was Cambridge, Massachusetts. Lester told *CGW* that he wanted Americans not "to think of our products as European games", and showed its editors an early version of *Caesar*. By the time the beta arrived, the magazine wrote, "every suggestion had been implemented".

## Caesar

Lester described *Caesar* as a bathtub idea. In 1998 he told *PC Player* it came from three games he had been playing: *[SimCity](/vault/games/sim-city)*, *Castles* and the Amiga's *[Defender of the Crown](/vault/games/defender-of-the-crown)*. Each had a weakness: *SimCity* ran out of new challenges once you had mastered the balance of a city. A Roman city that had to be walled and defended against barbarians added what was missing.

*Amiga Power* reviewed it in December 1992. "If you want Sim City with a few more barbarians, then Caesar's definitely your game," wrote Dave Golder. The player governed a province on two levels: a close-up city that needed housing, water, jobs, culture, education, a forum and roads, and a provincial map where armies had to be raised against raiders. He warned that it was harder to get into than *SimCity* and that mistakes were hard to undo, but called it "a pretty darned good and compelling game". *CGW* nominated it as a strategy game of the year in 1993 and wrote that *Caesar* had "moved Impressions up in class from British importer to American software publisher".

The credits show who made it. *Amiga Power* listed David Lester and Simon Bradbury as the authors of *Caesar Deluxe* in 1993, and Lester, Bradbury and Chris Denman for *Global Domination* the following year. In October 1994 Bradbury, by then programming the Amiga version of *Lords of the Realm*, walked *Amiga Power* through that game's castle building and sieges, and the magazine noted that he had also written *Caesar*. *Lords of the Realm*, designed by Lester and Chris Foster, was one of the best received of Impressions' games; *AmigaWorld* called it "easily the best game of its type on any platform".

## Inside Sierra

Sierra On-Line bought Impressions in 1995 for an undisclosed sum. Lester told *CGW* the sale was a sign of consolidation in the industry and gave his company "distribution clout"; development, the magazine reported, would stay autonomous. In 1998 Lester said Sierra had no direct influence on the games, but that its money let a project run longer, which mattered for quality. *PC Player* visited Impressions that year on Upper Richmond Road in London, where *Caesar III* was being made; two other projects were under way at its Boston office.

The Roman games became a series. *Caesar II* (1995) and *Caesar III* (1998) were followed by *Pharaoh* (1999), set in ancient Egypt. Its brand manager, Alex Rodberg, told *PC Zone* that the team had asked its fans where the next city game should be set, and Egypt was by far the most popular choice. The team's bibliography ran to well over a hundred books, many of them borrowed from the Boston Public Library, and the annual flooding of the Nile forced a new food model: one harvest a year to feed the city, and farmers with nothing to do for months. *Zeus: Master of Olympus* (2000) moved to Greece, and *Emperor: Rise of the Middle Kingdom* (2002) to China. Reviewing *Emperor*, *CGW* called it the "same old game system they've been honing since Caesar started it all 10 years ago".

Impressions survived Sierra's 1999 reorganisation, when *CGW* listed it among the studios kept on. Its last game was *Lords of the Realm III*, published by Vivendi Universal in 2004. By then key members of the team had left to found Tilted Mill Entertainment, whose first game, *Children of the Nile*, looked and played, in *CGW*'s words, "like the Impressions city-building games". Impressions itself closed in 2004.

## Firefly and Stronghold

Simon Bradbury and Eric Ouellette, who had met at Impressions working on the *Caesar* and *Lords of the Realm* games, founded Firefly Studios in 1999. Their first game, *Stronghold* (2001), was about building and defending a medieval castle, the part of *Lords of the Realm* that Bradbury had described to *Amiga Power* in 1994. It was a success, and Firefly made a series of it. By 2003 *CGW* was calling Bradbury Firefly's director, and in 2004 he and Ouellette, as the designers of *Stronghold 2*, said their aim was "not to reinvent the wheel" but not to repeat the same game either. Devolver Digital bought Firefly in 2021. By Firefly's account, *Stronghold: Definitive Edition* topped a global top-sellers chart on its release in 2023, and *Stronghold Crusader: Definitive Edition* sold a quarter of a million copies in its first three weeks in 2025.
