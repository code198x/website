## Overview

Impressions made wargames and strategy games for the Amiga and the ST — *Caesar*, *Cohort*, *Global Domination*, *D-Day* — at a time when the Amiga's commercial centre was somewhere else entirely. It is in the Vault because of what its credits turn into.

## Fast facts

- **Seen in the press:** *Amiga Power*, **1992–1994**, at up to **£34.99**.
- **Published:** *Caesar*, *Cohort* and *Cohort 2*, *Global Domination*, *D-Day*, and *Kenny Dalglish Soccer Manager* with [Zeppelin](/vault/companies/zeppelin-games).
- **Credited on *Global Domination* by *Amiga Power*:** David Lester, **Simon Bradbury**, Chris Denman.
- **Later:** published under [Sierra](/vault/companies/sierra), where *Caesar* became a series.

## Caesar, and what grew from it

*Caesar* (1992) asks the player to build a Roman city — lay roads, site housing, get water and food to it, keep the citizens content enough to stay — and then defend it. It is a **city builder in which the city has to work**, not merely be drawn.

That genre is now enormous and it barely existed then. Under Sierra the game became *Caesar II* and *Caesar III*, then *Pharaoh*, *Zeus* and *Emperor*: the same engine of needs, supply routes and walkers, moved across four ancient civilisations over most of a decade.

Simon Bradbury, whose name *Amiga Power* printed in a review header in 1993 alongside two others and no further comment, was the designer through that run. He then co-founded Firefly Studios and made ***Stronghold*** — a castle-building game descended directly from the same ideas, whose sequels and remasters are **still being released today**.

## Following a name instead of a company

This entry is really a demonstration of a method.

Impressions the company is hard to follow: absorbed into Sierra, folded into Sierra's own restructurings, gone as a brand. Company histories dead-end like that constantly, which is why so much of the era looks like it stops.

**The people do not stop.** Follow a name out of a credit block and the line stays continuous — Impressions to Sierra to Firefly, one designer, one set of ideas, thirty years. The credit that makes it traceable is four words in a magazine review header, printed because *Amiga Power* happened to list authors as well as publishers.

That is a strong argument for indexing credits, and a reminder of how much of this history is recoverable only from the press.

## Why it mattered

Impressions is the entry for **the city builder's origin** and for the way design lineages outlive the companies that house them.

It also marks something about the Amiga's late years. A machine remembered for platform games and demos was simultaneously the place where a management-game tradition was worked out — one that went to the PC, and stayed.
