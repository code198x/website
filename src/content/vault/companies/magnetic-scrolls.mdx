Magnetic Scrolls set out to beat Infocom at their own game—and often succeeded. Their adventures featured prose quality that approached literature, illustrations by professional artists, and a parser that understood natural English. *The Pawn*, *Guild of Thieves*, and *Corruption* represent the pinnacle of 1980s interactive fiction.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1984 by [Anita Sinclair](/vault/people/anita-sinclair), Ken Gordon, Hugh Sheridan.
- **Based:** London, UK.
- **Speciality:** premium interactive fiction with graphics.
- **Technical:** sophisticated cross-platform engine.

## A 68000 house first
Magnetic Scrolls wrote for the 16-bit machines and brought the results down,
which is the opposite way round from most British adventure publishers and
explains an otherwise odd fact: they barely register in the Spectrum press.
*Magnetic Scrolls* appears on **68 pages** of the Spectrum magazines held here
against **616** for [Level 9](/vault/companies/level-9).

*CRASH* introduced them in June 1986 in exactly those terms — "a company
specialising in adventures written for 68000 based machines" — in a report from
that April's Consumer Electronics Show, where **[Rainbird](/vault/companies/rainbird)** announced tie-ins
with Magnetic Scrolls and Level 9 at the same time.

Rainbird was [Firebird](/vault/companies/firebird)'s premium imprint and, like
Firebird and [Beyond](/vault/companies/beyond-software), a wing of British
Telecom's software operation. So the two best-regarded British adventure houses
of the period both ended up published by the phone company.

*The Pawn* went out at **£14.95** — roughly twice a typical Spectrum game, and
a price that says plainly which market the company thought it was in. *CRASH*'s
review singles out the presentation: "superbly redesigned and readable text
against a dark background", with the text intensity adjustable by the player.

## What the period record holds

Worth stating plainly, because it constrains what can honestly be claimed about the
company's reception.

***The Pawn*** took **90%** in *CRASH* (issue 41, page 58), published under the **Rainbird**
imprint — which is to say under [Telecomsoft](/vault/companies/firebird), British Telecom's
software arm. Magnetic Scrolls was a developer with a phone company for a publisher, in the
same group as Firebird's £2.50 budget shelf.

⚠ **Beyond that, the *CRASH* index of January 1988 is thin on Magnetic Scrolls**, and the
reason is a scoring convention rather than neglect: the magazine reviewed most adventures in
its **Adventure Trail** column, **out of ten**, not as percentages, and those ratings are not
convertible. *Guild of Thieves*, *Jinxter* and *Corruption* do not appear in the percentage
index at all. Any statement here about how Magnetic Scrolls "scored" relative to
percentage-rated arcade games is comparing two units.

The same caution applies to [Level 9](/vault/companies/level-9), and for the same reason.

## The clause that survived the sale

The most consequential thing Magnetic Scrolls did with Telecomsoft was not a game. It was a
contract term.

In 1989 British Telecom sold Telecomsoft to **[MicroProse](/vault/companies/microprose)**,
which meant the new owner inherited BT's software catalogue — Firebird, Rainbird, and
whatever they published. *ACE* reported in July 1989 that Magnetic Scrolls had anticipated
exactly this:

> According to **Anita Sinclair**, [Magnetic Scrolls'] contract with BT was **"assignable"** —
> that is, **in the event of BT being sold, the rights to distribute Magnetic Scrolls product
> was not to be sold with it.**

So when the phone company sold its software arm, the Scrolls games did not go with it. A
small London developer had written a change-of-control provision into its publishing deal
and it held.

What followed was conspicuously civil. Sinclair on the buyer:

> **MicroProse are a very competent organisation**, and I've got the greatest respect for
> Stuart. **We are ready to discuss the situation at any time.**

And Telecomsoft's **Stuart Bell** on the games he had just failed to acquire: they are
"**quality adventures that we'd be proud to sell**".

⚠ Note what this establishes about the period. Publishing contracts of the mid-eighties were
informal enough that the [bedroom-coder](/vault/phenomena/bedroom-coder) entry records
authors losing their games when a publisher went under. Magnetic Scrolls was on the other end
of that spectrum — professionally advised, and structured to survive its publisher changing
hands.

## Anita Sinclair

One of the few women running a British software house in the period, and the press treated
her as a figure rather than a curiosity. *ACE* called her, with evident affection, "the
infamous Anita Sinclair".

She was also asked to represent a position she flatly declined. In January 1988 *ACE* ran a
feature on why women were not playing or making games; Sinclair objected to a competition
aimed at women players as a **cynical and patronising marketing device**, and then refused
the label the magazine offered her:

> **"I cannot stand or tolerate feminism of any form."**

The same feature notes she "doesn't have much time for arcade games" — adventures "demand
thought" and are "something you can stop and start" — which is as good an account as any of
why the company made what it made. It also names **Hanan Samara**, who ran the development
house **Dalali**, as "one of the few women making a living from developing arcade games".

⚠ Recorded here as printed. The quotation is a period statement by a named public figure
about her own views, and it sits oddly beside her later status as the most-cited woman in
British 8-bit publishing.

## The family in the credits

[Level 9](/vault/companies/level-9) is the family business of the British adventure scene.
Magnetic Scrolls was partly one too. *ACE*, December 1987, on *Jinxter*:

> *Jinxter* has some great text, in part written by **Georgina Sinclair, sister of the
> infamous Anita Sinclair** from Magnetic Scrolls, with additions from other quarters.

The prose the company is praised for has a name attached to it, and it is not one of the
three founders listed in the fast facts.

## End

The adventure game market collapsed in the early 90s. A planned Magnetic Scrolls collection for CD-ROM never materialised; the company closed in 1992.
