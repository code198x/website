## Overview

Milton Bradley, the American board game company behind *The Game of Life* and *Battleship*, made an ambitious foray into video games. Their most notable contribution was the Vectrex, a unique vector-graphics console with built-in display. While the video game division didn't survive the 1983 crash intact, the Vectrex remains a beloved curiosity.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1860 by Milton Bradley in Springfield, Massachusetts.
- **Video game era:** 1979-1984 (primary activity).
- **Key hardware:** Vectrex (1982).
- **Acquired by:** Hasbro (1984).
- **Legacy:** board game adaptations continued under Hasbro.

## The Vectrex

A unique console:
- **Built-in display:** monochrome vector monitor.
- **Vector graphics:** crisp line-based visuals.
- **Colour overlays:** plastic screens added colour.
- **Library:** *Mine Storm*, *Scramble*, *Star Trek*.
- **Market timing:** released just before the 1983 crash.

⚠ **Milton Bradley did not develop the Vectrex.** *BYTE*'s first look, December 1982, is
unambiguous: the machine was "**developed and distributed by General Consumer
Electronics**" — see [GCE](/vault/companies/gce) — at **$200**, built around a **Motorola
68A09** processor. Milton Bradley acquired GCE and took the system over. The distinction
matters because the Vectrex is the most interesting thing in this entry and it was somebody
else's design.

## The death, reported live

*Big K*, June 1984, caught the end as it happened, and the detail is better than any
retrospective:

> In America, Vectrex [company] **Milton Bradley has stopped production of the system and is
> dumping redundant stock at low prices**. It has, however, [promised] to continue servicing
> units.

The British end of the story is the part worth keeping. The magazine went to **MB's British
operation** for confirmation and got a stonewall: Vectrex sales manager **John Garner**
"would only confirm" that the system had been discontinued in the US, adding that
"**we're reviewing our marketing strategy**".

A console discontinued in its home market while the importing subsidiary declines to say
whether it is discontinued here — that is what a hardware withdrawal looked like from the
buyer's side in 1984, months before anyone would write the word "crash" about it.

## What it was going to become

Worth recording because it did not happen. The Italian magazine *Computer Games*, April
1984, reports that **Milton Bradley**, as "producer and distributor of the Vectrex system",
had already planned "**the production of a module that would transform this console into a
home computer**", and that the machine was designed to accommodate a **voice synthesiser**.

The Vectrex is remembered as a curiosity that arrived too late. The plan was for it to become
a computer, which is the same plan almost every console manufacturer of the period had, and
almost none delivered.
