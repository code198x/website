For about fifteen years, if a British publisher had bought the home rights to an arcade hit or a film and needed it on every format at once, there was a good chance the work went to an office block in Croydon, south London. Probe Software converted *Out Run* for the 8-bit computers, *Golden Axe* and *Smash TV* for the 16-bit ones, and *Mortal Kombat* for the Mega Drive. By 1995 *3DO Magazine* called it "the UK's largest independent developer". Its founder, Fergus McGovern, was still a teenager when he started it, and by *Edge*'s account never wrote a line of code: the business side was his job.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 7 June 1984, by Fergus McGovern and the programmer Vakis Paraskeva.
- **Based:** Croydon, south London.
- **Business:** at first a small publisher of its own games; from about 1986 a developer working for other publishers, above all on arcade conversions and film licences.
- **Clients:** [U.S. Gold](/vault/companies/us-gold), [Ocean](/vault/companies/ocean-software), [Firebird](/vault/companies/firebird), [Mastertronic](/vault/companies/mastertronic), [Mirrorsoft](/vault/companies/mirrorsoft), [Virgin](/vault/companies/virgin-games), Sega and [Acclaim](/vault/companies/acclaim), among others.
- **Bought by:** Acclaim, in 1995. Renamed Acclaim Studios London by 1999; the studio closed in 2000.

## A publisher first

McGovern came to computers through a summer job. At 16 he was the third employee of P&P Micros, a distributor of Apple computers, and his brother Kevin, later Probe's financial director, was the fifth. McGovern told *Edge* that the firm began when his next-door neighbours made £1,800 selling on a single copy of the spreadsheet VisiCalc that they had brought back from California.

His friend Vakis Paraskeva was writing Commodore 16 and 64 games for Romik Software. McGovern persuaded him that they should work for themselves, "with him writing the games and me handling the sales and marketing". According to *Amiga Power*, Probe started on 7 June 1984, and a friend of McGovern's thought up the name one night at a disco. Its first game was *Escape from Alcatraz* for the Commodore 64. It followed that with *Defence 16*, a *Defender*-style game for the Commodore 16, and was delighted when WH Smith agreed to stock it. Then came *Devil's Crown* for the Amstrad CPC, written by Brian O'Shaughnessy, which McGovern called "our most successful game at that time": it sold well in Europe and even reached Harrods. Probe also published *The Adventures of Basildon Bond*, starring the television comedian Russ Abbot. "The licence cost us nothing," McGovern said in 1992; it was done on a royalty basis.

Everything, from the packaging to the marketing, was done by the two directors, and it was "turning into a 24 hours a day job". The company had cash but no sales team, so it could not afford to fund original games. After selling its Spectrum version of *Devil's Crown* to Mastertronic, Probe stopped publishing and began developing games for other publishers, "letting them add their own marketing expertise and take their own risks".

## A conversion house

In August 1988 *Amstrad Computer User* visited Probe's "bright, modern rooms in an award-winning block in Croydon", where an arcade *1943* machine cycled through its demo. McGovern listed what the company had converted: *Xevious*, *Metrocross*, *Slap Fight*, *Arkanoid*, *Solomon's Key* and more, for Ocean, U.S. Gold, Activision, Mirrorsoft, Psygnosis, Epyx, Mastertronic and Firebird. "We'd transformed into coin-op specialist," he said. Probe did not keep its programmers in one room. It grouped them into small internal teams of two programmers, an artist and a musician, and rented four-bedroom houses around the country for them to live and work in.

The games that made its name came in 1987 and 1988. For U.S. Gold it converted Sega's *[Out Run](/vault/games/out-run)*. The 8-bit versions were not well reviewed: *CRASH*'s three reviewers disagreed wildly over the Spectrum version, and *Edge* later called it "an appalling imitation of a state-of-the-art coin-op". But it sold. McGovern remembered it as "a number one title that sat in the charts for nine months", and his initials appear on the game's Ferrari. Looking back in 1992, he was frank: "At the time I was pleased with what we managed but with hindsight I'm not so happy."

Probe also made games of its own design for other labels. *Trantor: The Last Stormtrooper* (Go!, 1987) scored 68% in *CRASH*, and *Savage* (Firebird, 1988), a three-part fantasy action game, was written by [David Perry](/vault/people/david-perry), who later co-founded Shiny Entertainment in California. The access code to *Savage*'s last section was FERGUS. [Raffaele Cecco](/vault/people/raffaele-cecco) converted his *Cybernoid* to the Spectrum for Probe, and Probe's own Spectrum *[Turrican](/vault/games/turrican)* won 93% from *CRASH* in 1990.

## Consoles, and the Mortal Kombat games

Probe's way into consoles was a chess program. Frank Herman, who had bought games from Probe before, passed on a request from Sega in Japan for a Master System chess game in five months. Writing a chess engine from scratch in that time looked impossible, so Probe found an electronic chess board that used the same Z80 processor as the Master System, licensed its program from the author, removed the parts that talked to the board's hardware and added Master System graphics. "It worked and we finished it in four months," McGovern said. "That was it — we were official console developers." Its music was the first Master System job for [Matt Furniss](/vault/people/matt-furniss) of Krisalis, who went on to score many more Probe console games.

By March 1992 Probe had "nearly 60 programmers and graphic artists" working for it. McGovern explained the economics to *Amiga Power*: "the publisher gets 80 percent of the profit and we only see around 20 percent", but the publisher took the risk, and if a game flopped the developer still kept its advance. The deadlines were the hard part. On *Smash TV*, he said, the promised arcade graphics arrived four days after the conversion was finished, so the team had drawn them all from scratch. Film licences such as *Terminator 2*, *Alien 3* and *RoboCop 3* followed, alongside Sega arcade games including *Golden Axe*, *Super Monaco GP* and *Turbo OutRun*.

For Acclaim, Probe made the Mega Drive version of *[Mortal Kombat](/vault/games/mortal-kombat)*, released on "Mortal Monday", 13 September 1993. Unlike Sculptured Software's Super NES version, it kept all the fatalities behind a secret cheat. *C&VG* called it "the best one-on-one Megadrive beat 'em up ever". Probe converted *Mortal Kombat II* as well, and for *T2: The Arcade Game* on the Amiga it worked from source code that came directly from the arcade machine.

By 1995 the scale of the work had grown. Probe's project manager for *Primal Rage*, Robert O'Farrell, known as "Barg", had started it a year earlier "for no less than 17 formats". The 3DO version was Probe's first game for that machine, and O'Farrell admitted: "From its reputation, I thought it would be a slow machine. In fact, the finished game will run in 2 frames."

## Acclaim

In 1995 Acclaim bought Probe, as it bought Iguana Entertainment and Sculptured Software the same year. *Edge* reported that the deal was rumoured to be worth £60 million, and that McGovern had been made a vice-president of Acclaim. Much of the payment was in Acclaim shares. *Edge* counted more than 400 Probe titles by then, with retail sales of over $1.6 billion.

Under Acclaim, Probe moved to 32-bit consoles and to original games: *Alien Trilogy* and *Die Hard Trilogy* for the PlayStation and Saturn, and *Forsaken* (1998), a PC game that *Edge* thought looked "like taking the *Descent* concept about as far as it can go". In December 1997 *Edge* reported that Fergus and Kevin McGovern had left the company, though Acclaim at first denied it; by September 1998 it was describing Fergus as the "now-departed Probe founder", with a new company of his own. By late 1999 the studio was working as Acclaim Studios London, "formerly Probe Entertainment", on games such as *Armorines* for the Nintendo 64. In 2000 *Edge* reported that its Croydon studio, "formerly known as Probe, the veteran 'hit' factory", was to close.

McGovern went on to co-found HotGen, a toys and games company, and served on the board of Crystal Palace Football Club. He died in February 2016, aged 50.
