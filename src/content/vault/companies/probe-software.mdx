## Overview

Probe converted other people's games onto other people's machines, for over a decade, at a pace few British studios matched. *OutRun*, *T2: The Arcade Game*, *Alien 3*, the console *Mortal Kombat* conversions — the *Amiga Power* writers refer to "Probe's 3D conversion of…" and "the original *OutRun* conversion" as landmarks their readers would recognise without explanation.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** London, 1984, by Fergus McGovern.
- **Seen in the press:** across the 8-bit and 16-bit years; *T2: The Arcade Game* in *Amiga Power*, 1994.
- **Specialised in:** arcade conversions, film licences, and multi-platform console versions.
- **Later:** absorbed by Acclaim and renamed Acclaim Studios London.

## The licence conversion as a job

Probe's work sat at the intersection of the two hardest commercial constraints in the industry.

**A licence has a date.** A film tie-in ships when the film does, an arcade conversion while the cabinet is still in arcades. The deadline is set by somebody else and does not move, which inverts the usual relationship between scope and schedule: you cut the game to fit the calendar.

**A conversion has a reference.** Everyone reviewing it has played the original, and the review is a comparison. There is no credit available for a good game that differs from the arcade version — the job is fidelity on hardware that cannot manage it, which means deciding **what to sacrifice** and being judged on that choice.

Doing this repeatedly, on time, across a dozen machines, is a genuine industrial capability, and it is nothing like the capability that makes an original game good. It also pays reliably, which mattered.

## How British developers ended

Probe's ending is the most common one in this Vault, and worth naming as a pattern rather than an event.

A studio does good work for a publisher. The publisher, which owns the licences and the distribution, comes to depend on it and buys it — this is cheaper and safer than losing it to a competitor. The studio becomes **an internal team, renamed after its owner**, and its own name disappears. When the publisher later contracts, the team goes with it.

Probe became Acclaim Studios London. [Gremlin](/vault/companies/gremlin-graphics) went to Infogrames, [Impressions](/vault/companies/impressions) into Sierra, [Beyond](/vault/companies/beyond-software) into British Telecom's software arm. The independent British studio of the eighties usually finished as a wing of somebody larger, and the trail goes cold at the point the name changes — which is why, as the [Impressions](/vault/companies/impressions) entry argues, following people works where following companies does not.

## Why it mattered

Probe is the entry for **conversion as a craft and a business**, the work that made a game exist on the machine you actually owned.

It also marks something about how the British industry's skills were spent. A studio capable of shipping accurate, on-time versions of somebody else's game on five platforms is an impressive thing to have built, and almost none of what it produced is remembered under its own name.
