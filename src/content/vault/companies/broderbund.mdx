Brøderbund began in a rented house in Eugene, Oregon, with a lawyer who had taught himself to write games on a TRS-80 and a brother who knew how to sell them. It grew into one of the largest software publishers in America, and it did so mostly with other people's programs. Dan Gorlin's *[Choplifter](/vault/games/choplifter)*, Doug Smith's *[Lode Runner](/vault/games/lode-runner)* and Jordan Mechner's *[Karateka](/vault/games/karateka)* and *[Prince of Persia](/vault/games/prince-of-persia)* came in from outside, and the company put their authors' names on the boxes. Then it found a second business that outlasted the games: *The Print Shop*, which turned a home computer and a dot-matrix printer into a greetings-card factory, and *Where in the World Is Carmen Sandiego?*, a detective game that children ended up playing at school. In 1993 it published Cyan's *[Myst](/vault/games/myst)*.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1980, in Eugene, Oregon, by Doug and Gary Carlston; their sister Cathy joined by 1981.
- **Based:** San Rafael, California, by 1982.
- **Best known for:** *Choplifter* (1982), *Lode Runner* (1983), *Karateka* (1984), *The Print Shop* (1984), *Where in the World Is Carmen Sandiego?* (1985), *Prince of Persia* (1989), and as publisher of *Myst* (1993).
- **Public company:** from November 1991.
- **Bought by:** [The Learning Company](/vault/companies/the-learning-company), in 1998, for $420 million.

## Two brothers and a Japanese neighbour

Doug Carlston was a Harvard-trained lawyer with a practice in Maine who had bought a TRS-80 in the late 1970s, officially for the office. He wrote a space strategy game, *Galactic Empire*, and followed it with *Galactic Trader* and *Galactic Revolution*; other publishers sold them, and by 1979, *Retro Gamer* later reported, he was earning more from programming than from law. He drove west to see his brother Gary, who had studied Scandinavian languages, coached a women's basketball team in Stockholm and was then living in Eugene. Doug proposed that they go into software publishing together.

The brothers started in 1980 with a few thousand dollars borrowed from their mother, her sister, their brother Don and a credit card. They looked for advertisements from people selling programs and telephoned them; by May 1980 they had sales of about $900 a month. At the West Coast Computer Faire Gary had an Apple II on his stand. The Japanese company on the next stand, Star Craft, had brought Apple games but nothing to show them on, so Gary lent them his computer and then bought the American rights to their games. One, *Apple Galaxian*, a copy of Namco's *[Galaxian](/vault/games/galaxian)*, sold $100,000 worth in a single month. "The Star Craft games were technically superior to anything produced in the US," Gary said, "and American programmers wanted to meet the Japanese guys and find out how they did it, so they contacted us."

By 1981 Cathy Carlston had joined as office manager, and the company was still a household business: Cathy did the accounts in the kitchen, Gary took phone calls in the living room and Doug packed disks. That year it sold more than $1 million of software. It moved to California soon after; *BYTE* still gave its address as a post-office box in Eugene in December 1981, and by September 1982 its advertisements came from Fourth Street in San Rafael.

### Why the ø

The slashed o is a joke on the computer habit of writing a zero as Ø. Gary Carlston told *Retro Gamer* that the name had a less comfortable origin: Doug had used "Broederbond" for a group of traders in one of his games, taking it from the Afrikaner secret society in South Africa, which he knew about from teaching in Botswana in the 1960s. The brothers changed the spelling to a Scandinavian form, which Gary said means "association of brothers" in any Germanic language, "to make it more benign".

## Games from outside

Gary ran product development. The company promoted its programmers on its packaging and in its advertisements, and even let David Snider name his pinball game *David's Midnight Magic*. In December 1982 it won *[Electronic Games](/vault/magazines/electronic-games)*' award for computer game of the year.

In 1982 a musician called Dan Gorlin walked into a computer shop in Los Angeles with a helicopter game he had written on a borrowed Apple II, and the shop told him to call the Carlstons. *Choplifter*, in which the player flies in to rescue hostages from the Bungeling Empire, was "technically top notch and just plain fun", Gary said, though he asked Gorlin to make the helicopter easier to fly. When the teenage Jordan Mechner sent Brøderbund an *Asteroids*-style game, Doug Carlston rang to turn it down, calling it "a little bit last year – kind of 1981", and posted him a copy of *Choplifter* with joysticks to play it. Mechner's answer was *Karateka* (1984), a martial-arts game animated from film of real people. Gary said he originally turned it down "because it didn't seem to have much gameplay", but it filled an empty monitor on the company's stand at the Consumer Electronics Show in Chicago, drew a crowd, and was signed.

*Lode Runner* (1983) came from Doug Smith. Gary first thought it looked like a copy of *Apple Panic*, then reached "about level 30" and decided the puzzles were "really clever"; the company paid Smith a $10,000 advance to expand it, and it shipped with 150 screens and an editor for making more. It and *Choplifter* were licensed to Japanese arcade manufacturers, a rare journey for American computer games; [Irem](/vault/companies/irem) made the *Lode Runner* coin-op in 1984. Will Wright's first game, *Raid on Bungeling Bay*, also came out through Brøderbund, and when he made *[SimCity](/vault/games/sim-city)*, [Maxis](/vault/companies/maxis) published it with Brøderbund as its distributor. Later outside games included Steve Waldo's *[Wings of Fury](/vault/games/wings-of-fury)*, reviewed as new in 1988, and in 1989 *Prince of Persia*, which Mechner made in offices he shared with the company. [Origin](/vault/companies/origin-systems) used Brøderbund as its distributor from 1987 until it "graduated" in 1989.

In Britain the games were first sold under other names. From 1985 [Ariolasoft](/vault/companies/ariolasoft), the software arm of the German record company Ariola, released *Lode Runner*, *Choplifter*, *Spelunker* and *Karateka*, and later [Domark](/vault/companies/domark) handled *Prince of Persia*.

## The Print Shop and Carmen Sandiego

*The Print Shop*, by David Balsam and Martin Kahn, came out for the Apple II in 1984. *Electronic Games* explained the appeal: pick a border, a picture and one of eight typefaces, and the program "prints out exactly the number of copies needed" of a greetings card, a sign or a banner. It sold for years, and in 1987 Brøderbund settled a "look and feel" lawsuit against Kyocera Unison, whose *PrintMaster* resembled it, with Kyocera making "a substantial payment". Brøderbund also published the *Bank Street Writer* word processor, developed with the Bank Street College of Education.

*Where in the World Is Carmen Sandiego?* (1985) was one of Gary's personal projects. He remembered the Carlston children "constantly quizzing each other on everything from state capitals to tallest mountains". The box held a copy of *The World Almanac*; the player chased Carmen's gang round 30 cities, with, the advertisement promised, "10 possible suspects" and "nearly 1,000 clues". Dane Bigham, who programmed it and designed its gameplay, said it was deliberately "a game first and educational second", and was filed under "explorational" because "educational" sounded boring. "Carmen wasn't meant for the educational market," Gary said: it "languished for about 18 months" until teachers found it. Sequels followed, among them *Where in the USA Is Carmen Sandiego?* and, in 1989, *Where in Time Is Carmen Sandiego?*.

## Growing up

The family partnership did not last. Gary left in 1989, and Cathy with him. "When I finally left in 1989, I remember saying that I wanted us all to go back to being a family; that I couldn't stand being business partners any more," he said. Doug stayed as chief executive, with Ed Auer as president.

In March 1991 Brøderbund and [Sierra On-Line](/vault/companies/sierra) signed a letter of intent to merge, in a stock swap the *Wall Street Journal* valued at over $88 million; Sierra's Ken Williams would have run the combined company. The deal fell through, and in November 1991 Brøderbund sold its own shares to the public. It grew with the CD-ROM: the Living Books series of animated storybooks began in 1992 with Mercer Mayer's *Just Grandma and Me*, which *Computer Gaming World* expected to become "the Carmen Sandiego of the talking book genre", and Brøderbund's children's catalogue included *Kid Pix*.

In 1993 Brøderbund published *Myst*, made by Rand and Robyn Miller's studio [Cyan](/vault/companies/cyan), first for the Macintosh on CD-ROM. *Retro Gamer* later put its sales at six million copies and called it the best-selling PC game until *The Sims*. In February 1994 Brøderbund agreed to merge with [Electronic Arts](/vault/companies/electronic-arts) in a deal worth about $400 million, but in May the two called it off after falls in their share prices, with Brøderbund paying Electronic Arts $10 million to end the agreement. In 1995 it bid for The Learning Company and lost to SoftKey. In 1997 it set up its own games label, Red Orb Entertainment, which published Mechner's *The Last Express* and the *Myst* sequel *Riven*.

## After 1998

In August 1998 the company that had beaten it to The Learning Company, and had taken that name, bought Brøderbund for $420 million, including Red Orb. *Computer Gaming World* suspected that the lure was *Myst*, *Riven* and *Carmen Sandiego*, and noted that more than $607 million of sales over the previous three years had made only $31 million in profit. About 500 employees were laid off, and Doug Carlston left.

Mattel bought The Learning Company in 1999 and gave it to Gores Technology Group in 2000. In 2001 Gores sold the entertainment titles, *Myst* and *Prince of Persia* among them, to [Ubisoft](/vault/companies/ubisoft), and the educational and home programs to the Irish company Riverdeep, later part of Houghton Mifflin Harcourt. The Brøderbund name lived on as a brand on programs such as *The Print Shop*, published by Encore under licence, and in 2017 Houghton Mifflin Harcourt offered the Brøderbund and *Print Shop* names for licensing. In 2012 Gary Carlston told *Retro Gamer* that a reunion had drawn "hundreds of people".
