For its first ten years Harmonix was very good at making music programs that nobody bought. Its first product, by its founders' account, sold about 300 copies, and its two PlayStation 2 games won admirers and hardly any customers. Then, in 2005, it made a game played on a small plastic guitar. Two years later the studio had grown from 90 people to 300, was shipping millions of plastic instruments made in China, and had helped build a market on which Americans spent about $1.9 billion in 2008.

## Fast facts

- **Full name:** Harmonix Music Systems.
- **Founded:** 1995, in Cambridge, Massachusetts.
- **Founders:** Alex Rigopulos and Eran Egozy, who met at the MIT Media Lab.
- **Best known for:** *Frequency* (2001), *Amplitude* (2003), *[Guitar Hero](/vault/games/guitar-hero)* (2005), *[Rock Band](/vault/games/rock-band)* (2007), *Dance Central* (2010).
- **Owners:** independent until MTV Networks, part of Viacom, bought it in October 2006; sold by Viacom in December 2010 to an affiliate of the investment firm Columbus Nova; part of [Epic Games](/vault/companies/epic-games) since November 2021.

## Music for non-musicians

Rigopulos and Egozy met as graduate students at the MIT Media Lab, where Egozy worked in Tod Machover's group on programs that generate music. Rigopulos wondered, Egozy recalled, "Wouldn't it be cool if we took a joystick that's a more friendly interface and used it to drive the parameters of our generative music system?" That became Harmonix's first product, *The Axe*, which let players improvise instrumental solos by waggling a PC joystick. Egozy later called it a "total flop". The company tried other routes, including a system for Disney at Epcot and a venture with karaoke machines in Japan, before, in Egozy's words, "it took us quite a while to discover the way to satisfy our mission, which is to let everyone in the world experience the joy of making music. As it turns out, that was through video games."

The push towards games came from a PlayStation game. "It began with Parappa The Rapper," Rigopulos told *Edge* in 2010: "it wasn't the first rhythm-action game, but it was the first great one, and it altered Harmonix's trajectory." He found Konami's Bemani arcade games "sorely deficient" by comparison, "the gameplay was shallow, the visual design was uninspired, the music selection and sound quality was poor", and saw "an opportunity to take music games to the next level" (see [PaRappa the Rapper](/vault/games/parappa-the-rapper) and [Bemani](/vault/companies/bemani)).

## Frequency and Amplitude

Several of the people who built Harmonix's first game came from [Looking Glass](/vault/companies/looking-glass), the studio behind *Thief*. One of them, Greg LoPiccolo, told *Edge* that they had deliberately set out not to make "a Looking Glass game". They worked on the *Frequency* prototype for a year, took it to Sony, and "they bought it in 15 minutes"; LoPiccolo remembered being halfway through the first pitch when Sony said yes: "We were like, 'But we have eight slides left!'"

*Frequency* (2001) sent the player down a neon tunnel in which each wall was one instrument of a song; completing a phrase on one track set it playing, and the player moved on to the next until the whole arrangement was running. *Amplitude* (2003) kept the idea, and *Edge* gave it 8 out of 10. Neither sold. "Frequency and Amplitude were both really fun games to play — for people who bothered to give them a try," Rigopulos said. "But those games were... strange." Harmonix kept going with work for other publishers: *Karaoke Revolution* for Konami, and *EyeToy: AntiGrav* for Sony, a hoverboard racer steered by the player's own body in front of the EyeToy camera, in which, as *Edge* put it, "Jump is jump".

## Guitar Hero

*[Guitar Hero](/vault/games/guitar-hero)*, made for the peripherals company RedOctane and released in North America in November 2005, was the game that sold. According to MIT News, Harmonix had 45 people when its sales began rising every month instead of falling after Christmas. LoPiccolo called it "a mutant child of *Amplitude* and *KR*". Rigopulos thought the earlier games had offered "no easy narrative metaphor for people to grasp on to, no aspirational element"; a guitar and a rock soundtrack supplied both.

The success brought offers. "We started getting lots of phone calls from lots of pretty big companies," the creative director, Josh Randall, told *Edge* in late 2006, and MTV Networks, part of Viacom, agreed to buy the studio. Viacom's accounts record that it completed the purchase in October 2006 for an initial $175 million. Randall expected little to change: "MTV isn't going to be our publisher: we're still going to be working with other publishers."

The same year Activision bought RedOctane for $100 million. Harmonix did not own the *Guitar Hero* name, "and so found itself cut loose", as *Edge* put it. It finished *Guitar Hero II* and left the series to Activision and [Neversoft](/vault/companies/neversoft).

## Rock Band

Harmonix's answer was a game for a whole band, and MTV did publish it after all, with [Electronic Arts](/vault/companies/electronic-arts) distributing. *[Rock Band](/vault/games/rock-band)* came out in North America in November 2007 with a guitar, a drum kit and a microphone, and songs taken from the original studio multitrack recordings. Building it changed the company. Harmonix grew from 90 to 300 staff in 24 months, "people sitting in the hallways with their laptops resting on skateboards" because there were no desks, and a software company that had never made hardware sent engineers to factories in southern China to oversee the instruments. "A lot of us felt as though we aged five years in 18 months," Rigopulos said. The risk was real: "Rock Band was our only franchise. If it had failed, it would have posed an existential threat to us as a company."

It did not fail. Viacom reported that in 2008 alone *Rock Band* and *Rock Band 2* sold more than 3.8 million instrument bundles in the United States, and that players downloaded more than 30 million songs. *The Beatles: Rock Band* followed in 2009, and *Rock Band 3* in 2010 added a keyboard and "Pro" modes played on a real guitar; *Edge* gave it 10 out of 10 and called it "simply magnificent".

## After the boom

The market collapsed almost as fast as it had grown. Rigopulos later admitted that Harmonix and Activision had both behaved as if "there was really only space for one [franchise]", overspending on marketing and music licences in a way "that didn't ultimately serve either of us well". Harmonix turned to Microsoft's Kinect camera with *Dance Central*, a dance game with no instrument to buy, which launched with the camera in 2010. In December 2010 Viacom sold Harmonix to Harmonix-SBE Holdings, an affiliate of the investment firm Columbus Nova.

The independent Harmonix revived its band game as *Rock Band 4* in 2015 and made *Fuser* (2020), published by NCSoft, a game of mixing the vocals, drums and bass of different hit records together like a DJ; *Wireframe* called it "a hugely impressive piece of music software" whose game got in the way of the fun. In November 2021 Epic Games bought the studio, saying its expertise was needed "to reimagine how music is experienced, created and distributed". Its *Fortnite Festival* launched inside *Fortnite* in December 2023: a band game with lanes for lead, bass, drums and vocals, which the [music games](/vault/genres/music-games) entry follows.
