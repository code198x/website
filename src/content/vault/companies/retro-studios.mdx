In the summer of 2002, before anyone outside the company had played a finished level of *Metroid Prime*, *IGN Unplugged* summed up the mood around its developer. After "two years of rumors regarding Retro Studios' stability", the playable demo at that year's E3 show was so polished that "almost instantaneously, Retro Studios was being heralded as one of Nintendo's best-kept secrets". In the midst of "numerous internal management firestorms", the magazine said, the team "had done the impossible" and made a first-person *Metroid* that felt like the old ones.

Retro was an unusual thing: an American studio founded to make games only for Nintendo, which within four years had cancelled most of its projects, laid off dozens of staff, lost its founder and been bought outright by Nintendo. The one game it had left began a trilogy, and Retro is still making *Metroid* games.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1998, in Austin, Texas, by Jeff Spangenberg with Nintendo's backing.
- **Owner:** [Nintendo](/vault/companies/nintendo), wholly since 2002.
- **First game:** *[Metroid Prime](/vault/games/metroid-prime)* (GameCube, 2002).
- **Metroid Prime series:** *Metroid Prime 2: Echoes* (2004), *Metroid Prime 3: Corruption* (Wii, 2007), *Metroid Prime Remastered* (Switch, 2023), *Metroid Prime 4: Beyond* (2025).
- **Donkey Kong:** *Donkey Kong Country Returns* (Wii, 2010) and *Donkey Kong Country: Tropical Freeze* (Wii U, 2014).
- **President:** Michael Kelbaugh, formerly of Nintendo of America, by 2004.

## A studio for the next console

Jeff Spangenberg had founded Iguana Entertainment, the Austin studio behind *Turok: Dinosaur Hunter*, and sold it to [Acclaim](/vault/companies/acclaim). In April 1999 the Brazilian magazine *SuperGamePower* reported that he was leaving the company he had founded to start another, Retro Studios. The new company worked only for Nintendo, on several projects, none of them for any console then on sale. The magazine took that as a sign that Nintendo's next machine was already up and running.

Nintendo was investing in developers it trusted. "We have other companies that we are making strategic investments in," Howard Lincoln, the president of Nintendo of America, told *Edge* that autumn, "but we're very careful to limit those investments to really first-class developers. Retro Studios comes to mind." Michael Kelbaugh, who had worked for Nintendo of America, helped to set Retro up.

Spangenberg recruited widely, partly from Iguana's *Turok* teams. The British programmer Andy O'Neil, who had moved to Austin in the summer of 1999 to work on *Turok 3* at Iguana, remembered Spangenberg picking him up in his Ferrari, taking him to brunch and offering "loads more money than I was on to join Retro Studios". "There are loads of Brits in the games industry in Austin," he told *Retro Gamer*; Spangenberg "knew there were loads of really good programmers in the UK" who were badly paid at home. The studio began four games for the console code-named Dolphin, which became the GameCube: an American football game, a vehicle combat game called *Car Combat*, a role-playing game called *Raven Blade*, and an action-adventure.

## Cancellations

The projects did not survive. In April 2001 *IGN Unplugged* reported that "Nintendo forced the layoffs of more than 20 Retro employees and ordered that all work cease immediately" on *Car Combat* and *Football 2002*, even though *Car Combat* was the most complete game the studio had. *Raven Blade* followed on 20 July 2001. "Retro, along with Nintendo, has decided its most effective approach as a videogame developer is to focus on Metroid and give it the attention the franchise deserves," Nintendo said. "To do that, they refined staffing and are laying off 26 people, mainly the team that worked on Raven Blade."

What was left was the action-adventure. *Retro Gamer*'s history says that on a visit to Austin, [Shigeru Miyamoto](/vault/people/shigeru-miyamoto) was unimpressed by most of what he saw, but liked one team's prototype with a female lead. Retro was given *[Metroid](/vault/games/metroid)*, and it was Miyamoto who suggested switching it from third person to first. The doubts were public. Miyamoto held back a playable demo at E3 in 2001 because of worries about the controls, and again at Nintendo's Space World show that August. "Discovering no sound and poor control just a few days ago made me decide not to present it," he told *Edge*, which reported "rumours coming out of Retro Studios of development hell".

In May 2002 Nintendo bought the shares in Retro it did not already own. The Australian magazine *Hyper* put the price at $1 million and reported that Retro was now "wholly owned by Nintendo"; Nintendo fans, it said, could sleep easy knowing that Retro "will only ever make GameCube and GBA games". *Retro Gamer* records that Spangenberg was ousted and the management changed.

## Metroid Prime and its sequels

*Metroid Prime* was released in North America in November 2002. *Edge*, which had been sceptical, gave it nine out of ten and admitted it "should have had a little more faith in Nintendo's decision and been a little more confident in Retro's ability". In May 2003 it named *Metroid Prime* its game of the year. The game's story, from the scan visor to the Morph Ball, is told in the [Metroid Prime](/vault/games/metroid-prime) entry.

Nintendo's producer on the series, Kensuke Tanabe, said later that the scan visor was "something that I thought up with Retro". At a round table in 2004 he recalled the reaction to turning a 2D series into a first-person game: people had told them "You can't do that." Kelbaugh, by then Retro's president, described his job as managing the business and "the daily interaction and communications with Mr. Tanabe". Retro followed the first game with *Metroid Prime 2: Echoes* for the GameCube in 2004, in which Samus moves between light and dark versions of the planet Aether, and *Metroid Prime 3: Corruption* for the Wii in 2007. *Edge* thought it "logical that Retro has allowed the Wii Remote to define the focus" of the third game, which leaned further towards action. The three were collected for the Wii in 2009.

## Donkey Kong and Mario Kart

Retro's next game revived another series that had been made outside Japan, Rare's *[Donkey Kong Country](/vault/games/donkey-kong-country)*. *Donkey Kong Country Returns* came out for the Wii in December 2010. *Edge* found that "with motion control, Retro Studios has combined the precision platforming of Rare's originals with the satisfying, chest-thumping physicality of *Jungle Beat*." Retro then built courses for the 3DS game *Mario Kart 7* (2011), and *Edge* remarked that the studio had found itself "becoming the 'new Rare'". *Donkey Kong Country: Tropical Freeze*, again produced by Tanabe, followed for the Wii U in 2014, and Retro ported it to the Switch in 2018.

In 2011 *Edge* asked Kelbaugh and Tanabe how the partnership worked. Kelbaugh said deadlines came second: "it's never done as far as we're concerned. It's constant refinement." He would "much rather be judged based on the quality of my product as opposed to whether or not it made it out on time". Tanabe said that over the years Retro had "gradually come to really think like Nintendo", so there was "a lot less need for explanation and supervision".

## Back to Metroid

In January 2019 Nintendo announced that development of *Metroid Prime 4*, begun elsewhere, had been restarted at Retro. In February 2023 it released *Metroid Prime Remastered* for the Switch, Retro's rebuilt version of the first game, and *Metroid Prime 4: Beyond* followed in December 2025.
