Software Projects was the company [Matthew Smith](/vault/people/matthew-smith) set up when he left [Bug-Byte](/vault/companies/bug-byte), and it ended up with both of his hits. It took *[Manic Miner](/vault/games/manic-miner)* away from its first publisher, and it published *[Jet Set Willy](/vault/games/jet-set-willy)*. One of its first advertisements, in the December 1983 *Computer and Video Games*, was not for a game at all. It was a pitch to programmers: "Would you like to retain your copyright and licence us to market your game?"

## Out of Bug-Byte

Three people left Bug-Byte to found it: Smith, and Alan and Soo Maton. *CRASH*'s first issue, in February 1984, reported that "Alan and Soo Maton left leading software house Bug-Byte to form Software Projects together with Matthew". Smith was still too young to own shares legally, the magazine said, and would receive them "on his eighteenth birthday, this February". *Personal Computer Games* described Alan Maton's career at Bug-Byte as a "rapid rise through the ranks... from despatch rider to marketing manager in a matter of months", and concluded that "it's not surprising that he now wants to run his own company". He was not Bug-Byte's managing director, as later accounts sometimes say.

The company worked from the Bear Brand Complex on Allerton Road in Woolton, Liverpool. *CRASH* named one more full-time member of staff: sixteen-year-old Chris Cannon, author of Bug-Byte's adventure *The Castle*, who looked after "the technical side of things".

## Taking Manic Miner

*Manic Miner* came too. *Personal Computer Games* ran the news under the headline "SMITH LEAVES BUG-BYTE": "The new company is off to a flying start since Matthew has taken the rights to Manic Miner with him, and the game is now available from Software Projects in a slightly modified form."

*Sinclair User* explained how. Bug-Byte "held a licence to produce *Manic Miner* but not the copyright". Its director Tony Baden told the magazine that Bug-Byte was not too hard hit: "It is still an excellent game and we have 50,000 copies of it left. We wish it the best of luck under any label." Baden gave a fuller account to *Big K* in July 1984. The contract was ambiguous because of a misplaced comma, he said, and Bug-Byte's lawyers advised that against a minor in open court "nobody has a chance", so it let the game go. He also said that Smith had been paid £50,000 for the game while Bug-Byte sold it. The [Bug-Byte](/vault/companies/bug-byte) entry has that side of the story.

## Jet Set Willy

The new company's first announcements were the *Manic Miner* sequel, *Jet Set Willy*, with "action in a cliff-top mansion bought with the loot from his mining activities", a Commodore 64 *Manic Miner*, *Crazy Balloons* for the 64, and three Spectrum games: *Push Off*, based on the arcade game *Pengo*; *McKensie*, a space adventure; and *Thrusta*, a shoot-'em-up.

*Jet Set Willy* took longer than planned, and went on sale in spring 1984 at £5.95. *CRASH* gave it 95%, and it went to number one in *Personal Computer Games*' chart. It also shipped with a bug that could wreck a game in progress, and Software Projects spent the summer first calling it deliberate and then giving magazines permission to print the POKEs that fixed it. The [Jet Set Willy](/vault/games/jet-set-willy) entry tells that story. Software Projects released a Commodore 64 version at the end of 1984, and an Amstrad CPC version followed in 1985. *Jet Set Willy II*, which *CRASH* credited to "Messrs Smith and Lawson", followed in 1985 at £6.95.

From late 1984 the catalogue turned to conversions of games from other machines and licensed characters. The Spectrum *[Lode Runner](/vault/games/lode-runner)* had 150 screens, 75 on each side of the tape. *BC's Quest for Tyres* was based on the newspaper cartoon character B.C., and *CRASH* ran a competition asking readers who had created him.

## Dragon's Lair

The biggest licence was *Dragon's Lair*, Don Bluth's laserdisc arcade game. *Amiga Computing* later summed up the original: "The gameplay wasn't up to much, but it was worth your while just to see the Disneyesque cartoon plot unfold." In the summer of 1986 *CRASH* sent a reporter to Liverpool to see Software Projects' conversion. Interest ran high, the magazine recalled, "partly because no-one could see how Coleco's complex, interactive game would come out on a home micro".

The answer, written by Paul Hodgson and Andy Walker and sold at £9.95, was nine single-screen scenes of Dirk the Daring on his way to rescue Princess Daphne from the dragon Singe: a falling disc, flaming ropes, a phantom knight on a checkerboard. Each scene loaded from cassette in turn, about 30 seconds apiece, and a player who ran out of lives had to rewind the tape and start again from the first. *CRASH*'s reviewers liked the large, smoothly animated characters, but found the graphics "hardly video disk quality" and the game "frustrating without being addictive". It scored 54%. Looking back in 1988, *CRASH* said the cartoon graphics were "largely regarded as the only really interesting element of the arcade machine", and without them the game "let it down dismally on the Spectrum". The sequel, *Escape from Singe's Castle*, did better, with 83% in April 1987.

## Later years

By the beginning of 1988 *ACE* could list Software Projects among the smaller independent publishers in trouble: it had "cut their releases down to the bone". It was still trading two years later. In 1990 it released *Manic Miner* for the Amiga, with large scrolling sprites, new music and digitised speech. *ACE* approved: "It is nice to note that Software Projects have taken the plunge and given 16-bit gamers what they have asked for." Public sources do not record when the company stopped trading.
