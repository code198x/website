Founded by a sixteen-year-old, Argonaut Software spent two decades at 3D gaming's frontier. Jez San's company progressed from Commodore 64 vector graphics through Amiga filled polygons to console hardware co-design. The Super FX chip—developed with Nintendo—enabled Star Fox and proved British programmers could innovate at silicon level. Argonaut's closure in 2004 ended an era, but alumni spread across studios that would define modern gaming.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1982 by [Jez San](/vault/people/jez-san) (aged 16).
- **Location:** London, later Edgware.
- **Key achievement:** Super FX chip development.
- **Closed:** 2004.

## Nintendo partnership

San's demonstration of Game Boy 3D led to unprecedented collaboration. Argonaut engineers worked with Nintendo's hardware division to create the Super FX chip—a RISC processor embedded in cartridges. The relationship soured after Nintendo worked with other developers on Super FX titles, but the chip's impact was lasting.

## Argonaut in 1994, profiled

*Edge* devoted its February 1994 prescreen feature to the company, and it is the fullest
picture of Argonaut this library holds. The opening line is the business model in one
sentence:

> **Producing 3DO and PC CD-ROM games for Sony and Virgin, and Super FX games for American
> third parties — that's the Argonaut Software of 1994. And all this while still in bed with
> Nintendo.**

The magazine confirms the founding detail above: "**since establishing
Argonaut at the age of 16, Jez San has landed on his feet time and time again** — with his
company now one of the most prominent in the business".

It also punctures the offices. Anyone expecting the trappings of a company with a Nintendo
contract "would surely be" disappointed — *Edge* found a modest North London operation, and
named the people in it: **Carl Graham**, **Ian Crowther** on product support, graphic artist
**Marcus Punter** and musician **Gwynn** among them.

## The honest assessment

Argonaut's reputation rests on the technology. *Edge* was not persuaded, and the dissent
belongs here because it comes from the magazine least inclined to sneer at technical
ambition:

> **Apart from *Starglider* and its technically stunning sequel, it's difficult to find**
> [an explanation for] **the company's spectacular success. Some of the games that followed
> were poor.**

Two landmark games and a chip, across a dozen years, with a catalogue in between that the
period's most serious magazine called poor. That is a fairer summary than "two decades at 3D
gaming's frontier", and it does not diminish the Super FX.

## The Mario chip

The Super FX had a working name that stuck internally. *Edge*, describing the successor
silicon:

> The **SFX II**, running approximately **30% faster than the original Mario chip** — **the
> real name for the Super FX**.

"Mario chip" was the internal designation; "Super FX" was the marketing. A **RISC processor
sold inside a cartridge**, so that the console's capability shipped with the game rather than
the machine — which is why *Star Fox* could do something no other SNES cartridge could.

## How the Nintendo relationship ended

The entry says the partnership "soured after Nintendo worked with other developers on Super
FX titles". *Edge* shows the mechanism, and it is more specific than that:

> ***Starfox II* is currently being coded in-house** [at Nintendo], while ***FX Trax* is being
> handled by two temporary Argonaut defectors, under the** [guidance] **of the game god
> himself, Shigeru Miyamoto, in Nintendo's Kyoto headquarters.**

So by early 1994 Nintendo had taken the *Star Fox* sequel in-house, and was running another
Super FX title in Kyoto using Argonaut staff seconded there. The technology Argonaut
co-designed was being used without Argonaut — with Argonaut's own people, in Nintendo's
building. *Star Fox 2* was famously never released.

⚠ The *Star Fox* above is the **SNES original of 1993**, which Argonaut co-developed.
*[Star Fox 64](/vault/games/star-fox-64)* (1997) is Nintendo's own, with no Argonaut
involvement — the two are easily confused.

## The sequel that never came

A footnote with a familiar shape. In June 1992 a Hungarian magazine reported that **Jez San
had "finally given in"** and would produce ***Starglider 3*** that year, to be published by
Argonaut itself.

⚠ Single-source, and a rumour rather than an announcement. It never appeared. Recorded here
only because the *Starglider* pair is what *Edge* judged the company's real achievement, and
a third was still being asked for six years after the second.

Argonaut staff founded or joined:

## Closure

Harry Potter games provided late commercial success, but development contracts became unsustainable. Argonaut entered administration in 2004, ending 22 years of 3D innovation.

## Starglider, and who else touched it

*Starglider* (1986) is the release that made Argonaut's name, and two facts about its
publication connect it to the rest of this Vault.

It was published under **[Rainbird](/vault/companies/rainbird)** — which is to say under **Telecomsoft**, British
Telecom's software arm, the same group that ran [Firebird](/vault/companies/firebird)'s
£2.50 budget shelf. *CRASH* rated it **95%**, and **97% for the 128K version** (issue 36).

And Argonaut did not do the 8-bit conversions. *ACE*, August 1988, credits those to
[Realtime Games Software](/vault/companies/realtime-games) — "Realtime produced *3D
Starstrike*, *3D Starstrike II* and **the 8-bit conversions of *Starglider***" — before
Realtime went on to *Carrier Command*.

The same *ACE* piece notes what Telecomsoft was assembling: solid 3D was "a field that
Realtime pioneered and **BT are swiftly monopolising**". *Amiga Format*, in December 1989,
lists the cohort that field consisted of — Braben with *Virus*, Realtime with *Carrier
Command*, Mike Singleton with *Whirligig*, Paul Woakes with *Mercenary*, and "**Jez San's
company Argonaut did a sequel to *Starglider***".

Argonaut is usually told as a story that runs from a teenager's bedroom to Nintendo silicon.
The middle of it is less solitary than that: a small British vector-graphics scene, several
studios deep, with the phone company buying up most of it.
