Argonaut is the British company that taught a Nintendo console to draw in 3D. In 1993 the Super Nintendo's *Star Fox*, *Starwing* in Europe, ran on a graphics chip Argonaut had designed and sold inside the cartridge, so the extra power shipped with the game rather than the machine. Seven years earlier the company had been one teenager, Jez San, trading under a name, writing *[Starglider](/vault/games/starglider)* for [Rainbird](/vault/companies/rainbird). Argonaut went on to make *Croc* and to sell processor designs to the mobile-phone industry. It ran out of money in October 2004.

## Fast facts

- **Founder:** [Jez San](/vault/people/jez-san), who began trading as Argonaut in 1982, at sixteen.
- **Addresses:** Mill Hill, north London (1986); Colindale (1994); Edgware (1998).
- **Games:** *Skyline Attack* (1984–85, San's first), *Starglider* (1986), *Starglider 2* (1988), *Birds of Prey* (1992), 3D on the [Game Boy](/vault/systems/game-boy) (previewed as *Eclipse*, 1990), *[Star Fox](/vault/games/star-fox)* with Nintendo (1993), *Croc* (1997), PlayStation versions of the first two Harry Potter games (2001–02).
- **Hardware:** the [Super FX](/vault/techniques/super-fx-chip), known inside the company as the Mario chip; later the ARC processor cores, spun off as a separate company in 1998.
- **End:** administration, October 2004. San relaunched the name as a publisher in 2024.

## A teenager trading as Argonaut

*The One for ST Games* profiled San in December 1991. He got his first computer, a Tandy TRS-80 Model 1, at twelve and a half, and in 1981 was one of the first people to buy a BBC Micro. A Saturday job at Microage Electronics, "the first BBC Micro shop", led to writing books, games and manuals; *[Acorn User](/vault/magazines/acorn-user)* reviewed a disc-drive manual by "Foudan Katan and Jeremy San" in October 1983. A German magazine later put Argonaut's beginning in 1982, "in Jez San's bedroom", and *Edge* said he established it at sixteen.

His first commercial game was *Skyline Attack* for the [Commodore 64](/vault/systems/commodore-64), credited to Jeremy San, Fouad Katan and Andrew Glaister. San called it "a Defender clone with nice graphics, a bit like *Drop Zone* in some ways". Century Hutchinson published it at £7.95, along with his book on the [Sinclair QL](/vault/systems/sinclair-ql), *Quantum Theory*. Its selling point was a game of Snake to play while the main program loaded.

## Starglider

San's break came through British Telecom's games business, [Telecomsoft](/vault/companies/telecomsoft). He worked on the development system used to convert *[Elite](/vault/games/elite)* to the Commodore 64, and people at Rainbird suggested he write a game of his own. He began a 3D game on the Apple Macintosh, then moved it to the [Atari ST](/vault/systems/atari-st) and [Amiga](/vault/systems/commodore-amiga) when they appeared. That game was *Starglider*, a fast vector-graphics space combat game, published by Rainbird in 1986. The 8-bit versions came from [Realtime Games](/vault/companies/realtime-games), and *[CRASH](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine)* gave the Spectrum one 95%, and 97% on the 128K.

While he worked on it, Argonaut had a sideline. *Commodore Computing International* reported in November 1986 that Argonaut Software, of 129 The Broadway, Mill Hill, was the British distributor of NewTek's Digiview video digitiser for the Amiga, and estimated it had supplied 17% of all British Amiga owners with one in its first month. San, "Argonaut's MD", had sold his whole stock apart from the unit he was using for *Starglider*'s titles.

Until *Starglider*'s royalties arrived, San told *The One*, he "had been trading as Argonaut, even though he was effectively a one-man company". The money let him set up a proper company and hire teams. *Starglider 2* (1988) swapped the wireframes for solid, filled 3D and flew both above and below planet surfaces; *[ACE](/vault/magazines/ace-magazine)* rated it 927 out of 1000 on the ST and Amiga. The next project went less well. The flight simulator *Birds of Prey* took four years: "We just thought of doing a flight sim with *Starglider* technology. We kept making it bigger and more realistic... It was commercial suicide, and we're unlikely to do it again." By late 1991 Argonaut had 30 staff and was moving into 10,000 square feet of offices.

## 3D on a Game Boy

The route to Nintendo ran through its black-and-white handheld. In September 1990 *ACE* previewed *Eclipse*, a 3D space game for the Game Boy. "We wanted to program *Starglider* on the Gameboy," San said. "The Nintendo Gameboy and NES consoles weren't designed to run 3D games." *The One* called Argonaut the first team to get 3D running on the Game Boy.

*Retro Gamer* later described what followed. On the strength of the Game Boy game, two of Argonaut's programmers, Giles Goddard and Dylan Cuthbert, went to work at Nintendo's headquarters in Kyoto, where they developed the Mario chip, a co-processor built into the cartridge that let the [Super NES](/vault/systems/super-nintendo) draw polygons. *Edge* confirmed in 1994 that "Mario chip" was "the real name for the Super FX". The first game to use it was *[Star Fox](/vault/games/star-fox)*, made with [Shigeru Miyamoto](/vault/people/shigeru-miyamoto). Nintendo's development manual gives the Super FX a clock of 10.74 MHz, "six times as fast as the Super NES CPU". In 1993 *Super Play* reported that the chip and the game had both been designed for Nintendo by Argonaut.

By February 1994 *Edge* found Argonaut working on Super FX games for American publishers, a 3DO and PC shoot-'em-up, *Creature Shock*, for Virgin, and a game for Sony. Nintendo had taken the sequel, *Star Fox 2*, in-house, while *FX Trax* was being made in Kyoto under Miyamoto by "two temporary Argonaut defectors". A faster version of the chip, the SFX II, ran "approximately 30% faster than the original Mario chip". *Star Fox 2* was finished but not released at the time; Nintendo finally put it out in 2017, on the Super NES Classic Edition.

*Edge* was not convinced by everything in the catalogue. "Apart from *Starglider* and its technically stunning sequel," its profile said, "it's difficult to find" a reason for "the company's spectacular success. Some of the games that followed were poor."

San gave his side of the Nintendo years to *Edge* in 1998. The two companies had worked "very closely" for two or three years, and Nintendo "more or less said they would do as many games as we wanted to do with them", but Argonaut chose to stay independent rather than become, like Rare, partly owned by Nintendo. "Firstly it got us into hardware and I am eternally grateful to Nintendo for giving us that opportunity," he said. By his count *Star Fox* sold four million copies.

## Croc and ARC

Independence meant working for everyone. Argonaut wrote its own 3D programming library, BRender, which San said lost out once Microsoft bought a rival and built Direct3D on it. Its big success was *Croc* (1997), a 3D platform game published by Fox Interactive on the PlayStation and Saturn. San told *Edge* it had sold over a million copies, that Argonaut had done "even better with *Croc* than we ever did doing cartridge games", and that Sega's interest in the Saturn version led to Argonaut becoming "the first Dreamcast developer". San said Argonaut had shown an early version of *Croc* to Nintendo "about two years before *Mario 64* came out".

The hardware side went its own way. By 1998 the processor group was selling ARC (Argonaut RISC Core) designs "for use in mobile phones and other applications", and San told *Edge* the chip business and the games no longer had much in common. According to later public accounts it was separated from Argonaut and renamed ARC that year. The games company, Argonaut Games, went on to develop the PlayStation versions of *Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone* (2001) and *Harry Potter and the Chamber of Secrets* (2002) for Electronic Arts.

## Collapse

The end came quickly. In 2003 Vivendi Universal dropped Argonaut's action game *Malice*, and another game, *Orchid*, was cancelled. Tameem Antoniades, a co-founder of Argonaut's subsidiary Just Add Monsters, kept a diary for *Edge*. On 15 October 2004 Argonaut told them "they've run out of cash": its shares were suspended and half its staff let go. An administrator auctioned off or closed the subsidiaries; Argonaut Sheffield closed on 22 October with 50 more jobs lost, and on 1 November the rest of Argonaut's staff were let go. Just Add Monsters bought itself out with its founders' own money and San as a backing director, and carried on as Ninja Theory.

A sequel to Argonaut's first hit never appeared. In May 1992 *[The One](/vault/magazines/the-one)* reported that San had revealed that preliminary design work on *Starglider 3* had been done, and that it was "just a question of finding the right deal".

In August 2024 San and a co-chief executive, Gary Sheinwald, relaunched Argonaut Games as a small publisher, starting with a remaster of *Croc*. "I'm thrilled to see the Argonaut name back after twenty years away," San said. Sheinwald had been Rainbird's producer on *Starglider*: "I worked closely with Jez on the design and production of Starglider, Argonaut's first big hit back in 1986," he said, and the relaunch "brings me full circle".
