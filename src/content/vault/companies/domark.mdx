## Overview

Dominic Wheatley and Mark Strachan founded Domark — DO(minic) + MARK — in 1984,
and are remembered as licensing specialists: Bond, Star Wars, *Trivial Pursuit*,
*Hard Drivin'*. Quality ranged from good conversions to rushed tie-ins, and the
strategy built a substantial publisher.

But the licences came second. Domark's first release was an original game with a
five-figure treasure hunt attached, and understanding it explains the company
better than the catalogue does.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1984.

## Eureka!, and the £25,000

*Eureka!* (October 1984, £14.95) is one of the strangest products of the British
8-bit era, and Domark's whole approach is visible in it.

**The game.** Five complete adventures, each with its own arcade section, in "more
than 250K" — you fight dinosaurs, outwit Nero and his gladiators, join the Knights
of the Round Table, escape from Colditz, and finally arrive in 1984 to stop a
Caribbean villain holding the world to ransom.

**The author.** Devised by **Ian Livingstone** of *Fighting Fantasy*, who also
wrote the scripts and an accompanying colour-illustrated book of clues and symbols.

**The prize.** A **secret telephone number** was hidden at the end of the
adventure. The first person to find it and call it would be paid **£25,000**.
Domark's own spokesman estimated the puzzle "may take as long as **15 months** to
solve", and if nobody claimed it by the deadline the money was to be shared among
those who had come closest.

**The programmers.** *Commodore Computing International* put it plainly in October
1984: the game "was compiled by a group of **Hungarians programming in secrecy
behind the Iron Curtain**".

It worked. By February 1985 *Big K* reported **50,000 copies sold in Britain
alone**, 11,000 bound for America, a French edition with its own separate prize
fund, and a Hungarian edition in preparation. Domark was, the same report notes,
besieged by pleas for help from stuck players.

## The Andromeda pipeline

The Hungarian team was **Andromeda**, and the connection is worth following
because it runs straight into another entry in this Vault.

*Computer & Video Games*, in 1986, describes the working method for a later
Domark title: the "relatively unknown Andromeda team" had never seen the sport
they were programming, so **David Bishop** ferried videotapes, course details and
an actual golf club and balls out to them. Its summary of the studio's reputation:
"probably their most famous game to date came out of the collaboration between
Domark and Ian Livingstone."

Andromeda is the same Budapest operation that handled the Western rights to
**[*Tetris*](/vault/games/tetris)** — the chain of sub-licences that ended with
[Mirrorsoft](/vault/companies/mirrorsoft) holding European rights and everybody in
court. Two of the most consequential British software stories of the decade came
down the same wire from the same city, and the first of them was a Fighting
Fantasy author's treasure hunt.

## A View to a Kill

The Bond licence (1985) shows the model in its mature form, and it is more
structured than tie-ins of the period usually were: **three distinct games —
Paris, City Hall and The Mine — linked by a code awarded on completing each**, so
that the game is only finished when all three are. *Computer Gamer* found Paris
the weakest and the other two "good enough to stand on their own".

The American release advertised "the computerised version of the hit single *A
View To A Kill* by **Duran Duran and John Barry**" — a film licence, a music
licence and a games publisher in one box, in 1985.
