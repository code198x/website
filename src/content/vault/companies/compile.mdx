Compile was set up to sell office software, and its office software would not sell. What kept the young Hiroshima company alive was a conversion of Namco's *Galaxian* for the Apple II, which shifted a few hundred copies. Over the next fifteen years Compile made some of the fastest and hardest shoot-'em-ups on Japanese computers and consoles, then a falling-blob puzzle game, *Puyo Puyo*, that drew hundreds of players to tournaments in Japan and outlived the company. *Wireframe* noted the irony of its end in 2019: Compile "ended as it had begun – trying to sell business software that few particularly wanted to buy."

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** April 1982 in Hiroshima, as Programmers-3, Inc., by Masamitsu "Moo" Niitani; renamed Compile in the mid-1980s.
- **Shoot-'em-ups:** *Zanac* (1986), *Aleste* (1988), *The Guardian Legend* (1988), *Gunhed*/*Blazing Lazers* (1989), *M.U.S.H.A.* (1990), *GG Aleste* (1991), *Super Aleste*/*Space Megaforce* (1992).
- **Other series:** the *Madou Monogatari* role-playing games and the *Disc Station* disk magazine (1988–2000).
- **Biggest hit:** *[Puyo Puyo](/vault/games/puyo-puyo)* (1991), known in the West as *Dr. Robotnik's Mean Bean Machine*.
- **End:** pledged the *Puyo Puyo* rights to [Sega](/vault/companies/sega) in 1998; dissolved in December 2002; declared bankrupt in November 2003.

## Porting for Sega

Niitani, who had "dabbled in game development a bit in the past", kept Programmers-3 going with that *Galaxian* port while its business programs "flatly refused to budge", *Wireframe* recounts. The next work came from [Sega](/vault/companies/sega): converting its early-1980s arcade games, such as *Borderline*, *Safari Hunting* and *Hustle Chumy*, to its Japan-only SG-1000 console. That began a working relationship between the two companies that lasted into the 2000s. After the change of name to Compile, the studio spent a couple of years converting arcade games such as *Lode Runner* and *Choplifter* to the [MSX](/vault/systems/msx).

## Zanac and Aleste

Its own style arrived with *Zanac* (1986), a vertically scrolling shooter for the MSX designed by Niitani with the programmers Koji Teramoto and Takayuki Hirono. *Wireframe* found that despite the MSX's limits it "had the pace and difficulty of a proper arcade title", with an unusually complex power-up system. It also watched the player: "the more efficiently the player collected power-ups and slaughtered enemies, the more brutally the game would respond." The [difficulty design](/vault/design/difficulty-design) entry explains how that worked. The game was converted to the [NES](/vault/systems/nintendo-entertainment-system) a year after the MSX original.

*Aleste* followed in 1988, for the [Master System](/vault/systems/sega-master-system) and the MSX2; American Master System owners knew it as *Power Strike*. When it was finished, *Wireframe* explains, the game fitted on a single MSX2 floppy disk with room to spare, so Compile filled the space. The result was *Disc Station*, "a magazine-software hybrid" of games, demos and music that Compile published from 1988 to 2000. Later editions cost around $40, and Niitani said they still sold well. The *Madou Monogatari* role-playing series first appeared on it in 1989.

Compile also made shooters for other companies' labels. *Gunhed* (1989), released in the West as *Blazing Lazers*, was published by Hudson for the [PC Engine](/vault/systems/pc-engine). *Wireframe* called it "essentially an unofficial entry in the Aleste series", with "ten or so different kinds" of weapons. Its pick of the run was *Musha Aleste* (1990) for the [Mega Drive](/vault/systems/sega-mega-drive), sold in America as *M.U.S.H.A.*, which mixed mecha with medieval Japan: a giant tank disguised as a castle, a flying gunship "capped with a smiling Noh theatre mask", and a synth-metal score by Toshiaki Sakoda. There was more variety than the shooters suggest. *The Guardian Legend* (1988) crossed a *Zanac*-style shooter with a free-roaming adventure in the manner of *Zelda* and *Metroid*, and Compile developed pinball games and a *Ghostbusters* platform game for other publishers, sometimes without credit.

On the Super Famicom, *Super Aleste* (1992) came out in America as *Space Megaforce*. The American fanzine *Digital Press* argued in 1999 that it had been overlooked because it was on "a system not exactly known for killer 2D shooters", and called it "bar none, THE best 16-bit overhead shooter", with eight upgradeable weapons that the player could switch between modes at will.

## Puyo Puyo

In 1991 Compile took characters from *Madou Monogatari* and put them in a puzzle game. *Puyo Puyo*, which *Wireframe* credits to Niitani himself, first came out for the MSX2 and Nintendo's console. Pairs of coloured blobs fall into a well; join four of a colour and they burst. Sega offered to make an arcade version, and it was the two-player game that took off: clearing blobs sends a shower of blank "garbage" blobs on to the opponent's side. *Wireframe* called the competitive mode "a masterstroke", with "hundreds of players" turning out for live tournaments in Japan.

*Edge*, reviewing the Super Famicom version in 1994, reported that the Mega Drive game had stayed at the top of the Japanese Mega Drive charts for about six months after its release in December 1992, helped by a price about £20 below most Mega Drive titles. It thought *Puyo Puyo* "isn't quite the blatant Tetris rip-off that initial impressions suggest. In fact, it shares more similarities with Sega's Columns", and it liked the two-player game best: "a counter-shower of clear blobs rains down on the other player". Sega renamed the Mega Drive game *Dr. Robotnik's Mean Bean Machine* for the West and dressed it in *Sonic* characters.

The puzzle game changed the company. When *Edge* visited MileStone, a shooter studio founded by former Compile staff, in 2005, its head said that "many of us had joined Compile to develop [shooting games], but the company was only interested in Puyo Puyo, so we didn't really have the chance to fulfil our dream."

## Collapse

As the 1990s went on, *Wireframe* writes, several key staff left, and a bigger blow followed: Power Acty, a piece of business software given an expensive launch in 1998, failed to sell despite "a few cute faces from Puyo Puyo". Japanese Wikipedia describes it as groupware for internal communication at large companies, whose development costs ran out of control. In 1998 Compile was forced to restructure, and it pledged the *Puyo Puyo* rights to Sega in return for a loan. Sega Retro reports that Compile could go on using the licence until August 2002, with the option to buy the rights back if its finances recovered. It could not. Compile was dissolved in December 2002 and, according to Japanese Wikipedia, declared bankrupt by the Tokyo District Court in November 2003.

Among its last games, *Wireframe* noted, were *Zanac X Zanac* (2001), a PlayStation collection of the original *Zanac* and a new sequel, and its last commercial release, the Game Boy Advance puzzle game *Guru Logi Champ* (2001).

## After Compile

Sega kept *Puyo Puyo*, and its own studio, [Sonic Team](/vault/companies/sonic-team), took over the series. Sega Retro records that the rights to *Madou Monogatari* went to a company called Aiky in 2003 and that D4 Enterprise bought the rest of Compile's back catalogue in 2005. Former Compile staff founded MileStone in 2003 to make the shooters they had wanted to make; *Edge* found it working on the arcade shooter *Chaos Field*.

Niitani ran Compile Heart, a subsidiary of the publisher Idea Factory, from 2006 until he retired in 2012. In 2016 he came back with a new company, Compile Maru, and a falling-object puzzle game, *Nyoki Nyoki*, which he hoped would be "the third generation" of puzzle games after *[Tetris](/vault/games/tetris)* and *Puyo Puyo*.
