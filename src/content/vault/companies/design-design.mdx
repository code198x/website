Most software houses of the early 1980s wrote games on the machine they were selling to, or bought an expensive development system. The programmers of Design Design built their own. In July 1984 Graham Stafford and Simon Brattel carried a home-made computer called Basil into the offices of *[CRASH](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine)* in a battered suitcase, wired it up and showed the reviewers *Dark Star*, a space game in 3D vector graphics. *CRASH* later called it "just about the fastest 3-D vector graphics game ever written". Until that summer the company had been called Crystal Computing, and it had come out of a hall of residence at Manchester University.

## Crystal Computing

Crystal began by selling programs by mail order. Its advertisements in *Your Computer* at the end of 1982 offered machine-code games for the [ZX81](/vault/systems/sinclair-zx81), including the trading game *Merchant of Venus*, and Zeus, an assembler for the 48K [ZX Spectrum](/vault/systems/sinclair-zx-spectrum) "developed from the tried and tested Nascom package". An assembler turns a program written in a processor's mnemonics into the machine code it runs. *Sinclair User* reviewed Zeus in June 1983 and called it "the big daddy of all assembler programs", "very simple to use for the beginner"; it cost £8.95, with a matching monitor and disassembler at the same price.

According to Stafford, Crystal was started by Chris Clarke, with Stafford as his partner, in two rooms of Owens Park hall of residence at Manchester University. Stafford looked after the stock and the programming, Clarke the administration. Clarke later left for Artic Computing, and went on to share the credit for the football game *Match Day* with [Jon Ritman](/vault/people/jon-ritman).

Crystal's best-known game was *Halls of the Things*, a 48K Spectrum maze game from the end of 1983, credited in its advertisements to Neil Mottershead, Simon Brattel and Martin Horsley. Brattel and Mottershead also wrote the Defender-style *Invasion of the Body Snatchas!*, and Crystal put together the Spectrum game of *The Warlock of Firetop Mountain* for Puffin. By spring 1984 Crystal's range also included Horsley's *Rommel's Revenge*, Stafford's *The Dungeon Master*, Martin Buller's *It's the Wooluf!*, *Tube Way Army* for the Dragon 32 by Rainbow Software, and a Commodore 64 assembler, Zeus 64. The adverts named the programmers of every game, and gave the company as "the trading name of Chandrex Ltd".

## A new name

*CRASH*'s September 1984 issue announced that Crystal had been "reborn under a new name". Stafford told the magazine the change was about image: "We design programs rather than sitting around in some isolated tower writing them. But the word Design on its own, or Design Software doesn't really sound right. The hard letter D at the front is good, but you should repeat it for effect, rather like Duran Duran." Brattel's own account, on his website, is that Design Design was a name he had invented in 1976 "to put on my early electronic designs", mostly audio equipment.

The core of the company was now four people: Stafford, who programmed mainly for the [Commodore 64](/vault/systems/commodore-64); Brattel and Mottershead, who worked on the Spectrum; and David Llewellyn, the administrator. They lived and worked in a large terraced house near Manchester. "We get up when we want — we program when we want — we go to the pub when we want," Stafford said. New games went for testing to a school where a brother of one of the team taught, where 60 pupils had Spectrums, and the team watched them play. By 1986 the advertised address was 125 Smedley Road, Cheetham Hill, Manchester.

Stafford also argued for crediting the programmer: "people like a Simon Brattel program — they go and buy a Simon Brattel game — it's not the company they're buying." The adverts did just that. Brattel's name appears on *Dark Star* and *Forbidden Planet*, and *CRASH*'s reviewer described *Forbidden Planet*'s sound as "un-Brattelian".

## Lurch, the Beast and Basil

Brattel and Stafford described themselves as electronic engineers who did not trust anyone else's hardware. *Halls* and *Warlock* had been written on a development system Brattel designed, the Beast, and when it became unreliable he built Basil. Basil had half a megabyte of RAM, a Z80 running at 7MHz and floppy disks, and ran their own operating system and the Zeus assembler, all "tailor-made for writing software for the Spectrum". They said it cost about £500 without its disks, and that for writing Z80 programs it outperformed the Sage computers that [Imagine](/vault/companies/imagine-software) had spent thousands on. "Imagine had 20 Sages and had placed an order for 34 more!" On his website Brattel remembers the machine he used for *Halls* in 1983 as Lurch, and that Basil's editor could soft-scroll text windows in hardware.

## The games

*Dark Star*, by Brattel, was reviewed in *CRASH*'s December 1984 issue at 89% and made a CRASH Smash. It had three screens: open space against alien saucers, a 3D hyperspace tunnel, and a city of towers on a planet's surface. The cassette also held a hidden program, *Spectacle*, locked by a password. After letters and phone calls from readers begging for it, *CRASH*'s March 1985 issue printed it with a phone call from "Big Simon Brattel": the words came from the chorus of Supertramp's "Just Another Nervous Wreck", and the clues had been in the game's high-score tables all along.

The engine behind *Dark Star* went into other games. *Tank Busters* (1985), a [Battlezone](/vault/games/battlezone)-style tank game for the [Amstrad CPC](/vault/systems/amstrad-cpc), was advertised as using "the advanced graphics techniques developed for the game Dark Star", and *Amtix* called it "the Brattel reinterpretation" of *Battlezone*. *Forbidden Planet* (1986), again by Brattel, was another high-speed vector game. *CRASH* gave it 86% in March 1986, but wondered whether it was different enough from *Dark Star* to be Smashed, and did not Smash it.

Stafford's games went in other directions. *On the Run* (1985), a maze game with large graphics by Stuart Ruecroft, scored 91% in *CRASH*. *2112 AD* (1986), whose title came from an old rock album, was an arcade adventure in which the player guides a robot dog called Poddy, named after the dog of *CRASH*'s Jeremy Spencer. It scored 83%. *Nexor* (1986), an isometric game with graphics by David Fish in which the last survivor of an alien attack has to recover the parts of a super-weapon, scored 81%.

Design Design also wrote about itself in *CRASH*. In autumn 1985 the magazine ran a four-part "autoprofile" written by the team, which introduced a 6809 programmer called Wook, who kept a 36-hour day, and quoted Brattel: "I look upon Design-Design as a viable commune."

## Working Backwards

In spring 1986 Design Design sold a compilation of four games on a single Amstrad disk for £14.95: *Dark Star*, *Tank Busters*, *On the Run* and *Halls of the Things*. The adverts presented it as a record, "the first computer concept album, only available on disc", which "includes the hit singles", with *Halls of the Things* as "an extended re-mix version (only available on this disc)". It was stocked by Woolworth, WH Smith and Comet. *Amstrad Action* called *Halls* "previously unreleased", which was true only of the Amstrad: the Spectrum original had been out since 1983.

## Programming for others

From late 1986 Design Design worked for other publishers. For Piranha, Macmillan's games label, it wrote *Nosferatu*, an isometric game of the vampire film that *CRASH* Smashed, and the *2000 AD* licence *Rogue Trooper*, which scored 79%. For [Domark](/vault/companies/domark) it programmed *Kat Trap*, the winning design from a *CRASH* and Domark competition, which scored 84%. In 1987 the team programmed Domark's game of the James Bond film *The Living Daylights*, released under the name Walking Circles, "who are really Design Design in new guise", as *CRASH* put it.

By November 1987 *CRASH* was calling Graham Stafford and David Llewellyn the two halves of Design Design. At the end of 1988 *ACE* reported that "the Walking Circles (ex Design Design) crew" were finishing Domark's *Spitting Image* game. In January 1989 *CRASH* reported that [Zeppelin Games](/vault/companies/zeppelin-games) would re-release old Design Design games, including *Nexor*, *Forbidden Planet* and *2112 AD*, at budget price, and in August 1991 *Nexor* was given away as a full game on a *CRASH* cover tape.

## Later

Brattel kept the Design Design name for his own electronics work, and later ran a display company, Maelor Display Systems, with a factory in Wrexham. On the Design Design website he has published the Z80 source code of many of the old games, reconstructed where necessary from disassemblies and fragments, and a free version of Zeus for the PC with an editor and emulator. He also released a version of *Halls of the Things* for the [ZX Spectrum Next](/vault/systems/zx-spectrum-next) in 2020, and a version of Zeus is included with the Next's system software.
