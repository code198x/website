Design Design Software, of **125 Smedley Road, Cheetham Hill, Manchester M8 7RS**, sat in a
category British software had few examples of: a house whose games carried an obvious
**authorial personality**, and whose reviewers wrote about the authors rather than the
publisher.

*Amtix*, reviewing *Forbidden Planet* in May 1986, credits **Simon Brattel** by name in the
review header and then makes a running joke of him inside the review copy — "the notorious
Brattel[ian] Objector" — which is not how the same magazine wrote about Ocean or US Gold.

## Working Backwards

The thing worth recording about Design Design is a piece of packaging, and it arrived three
years before the compilation became an industry staple.

***Working Backwards*** (1986, £14.95 on disk) collected *Dark Star*, *Tank Busters*, *On
the Run* and *Halls of the Things*. Its advertising billed it as "**the first [computer
game] concept album**" and listed its contents as "the hits", in the format of a record
sleeve:

> **WORKING BACKWARDS** — includes the hits **DARK STAR ● TANK BUSTERS ● ON THE RUN ●
> HALLS OF THE THINGS** … from **Woolworth**, Comet and all leading software stockists.

*Amstrad Action* reviewed it in April 1986 and flagged the detail that makes the joke land:
one of the four, *Halls of the Things*, was "**the previously unreleased**" title. A
greatest-hits album containing a track that had never been a hit because it had never come
out.

Set that beside [Elite](/vault/companies/elite-systems), whom *ACE* credits with inventing
the "blockbusting compilation" — Elite's model was a back catalogue of licensed hits
repackaged for volume. Design Design's is the same commercial move made as a **gag about
the record industry**, complete with a B-side.

## Why it mattered

Design Design belongs in the Vault for the same reason [Alligata](/vault/companies/alligata)
does and for the opposite reason. Alligata is what the middle of the market looked like when
it had nothing distinctive. Design Design is what the middle of the market looked like when
it had **a voice**.

Ninety-one, eighty-six, eighty-one is a respectable record and not a famous one. What
survives is the tone — the named programmer, the in-jokes in the review copy, the concept
album, the Woolworth shelf — and the tone is the evidence that in 1986 a small Manchester
publisher could behave like a band and have the press play along.
