*Amiga Power*'s review headers name Kompart as publisher and, underneath, the people who made the games: **Blue Byte**, **Max Design**, Floating Point. Kompart is the British end of a German-speaking development scene that was, by the mid-nineties, producing some of the most durable strategy games anywhere.

## Fast facts

- **Press evidence in this library:** *Amiga Power*, **1993–1994**, at around **£30**.
- **Published in the UK:** *Burntime* (Max Design, Austria, £30), *Naughty Ones*, and Blue Byte's Amiga titles.
- **Blue Byte's standing at the time:** *Amiga Format* noted its games had been **top of the German software charts** for an extended run.

## The German strategy tradition

Blue Byte and Max Design were doing something the British industry had largely stopped doing: **long, systemic games about running things**.

Blue Byte's *Battle Isle* and then *The Settlers* built worlds where the interest is in supply chains and production loops — you place a woodcutter, which needs a path, which needs a carrier, and watch an economy assemble itself. Max Design's *Burntime* is post-apocalyptic survival with resource management underneath; the same studio went on to make the *Anno* series, which is still being published today, thirty years later.

That lineage is the point. **The German strategy game did not have a boom-and-bust**; it built slowly, kept its audience, and is now one of the few continuous threads running from the Amiga to the present. *Anno* and *The Settlers* are direct descendants of games Kompart was putting into British shops for £30.

## Reading the header carefully

The Kompart entries are also a good demonstration of why *Amiga Power*'s two-line header matters so much to this library.

"Publisher: Kompart. Authors: Blue Byte." Without the second line, the record shows a small British company publishing Amiga games in 1993 and nothing else — no German scene, no Max Design, no thread forward to *Anno*. **The developer credit is what makes the history traceable**, and it survives only because a magazine chose to print it.

Most publisher-only records lose exactly this. It is the same problem the [Impressions](/vault/companies/impressions) entry runs into from the other direction, where following a person's name instead of a company's is the only way through.

## Why it mattered

Kompart is **the German-language development scene reaching Britain**, and a strategy tradition that the British and American industries never matched and never displaced.

It also completes a picture the Vault has been assembling one publisher at a time: by 1993 the Amiga's software was arriving in Britain from France, Germany, Austria and Finland, published by small British outfits whose names nobody remembers, for a machine Britain had led five years earlier.

## Wider than German and Austrian

The subtitle above says German and Austrian, and the period record extends it further east.

*Amiga Computing*, December 1994:

> Publishers **Kompart** are planning quite a few releases for the Amiga, with games from
> developers such as **Croatian Black Legend, NEO, Max Design and Arcane**.

**Croatian.** In 1994 — with the war still running — a small British publisher was bringing
Croatian Amiga development into UK shops alongside Austrian and British studios. Black Legend's
*Embryo* and *Spherical Worlds* are listed in the same piece, the latter described as working
"similar to that used in *Alien Breed*".

That widens the role. Kompart was not just the German connection; it was a route into
Britain for continental European development generally, at a moment when the
British industry's own attention had moved to consoles.

## Voluntary liquidation

*Amiga Computing*, April 1996, in a survey of the wreckage after Commodore:

> The future of **Kompart**, another company which was prevalent in the Amiga games scene,
> remains uncertain. Reports are coming through that **Kompart, publisher of *Football Glory*
> and *Tactical Manager*, have hit problems and have fallen into voluntary liquidation**. The
> company handled numerous firms such as **Arcane, and Max Design**.

It went down in the same paragraph as the rest of the British Amiga trade: **SDL**, the
distributor that owned the Silica retail chain, into an Administration Order; **Rasputin**,
publisher of *Base Jumpers*, gone with its staff absorbed by Soundscape Multimedia.

An importer's business is the platform it imports for. When the Amiga went, the route went
with it, and the developers on the far end of it lost their way into Britain.
