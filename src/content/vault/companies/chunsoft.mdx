Chunsoft began as a programming contest prize and a student start-up. Koichi Nakamura won a place in a game-programming contest run by the publisher [Enix](/vault/companies/enix) with a puzzle game, *Door Door*, and in April 1984, aged 19, set up his own company with four other university students. Two years later Chunsoft programmed *[Dragon Quest](/vault/games/dragon-quest)*, and it built the first five games of the series. It then made two kinds of game that were very much its own: the Mystery Dungeon games, which made the computer roguelike popular on Japanese consoles, and "sound novels", stories told in text, pictures and sound in which the reader's choices change what happens.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 9 April 1984, Tokyo.
- **Founder:** Koichi Nakamura, with four fellow students.
- **Developed:** *Dragon Quest* I to V for Enix (1986–1992).
- **Own series:** Mystery Dungeon (from *Torneko's Great Adventure*, 1993); sound novels (from *Otogirisō*, 1992).
- **Later:** *[Pokémon Mystery Dungeon](/vault/games/pokemon-mystery-dungeon)* (2005); *999: Nine Hours, Nine Persons, Nine Doors* (in America, 2010).
- **Ended:** merged with Spike in April 2012 to form Spike Chunsoft.

## Dragon Quest

Nakamura met [Yuji Horii](/vault/people/yuji-horii), another winner of the Enix contest, when the two worked on the Famicom version of Horii's detective game *The Portopia Serial Murder Case*. *Edge*'s history of the series says it was Nakamura who proposed to Enix that he would like to make a role-playing game. Horii designed and wrote *Dragon Quest*, Akira Toriyama drew it, Koichi Sugiyama wrote its music, and Chunsoft programmed it; the company went on to develop the first five games, ending with *Dragon Quest V* on the Super Famicom in 1992. When *Nintendo Power* reported a panel of Japanese designers at the 1997 Tokyo Game Show, it introduced Nakamura as "programmer of Dragon Quest".

## Mystery Dungeon

Chunsoft's first big game of its own borrowed a *Dragon Quest* character. *Torneko's Great Adventure* (*Torneko no Daibōken*, 1993) gave the series' chubby merchant Torneko a dungeon of his own, and *Super Play* reported it as "out now" on the Super Famicom in December 1993. It was a [roguelike](/vault/genres/roguelike), built on the rules of the old computer game *Rogue*, and *Super Play* said two years later that it had been "a big hit in Japan". *Retro Gamer* later called Chunsoft's series "massively popular, providing an easy introduction to the sub-genre".

The sequel, *Fushigi no Dungeon 2: Fūrai no Shiren* (1995), gave the series a hero of Chunsoft's own, and at a Tokyo show that autumn *Super Play* noticed a giant model of him, in "a rather fetching upturned-fruit-bowl hat", beside Chunsoft's stand. In a 2006 readers' poll of the hundred best games, reprinted in *Edge*, it came 48th, *Torneko* 78th, and a later *Mysterious Dungeon* game of 2000 86th. Other companies licensed the idea. *Edge* reported in 1997 that Square's *Chocobo's Mystery Dungeon* was "based on Chunsoft's popular Siren Mystery Dungeon", with the *Final Fantasy* bird in the lead. In 2005 Chunsoft made *[Pokémon Mystery Dungeon](/vault/games/pokemon-mystery-dungeon)* for Nintendo, which in *Retro Gamer*'s words gave the old sub-genre "a cute new spin". *Shiren* itself was ported from the Super Famicom to the Nintendo DS in 2006, with a mechanic that let players ask others for help over the wireless link.

## Sound novels

Chunsoft's other line came out of the adventure game. *Super Play* explained that *Otogirisō*, "the first sound novel from Chun Soft, premiered on the Super Famicom in March 1992"; a sound novel, the magazine said, was basically an adventure, an "interactive book". For the second, *Kamaitachi no Yoru* (1994), Chunsoft commissioned the crime novelist Takemaru Abiko. Its story, as *Super Play* described it, traps thirteen people snowed in at a small hotel with a murderer, and the reader has to find the killer before becoming the next victim.

The sound novels mattered a great deal in Japan. In the same 2006 poll, *Kamaitachi no Yoru* came 90th, and Chunsoft's Sega Saturn adventure *Machi* (1998) came fifth, behind only *Final Fantasy X*, *Final Fantasy VII*, *Dragon Quest III* and *Dragon Quest VIII*.

English-speaking players met Chunsoft's storytelling in *999: Nine Hours, Nine Persons, Nine Doors* on the Nintendo DS, published in America by Aksys Games in 2010. Nine strangers wake on a ship and have nine hours to escape through numbered doors. *Edge* called it "more interactive novel than full-blown adventure title", liked its locked-room puzzles and the way its choices "have tangible impact" on the story, but found the interactions "spaced shockingly far apart", and gave it 6 out of 10.

## Spike Chunsoft

Chunsoft also tried other things. In 2005 it sold its online GameCube role-playing game *Homeland* only through its own website, a choice *Edge* described as "a constrictive caveat". On 1 April 2012 Chunsoft merged with another Tokyo developer, Spike, and the combined company took the name Spike Chunsoft. Under that name it went on making story games, among them *AI: The Somnium Files* (2019), a detective adventure. In 2019 Nakamura and other early staff gathered for the company's 35th anniversary, where one of them, Kazuya Asano, put its early games down to a studio where "there was no one to stop us".
