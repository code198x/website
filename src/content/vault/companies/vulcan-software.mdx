## Overview

Vulcan published Amiga games after the Amiga had commercially ended. *Valhalla and the Lord of Infinity* (£29.99, A500 to A1200, by **Lisa Tunnah and Paul Carrington**) came out in 1994, and Vulcan kept going for years afterwards, selling to a market that everyone else had written off.

## Fast facts

- **Seen in the press:** *Amiga Power*, **1994**, at **£29.99** — reviewed, and not kindly.
- **Known for:** the *Valhalla* isometric adventure series.
- **Credited authors:** Lisa Tunnah and Paul Carrington.
- **Distinctive:** Amiga-only, by choice, well past the point where that was a business decision anyone else would have made.

## Reviewed badly, sold anyway

*Amiga Power* was blunt about *Valhalla*: barren, empty, nothing living on screen, and after twenty minutes the only thing you would notice was how often you died. The magazine had a house style of enthusiastic cruelty, but the verdict was not unusual for Vulcan.

The games sold regardless, and the reason is structural. By 1994 the Amiga press was shrinking, the shops had cleared their Amiga shelves, and a publisher reached buyers **directly** — mail order, magazine adverts, cover disks, word of mouth in user groups. In that market a review is one voice among several rather than the gatekeeper it had been in 1987, and an audience with almost nothing new to buy is a forgiving one.

## What a platform's long tail looks like

Vulcan is the entry for what happens to a computer **after** its industry leaves, and the shape recurs for every machine in this Vault.

The mass market goes first, then the publishers, then the shops, then the magazines. What remains is a smaller, older, more committed group of owners who have not stopped wanting new software — and a few small companies willing to serve them at a scale that would have been failure a few years earlier.

The economics change completely. A game that would have needed fifty thousand sales in 1990 needs a few thousand in 1996; the budget shrinks to match; the ambition shrinks with it. Games from a platform's long tail often look thin next to its peak, and comparing them directly misses that they were made for a different market under different arithmetic.

This is also where the line between commercial software and community software blurs. Vulcan's operation is not far from [licenseware](/vault/distribution/licenseware) and the [public domain](/vault/distribution/public-domain) scene that kept the Amiga alive in parallel.

## Why it mattered

Vulcan is the entry for **loyalty as a business model**, and a useful corrective to tidy endings. Histories tend to close a platform with the manufacturer's bankruptcy — Commodore, 1994 — but the Amiga did not stop then. People kept using them, and a handful of companies kept writing for them, for another decade.

It also puts two names into the record. Lisa Tunnah and Paul Carrington are credited in a magazine header for a game reviewed badly and remembered fondly, and that credit is the only reason either name is recoverable at all.
