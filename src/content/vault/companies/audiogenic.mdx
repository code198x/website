Most British publishers of the eighties chased the same things: arcade licences, platform games, whatever had done well last quarter. Audiogenic found a different answer — **sports simulations for people who followed the sport** — and stayed with it long after most of its contemporaries had folded.

## Fast facts

- **Press evidence in this library:** reviewed from **1985 to 1995** across *[CRASH](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine)*, *[ZZAP!64](/vault/magazines/zzap-64)*, *[Amstrad Action](/vault/magazines/amstrad-action)* and *[Amiga Power](/vault/magazines/amiga-power)*, at **£12.95–£19.99** — one of the longest continuous runs in this Vault's company entries.
- **Known for:** *Graham Gooch World Class Cricket*, *Test Cricket*, *Wembley Rugby League*, *Emlyn Hughes International Soccer*, *FA Premier League*, *Loopz*, *Impact*.

## Cricket

The cricket games are what Audiogenic is remembered for, and they are a genuinely odd thing to find in a market aimed at teenagers.

Cricket is a poor fit for a computer game of this era on almost every axis. It is slow. A match lasts days. Its rules are unusually intricate, and the interesting decisions are field placings and bowling changes, not anything requiring reflexes. None of that suits a machine sold on arcade action.

Audiogenic's answer was to **take the sport seriously** — model the field, the bowling, the declarations — and accept a smaller audience that cared. *Graham Gooch World Class Cricket* on the Amiga is the high point, and it has the distinction of still being played and patched by enthusiasts decades later, which almost no games from these publishers can claim.

## The long tail

The press evidence shows something the catalogue alone would not: Audiogenic is reviewed across **a full decade**, from an Amstrad *Test Cricket* in 1986 to Amiga football in the mid-nineties. Most publishers in this Vault occupy three or four years and then stop.

Surviving that long meant crossing from 8-bit to 16-bit, which killed a great many British software houses — the costs rose, the teams had to grow, and a catalogue built on £7.95 cassettes did not transfer cleanly. A niche turns out to have been useful protection: sports audiences renew every season, and a cricket game does not compete with an arcade conversion for the same buyer.

## Why it mattered

Audiogenic is the case for **specialisation** in a market that mostly rewarded chasing trends. It shows that a British publisher could survive the industry's most dangerous transition by serving an audience nobody else wanted, not by getting bigger.

It is also a corrective to the idea that 8-bit British software was uniformly aimed at children. Someone was buying detailed cricket simulations in numbers sufficient to sustain a company for fifteen years.
