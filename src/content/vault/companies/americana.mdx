Americana sold American games in Britain at budget prices, several years after they had come out at home. In *ZZAP!64*'s July 1986 issue it took **three budget Sizzlers** — the magazine's mark for a game worth buying — in a single issue.

## Fast facts

- **Press evidence in this library:** *ZZAP!64*, **1986–1987**, budget prices.
- **Published:** *HES Games*, *Sentinel*, *Slamball*, *Necromancer*, *Breakdance*, *Space Warriors*, *New York City*, *Beer Belly Burt's Brew Biz*.
- **The pattern:** almost every title is an **American game given a cheap new lease of life** in Britain, in *ZZAP!*'s own phrase.

## What three Sizzlers in one issue means

*ZZAP!64* described them as they went: *HES Games*, "an older sports action compilation from AMERICANA given a cheap new lease of life"; *Sentinel*, "another Sizzler with this cheap re-release of the 'Star Raider' type game"; *Slamball*, "Low Cost Sizzler number 3, another from AMERICANA".

Sit with what that implies. In July 1986 the best value on the Commodore 64 in Britain was **three old American games at budget price**, competing against everything new and full-price in the same issue.

Two things caused it. The Commodore 64 arrived in America first and had a two- or three-year head start, so there was already a deep catalogue of proven games that British buyers had never seen. And a game that has finished earning at home can be sold abroad for almost nothing, because the development is long since paid for.

That combination made **the American back catalogue a genuine competitor to new British software** — not a bargain bin, but the thing that took the awards.

## The import routes together

Americana is the third of three ways foreign software reached British shops, and the differences are the interesting part.

[Ariolasoft](/vault/companies/ariolasoft) held territory rights to current American titles and sold them at **full price**, £9.95 and £14.95, as new releases. [Alternative](/vault/companies/alternative-software) took Spanish games at **£2.99** as budget stock. Americana took older American games at **budget** and had them beat the new releases on merit.

Same underlying fact each time — a catalogue that has earned out at home is nearly free to sell abroad — arriving at three different points on the shelf. Anyone reading British magazines of the period is looking at a market being continuously topped up from outside it, usually without saying so.

## Why it mattered

Americana is **the second life of a catalogue**, and a corrective to the idea that budget meant bad. The cheapest games in the shop were sometimes the best ones, because age and price had nothing to do with quality — only with how long the money had already been made.

It also quietly marks how young the market was. In 1986 a three-year-old game could still be new to most of the people buying it.
