## Overview

Alternative sold budget games from Yorkshire at £1.99 and £2.99 in the late eighties, went on selling them through the Amiga years, and **never stopped**. It is trading now, publishing licensed and family titles on current consoles, which makes it the longest continuous run of any company in this Vault's British section.

## Fast facts

- **Press evidence in this library:** *CRASH*, *ZZAP!64* and *Amiga Power*, **1988–1994**, at **£1.99–£7.99**.
- **Published:** *Meganova: The Weapon*, *'Allo 'Allo*, and a long list of budget and licensed titles.
- **Distinctive habit:** licensing continental European games for the British budget market.

## Spanish games at £2.99

*ZZAP!64* reviewed *Meganova: The Weapon* at £2.99 and opened with a joke about the inlay: "it's dodgy Spanish translation time again". The game is **Dinamic's**, from Madrid, and it scored 67% — an ordinary game, ordinarily reviewed, at a budget price.

The joke is more interesting than the review. A Spanish game reaching a British magazine in 1988 was normal enough to be teased, not remarked upon, and the only visible trace of its origin was a translated inlay that had not been checked by a native speaker.

This is the same route [Ariolasoft](/vault/companies/ariolasoft) used for Dinamic's *Camelot Warriors* two years earlier, at full price. By 1988 it had moved down-market: **continental catalogues were now cheap enough to be budget stock**, which is what happens to any back catalogue once it has finished earning at home.

## Why it survived

Nearly every publisher in this Vault died in one of two ways — bought during a boom, or bankrupted when the market they served disappeared. Alternative did neither, and the reason looks like deliberate modesty.

It never chased a generation change. It did not bet a company on the 16-bit transition, on CD, or on a mascot. It sold **inexpensive games to buyers who wanted inexpensive games**, a demand that has never once gone away, and it kept the operation small enough that a bad year was survivable.

That is an unglamorous strategy and it beat almost every glamorous one. [Ocean](/vault/companies/ocean-software), [US Gold](/vault/companies/us-gold), [Imagine](/vault/companies/imagine-software), [Psygnosis](/vault/companies/psygnosis) — all bigger, all better remembered, none of them still trading under their own name.

## The floor

Three Alternative titles appear in *CRASH*'s issue 48 index, and one of them is a useful
data point about how low the budget shelf could go.

| Game | Score | Issue |
|---|---|---|
| *Star Wreck* | **73%** | 44 |
| *Henry's Hoard* | **65%** | 27 |
| ***Howzat*** | **11%** | 37 |

**Eleven per cent.** That is among the lowest ratings *CRASH* issued to anything, and it is
worth recording next to the headline fact — that this is the longest unbroken run of any
British publisher here.

The two facts are not in tension. A label that survives four decades does so by never
betting the company on any one release, which means shipping a great deal that nobody
defends. Longevity in this business has never correlated with quality, and Alternative is
the clearest available proof.

## Why it mattered

Alternative is **continuity**, and it complicates the standard story of the British industry as a boom that ended. Something did end: the bedroom-coder economics, the £1.99 impulse cassette, the magazines. But a small Yorkshire publisher that started in 1985 has been shipping games without a break ever since, and that fact sits oddly against every account of the era as a lost golden age.

It is also a useful corrective to survivorship bias, running the other way. The companies history remembers are mostly the ones that failed spectacularly.
