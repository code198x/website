A Tiger handheld was easy to recognise: a flat plastic case, a printed background, the name of a film or arcade hit on the box, and a liquid-crystal screen on which the characters jumped between fixed positions. Tiger Electronics, a toy company from the suburbs of Chicago, made them for years, sold cheaply in toy shops rather than games shops. It also published Atari 2600 cartridges in the early 1980s, built a handheld console with a touch screen seven years before the Nintendo DS, and sold Furby, the toy craze of Christmas 1998, shortly after Hasbro bought it.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** by Randy Rissman and Roger Shiffman; Hasbro gave the year as 1979.
- **Based:** Mundelein, Illinois, in 1984; Vernon Hills, Illinois, by 1998.
- **Tigervision:** Atari 2600 and TI-99/4A cartridges in the early 1980s, including *Miner 2049er*.
- **Handhelds:** licensed LCD games; the R-Zone; the Game.com (1997).
- **Acquired:** by Hasbro, completed 1 April 1998, for about $335 million.
- **Afterwards:** Hasbro kept the Tiger name for its electronic toys and LCD games.

## Tigervision

In the video game boom of the early 1980s, Tiger Electronic Toys of Mundelein, Illinois, published cartridges under the name Tigervision. Most were conversions of little-known arcade games, such as *Polaris*, *River Patrol* and *Espial*, plus a few clones, such as *King Kong*, a *Donkey Kong* imitation, and *Threshold*. Its hit was the platform game *Miner 2049er*, which Tigervision published for the Atari 2600 and, at $39.95 on cartridge, for the Texas Instruments TI-99/4A. *Home Computer Magazine* reviewed the TI version in 1984 and praised its eight levels, their "unparalleled graphics" and the preview of every level at the start. The 2600 business did not last long; collectors later prized Tigervision cartridges for their glossy labels and box art, and *River Patrol* sold so briefly that it became one of the rarest 2600 games.

## Licensed LCD games

Tiger's main business was the handheld LCD game. Like Nintendo's [Game & Watch](/vault/systems/nintendo-game-and-watch), each unit played a single game on a screen of fixed segments, but Tiger's selling point was the licence on the box. Arcade games such as Sega's *Altered Beast*, *Golden Axe* and *Shinobi*, console games such as *Castlevania II: Simon's Quest*, and films such as *Batman* and *RoboCop* all became Tiger LCD games, usually much simplified. The *Digital Press* collectors' guide described Tiger after its 2600 days as continuing "to pump out handheld games (usually based on major licenses)". Its puzzle games sold too: in 1998 Hasbro listed *Lights Out* and *Brain Warp* among Tiger's top sellers, alongside Giga Pets and Laser Tag.

## R-Zone and Game.com

Tiger's first attempt at a system with interchangeable games was the R-Zone of the mid-1990s, which *Retro Gamer* compared to Nintendo's Virtual Boy: it projected LCD images onto a mirror in front of the player, and its cartridges carried licences such as *Batman Forever* and *Apollo 13*. Its second was far more ambitious. *Edge* reported in July 1997 that Tiger, "known mostly for its line of LCD games", had released the Game.com in the United States at $70. It had a larger screen than the Game Boy, two cartridge slots, a touch screen with a stylus, and built-in calendar, phone book, calculator and solitaire programs. An internet cartridge, $19.95 in the US, let owners with a separate modem and account send and receive email and use text-only web pages.

Everything ran on one Sharp SM8502 chip, which *Edge* described as Z80-based, and the 192-by-160-pixel screen showed five shades of grey to the Game Boy's four. Tiger developed every game itself or with contracted programmers, licensing names it could sell: *Batman & Robin*, *Duke Nukem 3D*, *Sonic Jam*, *Resident Evil 2*, *Fighters Megamix* and *Monopoly*. When *Edge* tried early versions in September 1997, it found that a slow screen update blurred moving sprites, and judged the machine "unlikely, at this stage, to give Nintendo many sleepless nights". That was the problem the system never solved. *Retro Gamer* later counted only about 20 games released. In June 1998 Tiger told fans online that retailers had treated the Game.com as a toy rather than a games system, leaving cartridges in warehouses instead of on shelves beside the hardware. Tiger announced a smaller Game.com Pocket Pro with a sharper, faster screen at E3 in 1998, but the Game Boy Color and Pokémon arrived the same year.

## Hasbro and Furby

On 9 February 1998 Hasbro announced that it would buy Tiger's operating assets for about $335 million. The deal was completed on 1 April 1998, and Hasbro's own accounts put the total cost at about $395 million once inventory and tooling were included. Tiger's next hit came months later: Furby, a talking furry toy, was on sale in Britain for Christmas 1998 at £30, and *Edge* advised readers to "place that order now". Tiger, now a Hasbro unit, eventually stopped making Game.com games, and went on using licences, among them Atari's, for LCD and keychain games.
