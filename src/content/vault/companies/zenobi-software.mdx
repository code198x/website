**Zenobi Software** was a UK publisher founded by John Wilson that specialised in text adventures, primarily for the ZX Spectrum and Amstrad CPC. While major publishers abandoned text adventures in the late 1980s, Zenobi kept the genre alive by publishing independently-written games, often using authoring systems like The Quill and PAW. They released hundreds of titles and built a dedicated community of writers and players.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1987
- **Founder:** [John Wilson](/vault/people/john-wilson), known in the magazines as the Rochdale Balrog
- **Location:** 26 Spotland Tops, Cutgate, Rochdale, Lancashire — printed in *CRASH*'s contact listings
- **Typical price:** £2.49, cheques payable to Zenobi Software
- **Speciality:** Text adventures
- **Output:** 400+ titles
- **Platforms:** ZX Spectrum, Amstrad CPC
- **Best-known series:** John Wilson's own *Behind Closed Doors*, called "irreverent" by *CRASH* in 1991

## An address, not an office
The most telling detail about Zenobi is the one *CRASH* printed in its contact
columns: a house number in Rochdale. Adventure players sent cheques there, and
tapes came back.

That is the whole operating model. By the turn of the 1990s *CRASH*'s adventure
column was describing "adventures being released by the sackload and new
adventure labels exploding onto the scene" — a genre the mainstream industry had
written off, sustained by mail order at **£2.49** a title.

Zenobi is therefore best understood as **a distribution service for a
community** rather than a publisher in the ordinary sense. The catalogue is
largely other people's work: what Wilson supplied was duplication, an inlay,
an address that people trusted, and continuity.

## The Balrog

John Wilson operated under a pseudonym — **the Balrog**, or the Rochdale Balrog — and the
name did more work than a byline. He was simultaneously:

- **a publisher**, running Zenobi by mail order from the Rochdale address above;
- **an author**, writing games under the Balrog name; and
- **a magazine columnist**, running the adventure column in *Amstrad Action*.

*Amstrad Action*, November 1991, prints a reader's letter that catches all three colliding at
once. Simon Netherwood writes in to ask about the column's "Lords and Ladies" feature, and
then:

> Also, in an advert by **Zenobi Software** in *Adventure Probe*, I noticed some games
> written by a person calling himself **'The Balrog'** (48K Spectrum games I hasten to add).
> **Is this an impostor?**

The Balrog answered in his own column. The reply confirms the arrangement was public rather
than concealed, and the same page carries the running joke that "*CPC Computing* had to close
due to chronic staff shortages **following a visit by the Balrog**".

The column also gives the market's condition in one line, from the man best placed to know
it: there was "**a lack of commercial (as to home-brew) adventure software around**", which
is why there was so little to put on the magazine's mail-order pages — and precisely the gap
Zenobi existed to fill.

⚠ Worth naming plainly, because a modern reader would flag it: the publisher of a genre's
games also wrote that genre's magazine column and reviewed in it. Nobody in 1991 appears to
have thought this needed disclosing. The same overlap turns up in this Vault at
[Zeppelin Games](/vault/companies/zeppelin-games), founded by *CRASH*'s adventure columnist.

## What the reviews said

Zenobi's reputation is warm, and the adventure columns were not always. *CRASH*, August 1991,
reviewing a Zenobi release at **£2.49** — *Wombat*, by **Mike Gerrard** — gives it **61%**
and explains exactly why:

> I have two criticisms. I found the majority of the adventure was just **a mapping
> exercise**, with too little to do in many places. Secondly… the programmer decided to use a
> wodge of memory putting 'clever' messages into the response table… **I can't take a game
> seriously when such excessive, irrelevant padding is used.**

The reviewer's comparison is the useful part, because it is a judgement about the house
style: that trick "was fine for **John Wilson's irreverent *Behind Closed Doors* series**"
but wasted memory in someone else's game. Wilson's own voice worked; imitating it did not.

*Wombat* is described as "a competently produced adventure with some interesting puzzles"
that "should have been a hell of a lot better" — which is a fair summary of what a curated
amateur catalogue produces. Zenobi's output was variable, and the columns said so while
continuing to cover it.

## The Amiga years, under emulation

The most technically interesting thing the company did is also the least remembered. By
1993 Zenobi was releasing on the **Amiga** as **licenceware** — and the releases were
not conversions at all. *Amiga Force*, September 1993:

> The first of Zenobi's many promised Spectrum 'conversions', *Retarded Creatures and
> Caverns* is actually **a Quilled game operating from the original Speccy code using the
> emulator supplied on the same disk**.

A [Quill](/vault/tools/the-quill)-authored Spectrum adventure, shipped on an Amiga disk with
a Spectrum emulator bundled alongside it, sold commercially in 1993. The magazine's verdict
on the approach was practical: "the game suffers little under emulation".

Other Zenobi Amiga releases covered in the same magazine include *The Balrog and the Cat* —
"penned by Zenobi supremo John Wilson" — and *Starbase 13*. The reviewer's summary of the
house voice is as good as any: Balrogs "have a strange affinity for green cheese sandwiches",
and "like all of Wilson's games", the puzzles are "interesting and actually logical".

⚠ This is emulation used as a **commercial distribution strategy**, years before emulation
became a preservation movement. A publisher with a back catalogue on a dying platform kept
selling it on a live one by shipping the old machine along with the game.

## The Zenobi Model

Unlike commercial publishers:

| Approach | Benefit |
|----------|---------|
| **Amateur authors** | Large catalogue possible |
| **Low prices** | Accessible to players |
| **Quick turnaround** | Rapid releases |
| **Community focus** | Built loyal following |
| **Mail order** | Direct to players |

## Publishing philosophy

John Wilson championed:

- Games that commercial publishers wouldn't touch
- New authors getting first releases
- Niche genres within adventures
- Quality control through curation
- Personal relationship with customers

## The Quill and PAW Connection

Most Zenobi games used authoring tools:

| Tool | Creator | Notes |
|------|---------|-------|
| **The Quill** | Gilsoft | Made adventure creation accessible |
| **PAW** | Gilsoft | Professional Adventure Writer, more powerful |

These tools democratised adventure game creation.
