## Overview

Mirrorsoft was the software division of **Mirror Group Newspapers**, and therefore of **Robert Maxwell**. For most of the eighties that was a footnote — it published good games, some of them excellent, and the ownership mattered less than the catalogue.

Then in 1991 Maxwell died, his empire was found to be built on missing pension funds, and Mirrorsoft went down with it. What made the collapse resonate beyond Britain was **what Mirrorsoft was holding at the time**.

## Fast facts

- **Parent:** Mirror Group Newspapers.
- **Seen in the press:** reviewed in *CRASH*, *ZZAP!64* and *Amstrad Action* from **1985 to 1988**, at **£12.99–£19.99**, with budget re-releases at **£1.50**.
- **Known for:** *Boulder Dash*, *Defender of the Crown*, *Dynamite Dan*, *Strike Force Harrier*, *Spitfire 40*, *Sai Combat*, *Soko-Ban*, *Andy Capp*, and **Tetris**.
- **Subsidiary:** Image Works, its late-eighties label for more ambitious 16-bit titles.

## The catalogue

A newspaper group's software house turns out to look much like anyone else's, with one tell: the **licences lean on properties the parent already owned or knew**. *Andy Capp* came from the *Daily Mirror*'s own comic strip — a cartoon about a work-shy Hartlepool drinker, turned into a computer game because the same company printed him every day.

Elsewhere the catalogue is strong and unremarkable in origin: *Boulder Dash* under licence, *Dynamite Dan* as a Spectrum platformer of the *Manic Miner* school, *Spitfire 40* and *Strike Force Harrier* as flight simulations, and *Defender of the Crown* — Cinemaware's lavish Amiga showpiece — brought to British shelves.

## Tetris, and the collapse

Mirrorsoft held European rights to [*Tetris*](/vault/games/tetris), and its sister company Spectrum HoloByte held them in America. Those rights had been assembled through a chain of sub-licences from the Soviet Union that turned out, on inspection, not to grant what everyone believed they granted.

The resulting fight — Mirrorsoft, Spectrum HoloByte, Atari Games, Nintendo and the Soviet state agency ELORG all claiming overlapping pieces of the same game — is the [Tetris legal battle](/vault/phenomena/tetris-legal-battles), and it was still unfolding when Maxwell's empire failed. The company's assets, including its games, were sold off in the wreckage.

## Why it mattered

Mirrorsoft is the clearest case in British software of a games publisher whose fate had **nothing to do with games**. It did not fail because its titles stopped selling or because it missed a platform transition. It failed because its owner was committing fraud several floors up.

That is worth an entry on its own terms, but it also marks a moment: by 1991 software was a large enough business to be worth folding into a media conglomerate — and large enough to be destroyed when one collapsed.
