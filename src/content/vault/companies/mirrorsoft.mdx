Mirrorsoft was the software house of the *Daily Mirror*, and so, in the end, of Robert Maxwell. It began in 1983 selling Mr Men programs and multiplication games through Boots and W. H. Smith, and eight years later it was one of the country's biggest games publishers, with the Bitmap Brothers, Cinemaware and *[Tetris](/vault/games/tetris)* on its books. In between, it sold the console rights to *Tetris* that it did not turn out to own, and lost them to Nintendo in a week of meetings in Moscow. It did not fail on its own account. When Maxwell drowned in November 1991 and his companies came apart, Mirrorsoft was, as *Amiga Format* put it, "a healthy company... dragged down".

## Fast facts

- **Owner:** Mirror Group Newspapers, part of Robert Maxwell's group. By the late 1980s Maxwell's son Kevin oversaw it.
- **Run by:** Jim Mackonochie, who started it as MGN's development manager.
- **First releases:** end of November 1983, for the BBC Micro and Electron.
- **Labels:** Mirrorsoft; PSS, 80% owned from 1987; Image Works, launched in 1988; the budget label Mirror Image. It also published Cinemaware's games in Britain.
- **Sister company:** Spectrum HoloByte in the United States.
- **Games:** *Dynamite Dan*, *Strike Force Harrier*, *Biggles*, *[Tetris](/vault/games/tetris)*, *Speedball*, *[Xenon 2](/vault/games/xenon-2)*, *Bloodwych*, *Bombuzal*, *Mega-lo-Mania*.
- **End:** liquidation at the end of 1991; Acclaim bought its remaining games early in 1992.

## Mr Men and the newspaper

*Acorn User* announced the new company in December 1983 under the headline "Mr Men star for Mirrorsoft". Three packs were due at the end of November, two of them for the BBC Micro and Electron, and the second release, *Quick Thinking*, came from Widgit Software. Jim Mackonochie, MGN's development manager, explained that Mirrorsoft would act as a publisher "with its own vetting team", aiming at "the games and home education sector". It had its own logo, in red, and its titles would be advertised in the *Daily Mirror* and *Sunday Mirror*. Software was "one of 25 projects MGN is expanding into, which include cable, video and satellite TV".

Mackonochie was already thinking about the paper's comic strips. The first three tapes did not use them, he said, but "if a software house wants to use a character in the paper, we will consider that": Andy Capp, perhaps, or Garth, or the Perishers. *Andy Capp* did become a Mirrorsoft game, in early 1988.

The early advertisements were for the family. In *Big K* in April 1984, under the line "Software for all the family", Mirrorsoft sold Widgit's *Sum Vaders*, in which only mental arithmetic stopped alien robots invading, "for all family members, from 8 years to adult", and *Robot Tables*, which taught multiplication by building robots. Orders went to PO Box 50, Bromley.

## Dynamite Dan

*CRASH*, looking back over its own history in 1987, marked the moment Mirrorsoft became a games publisher. After "a short and low-profile history of educational software, Mirrorsoft suddenly produced Dynamite Dan, the ever so attractive Jet Set Willy clone which because of its playability, clever map and fiendish difficulty well deserved the Smash it received". That review, in July 1985, gave the Spectrum game 94%. Dan had to find the dynamite, blow open Dr Blitzen's safe and escape in an airship, and Mirrorsoft promised that the first caller to name the tune played as the airship took off would win a flight in the Goodyear blimp.

The games did not replace the educational line; they sat beside it. A Mirrorsoft advertisement in *Amstrad Action* in September 1986 ran three columns side by side: games (*Dynamite Dan*, *Biggles*, *Boulder Dash*, *Sai Combat*, *Spitfire 40*, *Strike Force Harrier*), early learning (*First Steps with the Mr Men*, *Here & There with the Mr Men*) and children's entertainment (*Caesar's Travels*, an animated storybook). Orders now went to a freepost address in Paulton, near Bristol.

## Labels and the sixteen-bit years

Mirrorsoft grew by adding labels. In 1987 it took an 80% holding in PSS, the wargames publisher. It published Cinemaware's games, such as *[Defender of the Crown](/vault/games/defender-of-the-crown)*, and FTL's *[Dungeon Master](/vault/games/dungeon-master)* in Britain, and Spectrum HoloByte's *Falcon*. In September 1988 *Amstrad Action* reported the launch of a new entertainment label, Image Works, which joined Cinemaware and PSS "under the wing of Mirrorsoft". Its first releases included Tony Crowther and David Bishop's *Fernandez Must Die* and *Bombuzal*.

Image Works also had the [Bitmap Brothers](/vault/companies/bitmap-brothers). *Amiga Computing* reported that Mirrorsoft had offered them more money than Melbourne House, Mastertronic and Virgin could muster, and their *Speedball* (1988) and *Xenon 2: Megablast* (1989) came out on the label, with *Speedball 2* following in 1990. In April 1989 readers of EMAP's magazines voted Mirrorsoft the 16-bit software house of the year at the [Golden Joystick Awards](/vault/events/golden-joystick-awards). Later releases included [Sensible Software](/vault/companies/sensible-software)'s *Mega-lo-Mania* (1991).

## Tetris

The London agent Robert Stein offered *Tetris* to Mackonochie. By David Sheff's account in *Game Over*, Mackonochie was not sure it would sell, and showed it to Spectrum HoloByte, Mirrorsoft's sister company in America; Mirrorsoft would take Europe if HoloByte took the United States and Japan. The Maxwells had financial control of both companies. In June 1987 Stein signed a contract with the two, although he still had no contract with Moscow, and they released *Tetris* for home computers in January 1988 in a red box with St Basil's Cathedral on the front. The full story is told in [the Tetris rights wars](/vault/phenomena/tetris-legal-battles).

Mirrorsoft then sold on rights it did not securely hold. It traded the console rights for North America and Japan to Atari Games in exchange for Atari's *Blasteroids*, a deal Sheff says Kevin Maxwell backed over Spectrum HoloByte's objections. *Edge* later put the problem simply: Mirrorsoft and Spectrum HoloByte "understood their Tetris rights to be more extensive than they really were". In February 1989 Kevin Maxwell went to Moscow to negotiate with the Soviet agency Elorg, in the same week as Stein and [Henk Rogers](/vault/people/henk-rogers). According to Sheff, when Elorg showed him Rogers's Japanese *Tetris* cartridge, Maxwell said Mirrorsoft had not licensed video-game rights and the cartridge must be pirated, which helped Elorg argue that it had never sold those rights. Stein left with the arcade rights; Kevin Maxwell, *Edge* wrote, "came away with nothing but empty assurances". On 22 March 1989 Elorg signed the console rights to [Nintendo](/vault/companies/nintendo) and telexed Mirrorsoft to say so. Robert Maxwell's attempts to reverse the deal through the Soviet government failed.

## Maxwell and Sinclair

Maxwell had nearly owned a much bigger part of the British computer industry. In the summer of 1985 he agreed to take control of [Sinclair Research](/vault/companies/sinclair-research), and *Sinclair User* ran "Who is Robert Maxwell?" for its readers. The rescue fell through within weeks: the board of Maxwell's company Hollis decided, on its bankers' advice, that it "would not be advisable", and Sinclair's creditors and bankers kept the company going instead. In 1986 Sinclair sold its computer business to Amstrad.

## The collapse

Robert Maxwell was found to be missing from his yacht near the Canary Islands on 5 November 1991. It then emerged that he had used hundreds of millions of pounds from his companies' pension funds to prop up the group. Mirrorsoft was reputedly profitable through 1991, *Amiga Power* reported, but it was caught up in the turmoil inside the Mirror Group. Its staff got the news on New Year's Eve: the company had gone into liquidation just before Christmas, and hopes of a buy-out led by its managing director, Peter Bilotta, and sales and marketing director, Sean Brennan, came to nothing. Developers were left with unpaid work. Graftgold's Steve Turner, whose *Fire & Ice* was part-financed by Mirrorsoft, called it "another case of a major publishing company going under while owing money to its developers".

Early in 1992 Acclaim, until then a console publisher, bought what was left. *The One* reported that Acclaim had bought "all of Mirrorsoft's licences, trademarks and properties under development"; *Computer and Video Games* said it had taken over "all the 16-bit games from the now-defunct Mirrorsoft", and *CU Amiga* that it had "snapped up what was left". Other games found new homes. Strangeways, which had been writing *Apocalypse* for Mirrorsoft, used a clause in its contract to take it to Virgin; Graftgold's *Fire & Ice* went to [Renegade](/vault/companies/renegade); and UBI Soft acquired Vivid Image's *First Samurai* and reissued it with *Mega-lo-Mania*. The Bitmap Brothers held the trademarks to their games, and Virgin published the Sega versions of *Xenon 2* and *Speedball 2* in 1992.
