## Overview

**Bubble Bus Software** operated from a High Street address in Tonbridge, Kent, and is
remembered for one game. *Starquake* (1985) was written by **Stephen Crow**, and it is the
kind of game that outlives its publisher: a huge, interconnected map explored under time
pressure, still being referred to years later as an 8-bit classic without needing
introduction.

## Fast facts

- **Address, as advertised:** **87 High Street, Tonbridge, Kent TN9 1RX**. Telephone 0732 355962.
- **Key programmer:** **Stephen Crow** — *Wizard's Lair*, then *Starquake*.
- **Other titles:** *Hyperforce*, *Skaterock*.
- **Formats advertised:** an unusually wide spread — Spectrum, C64/128, Amstrad CPC and PCW, BBC, Atari, and the **Sinclair QL**.
- **BBC/Electron *Starquake*, 1987:** **£9.95** cassette, **£11.95** disc.

## Starquake

*Amtix!* named Crow as the author in July 1986 and placed the game in sequence after his
earlier *Wizard's Lair*. The premise is small and the execution is not: you are **Blob** — a
**Bio-Logically Operated Being** — and the planet's core needs rebuilding before it collapses.

What made it work was scale under constraint. Blob can carry only so much power and
ammunition at a time, replenished by collecting objects as you go, so the map is not just
large but has to be *managed*. *Acorn User* put the size at "**over 500 rooms**" when the BBC
conversion arrived.

## The Acorn conversion, and what it meant

Bubble Bus reached the BBC Micro late, and *Acorn User*'s reaction in 1987 says more about
Acorn owners than about Bubble Bus. Its news page introduced the company as "**a software
house new to the BBC micro market**", and the review by **Robert Miller** opened with
something close to relief — that at last other software houses were taking an interest in the
machine.

Miller's verdict was warm but not uncritical: the theme was not especially original, the
£9.95 asking price "**may be a little high**", but the game was varied and big enough to keep
you engrossed, and he recommended it to anyone who liked exploring and shooting in the same
sitting.

⚠ That is worth reading against the [Acornsoft](/vault/companies/acornsoft) and
[Superior Software](/vault/companies/superior-software) entries. The BBC's software problem
was not quality — it was **supply**, and a Kent publisher porting a two-year-old Spectrum hit
counted as news.

## Stephen Crow left

The pattern this Vault records repeatedly. Having written Bubble Bus's two best-known games,
Crow moved on: by January 1987 *Amstrad Action* was reviewing ***Firelord*** — his latest —
for **[Hewson](/vault/companies/hewson-consultants)**, the label that made a policy of putting
authors' names on the box.

Bubble Bus's catalogue after that is thin, and the company does not have a second act. A
publisher whose value was one programmer keeps that value exactly as long as the programmer
stays — which is the argument the [Graftgold](/vault/companies/graftgold) and
[Hewson](/vault/companies/hewson-consultants) entries make from the other end, where losing
two authors took the top off a company.

## Why it mattered

*Starquake* survived its publisher by decades. An Amiga review years later could refer in
passing to "the old 8-bit Bubble Bus classic" and expect readers to know what was meant —
which is a better measure of a game's standing than any score.

Bubble Bus itself is the entry for the **one-game publisher**: a real business, in a real
High Street, whose entire place in this Vault rests on having published the right programmer
once.
