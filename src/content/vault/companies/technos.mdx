In 1981 two men left their jobs at [Data East](/vault/companies/data-east) and, with a third partner, set up a games company of their own. According to *[Retro Gamer](/vault/magazines/retro-gamer)*, one story has Technos Japan starting in a single-room apartment, another in a small office with ten engineers. Within six years it had made two arcade games that changed the fighting game. *Nekketsu Kōha Kunio-kun*, released in the West as *[Renegade](/vault/games/renegade)*, let a street fighter be surrounded, grabbed and kicked by a gang. *[Double Dragon](/vault/games/double-dragon)* put two players side by side and sent them through a city to rescue a kidnapped girlfriend. Both came from the same designer, Yoshihisa Kishimoto. *Double Dragon*'s success, *Wireframe* wrote, sparked "a wave of both obvious copycat titles" and brawlers that improved on it.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1981, in Japan, by Kunio Taki (president) and Takashi Hanya, both formerly of Data East, with Takeo Hagiwara.
- **Known for:** *Renegade* (*Nekketsu Kōha Kunio-kun*, 1986), *Double Dragon* (1987), the Japanese *Kunio-kun* series including *[River City Ransom](/vault/games/river-city-ransom)* (1989), *WWF Superstars* (1989) and *WWF WrestleFest* (1991).
- **American arm:** American Technos, founded in 1987, closed at the end of 1999.
- **Ended:** filed for bankruptcy in 1996 and closed in 1997.
- **Afterwards:** the rights to its games passed to Million, a company joined by former Technos staff, and in 2015 to Arc System Works.

## From Data East to Kunio-kun

Technos's first game showed its origins. *Minky Monkey* (1982), in which the player climbed vines, grabbed fruit and dodged snakes, ran, *Retro Gamer* noted, on Data East hardware. Over the next three years Technos released nine more games, many of them licensed abroad through other companies. Data East sold its wrestling game *Big Pro Wrestling* in American arcades as *Tag Team Wrestling*. Taito America took *Exciting Hour*, another wrestling game, as *Mat Mania* in 1985; many of the staff were wrestling fans. Later accounts also credit Technos with Data East's *Karate Champ*, known in Japan as *Karate Dō*, and the list of Technos games that Million confirmed to *Retro Gamer* in 2005 includes *Karate Do — The Way of Karate*.

Kishimoto came from Data East too. He had directed its laserdisc games *Thunder Storm* and *Road Blaster* before Technos offered him the chance to make action games in 1985. His first project there was *Nekketsu Kōha Kunio-kun* (1986), a fighting game drawn, he told *Retro Gamer*, from his own teenage years fighting students from rival schools. The hero was named after the company's president, Kunio Taki. "He also was a bad guy when he was young!" Kishimoto said.

Its fighters could be attacked from both sides, so Technos gave the cabinet one button to hit left and another to hit right. For America the game was redrawn with a new hero, new enemies and new backgrounds and released as *Renegade*, which reached the arcades there in 1986 and British home computers the following year. Kishimoto called the redesign "a very big waste of time and money", and when Taki found him working on a *Kunio-kun* sequel he asked for a game that would sell everywhere. That game became *Double Dragon*.

## Double Dragon and American Technos

*Double Dragon* came out in 1987, with Taito America handling its arcade distribution outside Japan. To handle its own games in North America, Technos set up American Technos that year under Keiichi Iwamoto, who had once supplied arcade parts to Data East and had set up Data East USA's California offices. Greg Rice, a regional sales manager at Atari Games, joined late in 1988. He remembered his first visit to the Japanese offices, for the company party that November, with 200 staff, karaoke, and Kishimoto filling half a beer glass with tequila instead of taking shots. He also remembered developers sleeping at their desks to meet deadlines.

The home rights went in several directions. In Britain, Melbourne House, part of Mastertronic, signed exclusive worldwide home-computer rights to all Technos arcade games early in 1988, and *CRASH* reported that it was the first time a major Japanese coin-op maker had assigned computer rights to its future games. In America Tradewest, a start-up publisher from Texas, took the licence for several NES versions of *Double Dragon* and the merchandising rights that followed. Rice later called it the chance the company lost: "It was nothing for American Technos to receive a royalty check from Tradewest of $500,000 or $750,000 for units shipped. If American Technos had been shipping that product then I wouldn't be here talking to you today." Technos Japan, he explained, had wanted the guaranteed money, which paid for the *Kunio-kun* games at home.

American Technos took over arcade distribution of the *Double Dragon* sequels. Technos also licensed the World Wrestling Federation for two arcade games, *WWF Superstars* (1989) and *WWF WrestleFest* (1991). *WrestleFest* became American Technos's best-selling arcade game; Rice watched operators empty the coin box more than once. American Technos's first NES game in America was *River City Ransom* (1990), which Infogrames brought to Europe as *Street Gangs*.

## Kunio at home

In Japan, Technos's second office, in Niigata, was set up to work on the *Kunio-kun* series. On the Famicom, Kunio and his classmates became small, big-headed figures, and between 1991 and 1994 Technos put them into "possibly every NES/SNES sports style game it could try", *Retro Gamer* wrote, from baseball, basketball and ice hockey to dodgeball. A few reached the West under other names. *Super Dodge Ball* came out on the NES in America through CSG Imagesoft, and Nintendo itself published the football game *Nintendo World Cup*, which could take four players with the NES adapter. *River City Ransom*, which mixed the street fighting of *Renegade* with shops and statistics, became the best known of them abroad.

Most of the series stayed in Japan. The Super Famicom *Kunio* games, including *Shodai Nekketsu Kōha Kunio-kun*, a sequel to *Renegade*, were never translated, because, *Retro Gamer* explained, their stories would have needed changing again for America.

## The last years

Technos's 16-bit brawler *The Combatribes*, about cyborg fighters hunting a gang leader through New York, reached the arcades in May 1990, months after Capcom's *[Final Fight](/vault/games/final-fight)*, and the Super NES version in 1993. Its sales were mediocre, and it was the last Nintendo game American Technos published. Technos Japan tried other licences, including *Popeye*, as its money ran short. Rice summed up the problem: "For every ten bad ones you might get a good one. We just went through a period where we didn't have the good ones."

Technos turned to [SNK](/vault/companies/snk)'s [Neo Geo](/vault/systems/neo-geo). A new *Double Dragon* and *Voltage Fighter Gowcaizer* came out on Neo Geo cartridges in 1995. The last release was a *Kunio*-style dodgeball game for the Neo Geo in 1996. That year Technos Japan filed for bankruptcy, and in 1997 it closed. American Technos tried to carry on with a Taiwanese investor and a partnership with the British publisher [Elite](/vault/companies/elite-systems), and was wound up at the end of 1999.

## What happened to the games

Former Technos staff went to Million Corporation, which held the copyrights by 2005 and licensed new versions of the old games. Atlus published *Super Dodge Ball Advance*, *Double Dragon Advance* and *River City Ransom EX* for the Game Boy Advance. In June 2015 Arc System Works announced that it had taken over the rights to every Technos Japan game from Million, among them *Double Dragon*, *Super Dodge Ball*, *River City Ransom* and the *Kunio-kun* series. New games followed, from *Double Dragon IV* (2017) to *River City Girls* (2019).
