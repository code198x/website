Marshall Cavendish sold knowledge by the week. Its part-works were magazines that came out one part at a time from the newsagent, each part numbered and paged to follow on from the last, so that a reader who kept them all and bought the binders ended up with a set of books. In 1984 the company applied the idea to home computers. *INPUT*, "a Marshall Cavendish computer course in weekly parts", ran for 52 weeks and taught programming on the Spectrum, the Commodore 64, the BBC Micro and Electron and the Dragon 32 side by side. The same year it agreed to buy a series of games from Liverpool's [Imagine Software](/vault/companies/imagine-software) to go with the course, a deal reported to be worth millions. It fell apart within months and played its part in Imagine's collapse.

## Fast facts

- **Company:** Marshall Cavendish Limited, London, incorporated on 19 June 1967. Its part-works were sold by Marshall Cavendish Partworks Ltd, and readers' orders went to Marshall Cavendish Services in Hove, Sussex.
- **Computing part-work:** *[INPUT](/vault/magazines/input-magazine)*, 52 weekly parts in 1984 and 1985, 95p a part at launch.
- **Abroad:** *INPUT* was licensed to Editora Nova Cultural in Brazil and lent its illustrations to Ediciones Forum's *INPUT Commodore* in Spain.
- **Owner:** Times Publishing, of Singapore; Wikipedia dates the purchase to 1980.
- **Now:** Marshall Cavendish International (Asia), a general book publisher, and Marshall Cavendish Education, both in the Times Publishing group.

## Part-works

A part-work was cheap to start and expensive to finish. The first part of *INPUT* cost 95p, binders were extra, and a reader who stayed the course paid for all 52 parts. In return the parts built into a single course of more than 1,600 numbered pages, with an index in the last part. Marshall Cavendish had been selling serious subjects this way for years. Its *History of the Second World War*, edited by Sir Basil Liddell Hart, was still being cited in *COMPUTE!* in 1982 as a source for a wargame review, in the American edition of 1973–74, and the games writer Tim Hartnell borrowed board games from R. C. Bell's *The Board Game Book*, published by Marshall Cavendish in London in 1979.

## INPUT

*INPUT* began in 1984. Its first part opened with a football analogy: you could learn every skill before stepping on the pitch, "but you would learn slowly - and have very little fun in the process". So the course began with a guessing game, and from there worked through BASIC, machine code, games programming and practical applications, with each machine's version of a listing marked by its own symbol. The back cover of each part sold the next one under the slogan "Learn programming – for fun and the future". The 52nd part closed the course with an index to the other 51 and a note to readers: "Whether you have typed in all the programs yet or not, you will still want to keep INPUT as a source of reference material for the future." Later printings, at £1.00 a part, carried copyright dates up to 1987. The full story of the course is in [the INPUT entry](/vault/magazines/input-magazine).

## The Imagine deal

*INPUT* was meant to come with software. Bruce Everiss, Imagine's operations director, later called the deal Imagine's "mistake number five": "the Marshall Cavendish project to produce 26 games across five machines — this was worth as much as £11 million to Imagine over two years." *CRASH* reported that the contracts were signed early in 1984, and that Marshall Cavendish "became disenchanted by the lack of progress on their games. They had already paid out a lot of money and seem to have been unhappy with the quality of what was ready. They pulled out and wanted their money back." *Sinclair User* reported in May 1984 that Imagine had been due to produce 30 programs and that Marshall Cavendish had rejected them, and in August that the publisher had demanded the repayment of a £200,000 advance. *CRASH* put the advance at a quarter of a million pounds.

Imagine was wound up that summer. Of the games it had been writing, *Retro Gamer* notes that only *Pedro* was published, under Imagine's own name, for the Spectrum, Commodore 64, BBC Micro, Electron and Dragon: the same five machines *INPUT* taught. *INPUT* went on without tapes, as a course of listings that readers typed in themselves.

## After the part-works

Marshall Cavendish's British part-work company, Marshall Cavendish Partworks Ltd, was registered in 1982 and dissolved in 2011, according to Companies House. The company that held *INPUT*'s copyright, Marshall Cavendish Limited, is still registered in London as a book and periodical publisher. The name is now best known in Singapore, where Marshall Cavendish International (Asia) publishes general books, among them the *CultureShock!* travel series, and Marshall Cavendish Education publishes school materials; both describe themselves as members of the Times Publishing Group.
