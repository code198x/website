Sony tried three times to get into games before the PlayStation made it one of the biggest names in them. In the 1980s it built MSX home computers. At the end of the decade it designed the sound chip inside Nintendo's Super Famicom and agreed to build that console a CD-ROM drive. In June 1991 Nintendo walked away from the CD deal in front of the world's press. Sony answered with a console of its own, and when the PlayStation reached Japanese shops on 3 December 1994, *Edge* called it "the hardware launch of the decade". In the same launch coverage the magazine judged that Sony's earlier forays into games, starting with "the abortive MSX home computer standard", had "not been great successes".

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 7 May 1946 in Tokyo, by Masaru Ibuka and Akio Morita, as Tokyo Tsushin Kogyo; named Sony from 1958.
- **Home computers:** the HitBit range of [MSX](/vault/systems/msx) machines; the 64K HB-75 sold in Britain for £299 in 1984.
- **Sound chip:** the Super Famicom's sound unit, designed by Ken Kutaragi.
- **Games business:** Sony Electronic Publishing, set up in New York by 1991, with its games label Sony Imagesoft; Sony Computer Entertainment, set up in Tokyo in 1993 and renamed Sony Interactive Entertainment in 2016.
- **Consoles:** [PlayStation](/vault/systems/sony-playstation) (1994), [PlayStation 2](/vault/systems/sony-playstation-2) (2000), PSP (2004), PlayStation 3 (2006), PS Vita (2011), PlayStation 4 (2013), PlayStation 5 (2020).
- **Studio bought in 1993:** [Psygnosis](/vault/companies/psygnosis) of Liverpool, for £30 million.

## The HitBit

MSX was a shared standard for home computers, announced in Tokyo in June 1983, and Sony was one of the many electronics companies that built machines to it. Every MSX had the same Z80 processor, Texas Instruments video chip, General Instrument sound chip and Microsoft BASIC, so a cartridge for one ran on all of them. Makers competed on what they added, and Sony added software in ROM. When *Your Computer* explained MSX to British readers in January 1984, it singled out the "Sony Hit-Bit MSX micro" for its built-in database program.

The HitBit reached British shops later that year. In November 1984 *Computer & Video Games* lined up the MSX machines on sale in Britain: Sony's HB-75 cost £299 for 64K, with two joystick ports, a printer port and two cartridge slots, and an 8K utility ROM holding address book, memo and scheduling programs. A data cartridge could store what they held. A British advertisement that month sold the feature as a "Personal Data Bank", with a Sony Trinitron television to plug the computer into. Sony published cartridge games for the HitBit too; *Your Computer* had reviewed its maze game *Sparkie* in January.

MSX never took hold in Britain the way it did in Japan and the Netherlands, but the HitBit had a working life in British games all the same. When Peter Johnson converted Konami's *Yie Ar Kung-Fu II* for [Ocean](/vault/companies/ocean-software), he worked from a Japanese MSX cartridge of the game and "an imported Sony MSX HitBit computer". Sony carried on into the later MSX generations: in 1990 a Dutch importer was advertising a Sony MSX2+.

## The sound chip and the Nintendo deal

Sony's next route into games ran through [Nintendo](/vault/companies/nintendo). Nintendo asked Sony's audio research division for a sound chip for the Super Famicom, and the engineer who designed it was Ken Kutaragi, who would later lead the PlayStation. Both *Edge*, in 1995, and *Retro Gamer*'s history of the PlayStation name him as the chip's designer. The [Super Nintendo](/vault/systems/super-nintendo) entry explains how the unit worked: an 8-bit SPC700 processor with its own 64K of memory, driving a digital signal processor that played eight voices of sampled sound. *Edge* reported that Sony kept the rights to programming the chip and "charged Nintendo excessive fees" for the information Nintendo's developers needed.

In 1988 the two companies agreed that Sony would build a CD-ROM drive for the Super Famicom, and Sony planned a console of its own that would play both Super Famicom cartridges and a new CD format, the Super Disc. Sony called it the PlayStation. In June 1991, the day after Sony announced the machine at the Consumer Electronics Show in Chicago, Nintendo told its own press conference that it would work with Philips instead. The [PlayStation](/vault/systems/sony-playstation) entry tells the story in full, from two independent accounts: David Sheff's *Game Over* and *Edge*'s launch feature of February 1995.

The same month, *ACE* interviewed Olaf Olafsson, the 29-year-old president of Sony Electronic Publishing, a new New York company set up to make games and multimedia from the films and music Sony owned: Columbia Pictures and Sony Music, formerly CBS Records. Olafsson said Sony would make games based on its films *Hook* and *Hudson Hawk*, with [Ocean](/vault/companies/ocean-software) publishing them in Europe, and that the company was not tied to any format. "We would publish games on potato chips if it made sense," he said. *ACE* also passed on industry talk that the Super Famicom CD drive Sony was making would cost just under ¥100,000.

## Sony Imagesoft

Sony Electronic Publishing's games label was Sony Imagesoft, the new name for a business Sony already owned, CSG Imagesoft. *ACE* named CSG Imagesoft's first hit as *Solstice* for the [Nintendo Entertainment System](/vault/systems/nintendo-entertainment-system), written by Software Creations in Manchester. Most of what followed was film licences, and they did not help Sony's reputation. In 1995 *Edge* wrote that Sony Electronic Publishing, established in 1991, had "seen its reputation plummet with the release of sub-standard cartridge games like *Last Action Hero* and *Cliffhanger*". *Retro Gamer* later made the same judgement of the same two games, calling them "half-baked movie licences".

## Sony Computer Entertainment

Sony set up Sony Computer Entertainment in Tokyo in 1993, jointly with Sony Music Entertainment (Japan), to make the new console. The machine that surfaced late in 1993 as the PS-X was a dedicated games console with no cartridge slot. *Retro Gamer*'s account has Kutaragi arguing that a 16-bit machine tied to Nintendo was a dead end. Sony's president, Norio Ohga, first met his proposal with derision, until Kutaragi asked whether Sony's pride could let it walk away after the way Nintendo had treated it. Other senior executives still thought video games were toys for children, and the console was styled to look like a piece of hi-fi and video equipment instead.

Sony had no established in-house games division, as *Edge* pointed out, so from late 1993 it set about recruiting other people's. By the Japanese launch, *Edge* counted more than 250 companies signed up and some 700 development stations shipped. [Namco](/vault/companies/namco) wrote *Ridge Racer* for the launch and built arcade boards on the same hardware, and Konami was working on a dozen PlayStation games. SCE's slogan, which *Edge* quoted and *Retro Gamer* attributed to its director Akira Sato, was "If it's not realtime, it's not a game", a dig at the full-motion-video games of the CD machines before it.

Sony had also bought a studio. In June 1993 it paid £30 million for [Psygnosis](/vault/companies/psygnosis) in Liverpool, a purchase *Edge* said many people had questioned. Psygnosis's *[WipEout](/vault/games/wipeout)* was the best-selling game when the PlayStation reached Europe in September 1995. The console went on sale in Japan on 3 December 1994 at ¥39,800, in North America on 9 September 1995 at $299, and in Europe on 29 September 1995, at £299 in Britain. Sony counts more than 102.4 million sold.

In 1997 Sony let the public program it too, with Net Yaroze, a black PlayStation sold with a C compiler and Sony's libraries; *Edge* credited the idea to Kutaragi.

## Later consoles

The [PlayStation 2](/vault/systems/sony-playstation-2) followed on 4 March 2000 in Japan, at the same ¥39,800. It played DVD films and ran the older machine's games on the original PlayStation processor, which it kept on board as its input/output chip, and Sony counts more than 160 million sold, more than any console before it. The PSP, Sony's handheld console, went on sale in Japan in December 2004 at ¥19,800, about a week after Nintendo's DS.

The PlayStation 3 launched in Japan on 11 November 2006 and in the United States six days later. *Edge* wrote that delays and shortages had given Sony's American head, Kazuo Hirai, "a tricky year", which he ended by replacing Kutaragi as president of Sony Computer Entertainment. The PS Vita handheld arrived in Japan on 17 December 2011 and in most other countries in February 2012. The PlayStation 4 was announced at £349 and $399, undercutting Microsoft's Xbox One, and launched in November 2013.

Sony's studios changed too. It closed Studio Liverpool, the former Psygnosis, in August 2012, after seventeen years of *WipEout* games. In April 2016 Sony Computer Entertainment merged with Sony's network business to become Sony Interactive Entertainment, based in San Mateo, California. The PlayStation 5 followed in November 2020. Sony's published figures give the PlayStation 3 more than 87.4 million sales, the PlayStation 4 more than 117 million and the PSP more than 76.4 million.
