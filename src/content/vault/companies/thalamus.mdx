## Overview

Thalamus is the unusual case of a games label started by a **games magazine publisher**. [Newsfield](/vault/companies/newsfield) — the company behind [*CRASH*](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine) and [*ZZAP!64*](/vault/magazines/zzap-64) — set it up to publish software rather than write about it, and aimed it squarely at the Commodore 64 at the point where that machine's programmers were at their most technically ambitious.

## Fast facts

- **Parent:** Newsfield Publications.
- **Focus:** Commodore 64, later Amiga.
- **Seen in the press:** reviewed in *ZZAP!64* and *Amiga Power* from **1987 to 1994**, at **£9.95** on 8-bit and **£25.99** on 16-bit.
- **Known for:** *Hawkeye*, *Sanxion*, *Delta*, *Armalyte*, *Quedex*, *Creatures*.

## A magazine's taste, made into a catalogue

The premise was straightforward and slightly audacious: *ZZAP!64* spent every issue telling readers what an excellent C64 game looked like, so its publisher would make some.

What that meant in practice was a house style — technically showy games that pushed the machine's scrolling and sound hard, with strong soundtracks and a visual polish that reviewed well precisely because it was built to the standards the reviewers had spent years articulating. *Hawkeye* is the emblematic release: smooth multi-directional scrolling, a much-praised soundtrack, and a look that announced the machine was being taken seriously.

The obvious awkwardness — a magazine publisher owning a label its own magazines reviewed — was noted at the time, and is part of why Thalamus is interesting rather than merely another publisher. It sits at the exact point where the British games press stopped only observing the industry and started being part of it.

## Why it mattered

The label demonstrates something the era makes easy to forget: **the magazines were not neutral observers**. They shaped what got made, set the criteria games were judged by, employed the people who would go on to run studios, and in this case funded the software directly.

Thalamus also belongs to a specific moment of C64 craft — the late-eighties period when programmers had exhausted the obvious tricks and were competing on how much they could get the machine to do at once. Its catalogue is a reasonable shortlist for what that looked like at its best.
