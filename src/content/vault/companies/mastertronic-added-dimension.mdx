## Overview

**M.A.D.** — Mastertronic Added Dimension — was [Mastertronic](/vault/companies/mastertronic)'s answer to a problem its own success had created. £1.99 games sold in enormous numbers, and £1.99 would not pay for a game that took longer than a few months to write.

## Fast facts

- **Price:** **£2.99**, one pound above the parent label.
- **Seen in the press:** *CRASH* and *ZZAP!64*, **1985–1988**.
- **Published:** *Stormbringer*, *Revenge of the Mutant Camels II*, *Super Trolley*, *Plexar*.
- **The Magic Knight series:** *CRASH* describes *Stormbringer* as the fourth Magic Knight game, after *Finders Keepers*, *Spellbound* and *Knight Tyme*.

## The pound that bought a bigger game

The gap between £1.99 and £2.99 sounds trivial and was not. A budget publisher's economics are brutally simple: **the price, times the number sold, minus manufacturing, is what pays for the game**. Raise the price by fifty per cent and, if the audience holds, the development budget rises by roughly the same.

What that bought is visible in the catalogue. The Magic Knight games are proper arcade adventures — dozens of rooms, an inventory, a menu-driven interaction system, puzzles that need a map and notes. They are the kind of game that would have sat at £7.95 on another label, and they came out at £2.99.

Jeff Minter's *Revenge of the Mutant Camels II* on the same label makes the same point from another direction. A named, established programmer with a following was releasing at budget price, which in 1987 was no longer a statement about quality.

## The author problem

The Magic Knight games were written by **David Jones** — and here the Vault has to be careful, because that name belongs to at least two people in this period. The Magic Knight author is not the David Jones who founded DMA Design in Dundee and made *Lemmings* and *Grand Theft Auto*.

Both are British, both worked in the same decade, and a search on the name alone will merge them. This entry names him and deliberately does not link, because **an incorrect link is worse than a missing one** — it asserts an identity rather than leaving a gap someone can fill.

## Why it mattered

M.A.D. is the entry for **price tiering**, the mechanism by which the British budget market grew up. Between 1984 and 1988 the ladder filled in — £1.99, £2.99, £3.99, £9.99 mid-price, £7.95 to £9.95 full price — and each rung existed because somebody discovered the rung below it could not fund the games they wanted to publish.

It also complicates the neat division between budget and full price. By 1987 a £2.99 cassette could hold a large, well-made arcade adventure by a known author, and the only reliable way to tell what you were buying was to read a review.
