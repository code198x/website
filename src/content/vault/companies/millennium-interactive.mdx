*James Pond* was meant to be called *Guppy*. Chris Sorrell, the 18-year-old programmer and artist at the small developer Vectordean, had pitched an underwater game about a goggle-eyed goldfish to Millennium, a young Cambridge publisher. "The project went into development with the working title *Guppy*," he told *Retro Gamer*, "until one day the name James Pond was offered up by Millennium's MD." The fish in a secret agent's suit carried Millennium through the early 1990s. The company then put *Diggers* in the box with Commodore's CD32, and its last game under its own name was *Creatures*, a program about breeding artificial life. In 1997 its games team became a Sony studio.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** spring 1990, when Logotron's games arm became Millennium, run by Michael Hayward; Ian Saunter was development director.
- **Based:** Cambridge.
- **Published:** *Resolution 101*, *Thunderstrike*, *Cloud Kingdoms*, *James Pond* (1990), *James Pond 2: Robocod* (1991), *The Aquatic Games* (1992), *Global Effect* (1992), *James Pond 3: Operation Starfish* (1993), *Diggers* (1993), *Brutal Sports Football* (1993), *Morph*, *Wild Cup Soccer* (1994).
- **Outside developers:** Vectordean (Steve Bak and Chris Sorrell) for the *James Pond* games; Teque for *Brutal Sports Football* and *Wild Cup Soccer*.
- ***Amiga Power* scores:** *Robocod* 87%, *Brutal Sports Football* 88%, *Morph* 85%, *Diggers* 80%.
- **Afterwards:** *Creatures* (1996) came from its artificial-life team, CyberLife; the games team joined Sony in 1997 and finished *MediEvil* there.

## Logotron's games arm

Logotron was a Cambridge publisher of educational software, and in 1988 it began publishing games too, among them *Star Goose*. *Raze* told the next part in 1991: "It wasn't until the spring of 1990 that Logotron's entertainment arm split from its parent to become Millennium under the guiding hand of Michael Hayward." The first Millennium games were *Thunderstrike* and Paul Carruthers' *Resolution 101*, which, *Raze* said, "walked out of the shops on their own". For a while the two names ran side by side: an *AmigaWorld* advertisement in January 1991 described Millennium as "a trademark of Logotron Entertainment Ltd", and the next month *ST Format* called it "Logotron's new games arm".

*Edge*, interviewing Hayward in 1996, said the company had begun "picking up on an ad hoc basis" games from "bedroom independents" before its founders, Hayward and Ian Saunter, turned to development of their own.

## James Pond

Vectordean was three people: Steve Bak, Chris Sorrell and Tim Coupe. Sorrell had been drawing graphics for Bak's games, and *James Pond* was his first original design. He built it in seven months, borrowing the flying feel of Andrew Braybrook's *Gribbly's Day Out* for an underwater world. Millennium launched it at the end of 1990 as *James Pond: Underwater Agent*, a spoof of the James Bond films in twelve missions. Its advertisement promised a hero who would take on "anything, from retrieving radio-active canisters and plugging the leaks in oil tankers to rescuing doomed lobsters, mermaids and rare orchids from extinction". Electronic Arts took the game to the Mega Drive; Sorrell recalled that Steve Bak came back from EA's offices in San Francisco "with a custom-built, reverse-engineered Genesis dev-kit".

*Raze* called Pond "the only character to emerge from 1990 with a following". The sequel doubled the joke. In *James Pond 2: Robocod* the fish has a robot body, a parody of RoboCop, and has to rescue Santa's toy factory from the villain Dr Maybe. Sorrell said the team wrote it on the second floor of "a dodgy-looking used-car lot". *Amiga Power* reviewed it at £24.99 in November 1991, gave it 87% and called it "one of the best games we've ever seen".

*Robocod*'s success, Sorrell said, "eclipsed its predecessor with ease". Millennium followed it with a sports spin-off, *The Aquatic Games* (1992), which Bak made with Vectordean's artists, and then *James Pond 3: Operation Starfish* (1993). By then Sega had *Sonic the Hedgehog*, and Sorrell was open about the influence: he had been "struck by the flashiness and speed of the new mascot on the block: Sonic", and the third game "was an attempt to try and combine" Sonic with *Super Mario World*. It took "twice as long as the first two games combined", and he thought that in chasing Mario and Sonic, "we lost some of the identity and innocence".

## The Electronic Arts connection

Millennium's games reached a wider market through larger partners. EA published *James Pond* on the Mega Drive, and in 1992 *Amiga Format* reported that "Electronic Arts swallows up Millennium". The two companies' names appeared together on releases that year, among them Toby Simpson's god game *Global Effect*, listed in *PC Review* as "Millenium/Electronic Arts" at £34.99. Millennium still published Amiga games under its own name until 1994.

## Diggers and the CD32

*Diggers*, designed by Toby Simpson, was "the first game to be developed solely for the CD32 console", *Amiga Format* said in September 1993, and Commodore packed it with Flair's *Oscar* in the CD32's launch bundle. Mail-order advertisements that winter sold the "CD-32 console with *Oscar* and *Diggers*", along with AGA versions of both for the A1200. *Amiga Power* gave *Diggers* 80%. Commodore went into liquidation the following spring, so the console that *Diggers* was made to sell did not last long.

Teque's games did better with *Amiga Power*. *Brutal Sports Football* scored 88%. Its 1994 follow-up, *Wild Cup Soccer*, sold at £25.99; the reviewer won every game without trading players or buying weapons and found those additions "entirely superfluous".

## Creatures, CyberLife and Sony

By 1996 Millennium had stopped publishing and worked as a developer. *Edge* mentioned "work-for-hire jobs" such as Raymond Briggs's *The Snowman*, and *Defcon 5*. Its main project was *Creatures*. In October 1996 *Edge* put "Cambridge-based Millennium Interactive" at the front of a move into artificial life: in *Creatures* the player looks after small creatures called Norns, each with, as *Computer and Video Games* put it, "its own biochemistry and genetic make-up", which learn about their world and breed. Hayward told *Edge* that the next step was "to develop partnerships, which would enable us to exploit the underlying technology of *Creatures* before anyone else does". By the time the game shipped it carried the name of CyberLife Technology, the company built around that technology; Mindscape published it in America.

The traditional games team went another way. Chris Sorrell had joined Millennium from Vectordean in 1994, and his next game, *MediEvil*, began with "no certainty of which platform it would be on"; "we thought we might be signing up with Sega — we even started work on the Saturn", and it was about ten months into the project when the team met Sony. Sorrell told *Retro Gamer* that the part of Millennium that had built CyberLife "was rather more concerned with developing that technology than in developing regular games", and wanted to sell the games division. He arranged a meeting with the team's Sony producer, "and a few months later we became Sony's second United Kingdom development studio". In September 1997 *PlayStation Power* reported that Sony had bought "a company called Cyberlife whose previous best effort was *Creatures*". *MediEvil* came out for Halloween 1998.

The studio stayed in Cambridge as part of Sony. Sorrell, who spent 13 years at Millennium and Sony, went on to *MediEvil II*, *Primal* and a game of the television series *24* there. Under its later name, Guerrilla Cambridge, the studio closed in January 2017; *Wireframe* noted that it had been running for 19 years. The *James Pond* games went their own way: in 2007 the licence belonged to Gameware Development, run by Millennium's former development director Ian Saunter, which had ported *Robocod* to the Game Boy Advance, Nintendo DS and PlayStation.
