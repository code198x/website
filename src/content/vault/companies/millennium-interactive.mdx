Millennium's catalogue is short and unusually focused: **a fish in a suit**. *James Pond*, *James Pond 2: Robocod* and *The Aquatic Games* are one character worked three ways, and they belong to a specific moment — the years after *Sonic the Hedgehog* when every publisher in Europe wanted a mascot.

## Fast facts

- **Press evidence in this library:** reviewed in *[Amiga Power](/vault/magazines/amiga-power)* from **1991 to 1994**, including AGA versions of *Robocod* and *Diggers*.
- **Published:** *James Pond*, *James Pond 2: Robocod*, *The Aquatic Games*, *Diggers*, *Brutal Sports Football*, *Morph*, *Wild Cup Soccer*.
- **Developers credited by *Amiga Power*:** Vectordean, Chris Sorrell, Teque.

## The mascot problem

A mascot is not a character; it is **a piece of commercial infrastructure**. Its job is to be recognisable on a box at ten feet, work across sequels, survive being merchandised, and give a company something a competitor cannot copy. Sega demonstrated in 1991 what one was worth, and everybody spent the following three years trying to manufacture theirs.

Almost all of them failed, and the reason is worth naming. **Nobody adopts a mascot because a marketing department has one.** Sonic worked because *Sonic the Hedgehog* was a genuinely superb game that arrived free in the box with a console people were buying anyway. That is a distribution accident as much as a design achievement, and it cannot be reproduced by getting the character design right.

James Pond came closer than most British attempts, largely because *Robocod* is a good platform game, not a good idea for one — and it eventually reached exactly the position that makes mascots: bundled, in the box, with hardware.

## Diggers, and the CD32

*Diggers* is the other half of the story. It shipped with Commodore's CD32 in 1993, and inherits the awkwardness of everything designed to be a launch title: it had to show off the machine and be a game, and those pull in different directions.

The CD32 arrived months before Commodore collapsed, which makes its pack-in software an oddly precise marker. *Diggers* is what the Amiga's future was supposed to look like, frozen at the point the future stopped.

## Why it mattered

Millennium is **the Amiga chasing the consoles**. By 1992 the machine's commercial fight was with the Mega Drive and the SNES, and a good deal of what British publishers made in those years is best understood as an answer to that pressure: mascots, bright platformers, sports games, CD.

That the answer failed is not the interesting part. What is interesting is that a Cambridge publisher could look at what Sega had done and set about building the same thing, on a computer, with a fish — and get close enough that the game is still remembered by the people who played it.
