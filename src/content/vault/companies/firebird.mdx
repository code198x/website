In November 1984 a new publisher advertised twenty games at £2.50 each, with a promise printed on every box: "Seeing is believing". The screenshots on the inlays were real. The publisher was British Telecom. *CRASH*, reviewing its first Spectrum game, explained the name: Firebird was "the evil alter ego" of Buzby, the cartoon bird in BT's television adverts. Six months later the magazine visited its offices for a profile titled "The Boys from Buzby", admitting that it was "not immediately obvious why BT should wish to be 'The Power Behind the Joystick'".

## A phone company goes into games

*CRASH*'s answer was that BT was already in electronic publishing, through Prestel, Micronet and Gamestar. Dr Ederyn Williams, General Manager of BT's New Information Services Division, set up Firebird in the spring of 1984, and in May it started advertising for programs. Nearly two thousand arrived in the first three months. Tony Rainbird left his own label, Micro-Gold, to join in June, and James Leavey transferred from elsewhere in Telecom in July to run publicity. A marketing analyst, Theresa Jackson, surveyed the market that summer and backed the plan for two price tiers, Silver and Gold.

Firebird's head, James Scoular, formerly publisher of *Personal Computer Games*, died of a heart attack shortly before the profile appeared.

## The Silver range

The Silver range went on sale in the autumn of 1984. The November advertisements listed twenty titles across the Spectrum, Commodore 64, VIC-20 and BBC Micro, "all available at the remarkably low price of £2.50". The first *CRASH* Smash was *Booty*, John F. Cain's pirate-ship platform game, which *CRASH* thought "would easily have sold for £5.95". By May 1985 it had passed 100,000 sales.

Leavey explained the screenshots: customers were "getting disillusioned" by "hyped up artwork on the cassette inlay, and over the top claims in the blurb", so Firebird printed what the game looked like. Tony Rainbird argued that "a smaller profit per game, when it was achieved on a high volume of sales, could result in a larger profit overall"; *CRASH* compared it to selling tins of beans. Firebird aimed to keep 24 Silver titles on sale at a time, replacing eight or twelve every three months with better games or dropping the slowest sellers, and said it might also take on full-price games from other publishers that had "exhausted the market at that level".

It did not hire programmers. "We haven't any in-house programmers," Leavey said. Silver games came from freelancers, and Firebird would lend them money for equipment or pay royalties in advance. By mid-1986 Silver games cost £1.99, the same as [Mastertronic](/vault/companies/mastertronic)'s, and by 1988 the budget label was called Silverbird.

## Don't Buy This

Firebird also published its rejects. *Don't Buy This* collected five Spectrum games "which didn't quite make the Silver Range". *CRASH* called it "a masterpiece of anti-hype": the inlay suggested using the tape as a blank and disclaimed responsibility for "the total unplayability of the games it contains". Leavey told *Computer & Video Games*: "They are a good joke. You've got to have a sense of humour." *CRASH* gave it 39%.

*ACE* remembered it fondly in December 1988 as "a deliberate laugh in the early days of Firebird, when all they published was budget software". It described a dog that had to duck flying obstacles, "and to duck it had to stand up because it was tallest when it sat down", and a go-kart game "written by Tony Rainbird (the man who went on to set up Rainbird software for Telecomsoft), where the controls reversed as you went round a bend on the track".

## Gold, Hot and Elite

Herbie Wright joined from VNU at the start of 1985 to run the Gold range. "A Gold game has to be special," he said; "it's got to have Ingredient X." The first Gold releases were *Demons of Topaz* on the Commodore 64 and *Buggy Blast* on the Spectrum at £5.95. *Gyron*, at £9.95, came with the chance to win a Porsche.

As *CRASH*'s profile went to press in May 1985, Firebird was releasing *[Elite](/vault/games/elite)* for the Commodore 64 on disk at £17.95, with a Spectrum version promised for late summer. Acornsoft had published the original BBC Micro game in 1984; Firebird published it for other machines. *CRASH* gave the Spectrum version 92%. A mid-price Hot Range at £7.95 followed at the end of 1985.

## Telecomsoft: Beyond and Rainbird

BT's software business grew beyond Firebird. *ZZAP!64* reported in April 1986 that [Beyond](/vault/companies/beyond-software) "was the first to be captured", and that Tony Rainbird, by then an ex-Firebird executive, "has his own label, Rainbird, another BT investment". [Rainbird](/vault/companies/rainbird) published full-price games such as *[Starglider](/vault/games/starglider)*, and at the Consumer Electronics Show in 1986 it announced deals with the adventure writers [Level 9](/vault/companies/level-9) and [Magnetic Scrolls](/vault/companies/magnetic-scrolls). *CRASH* noted that Beyond "was the only other wing of British Telecom present — Firebird stayed at home on the nest". Tony Rainbird, "the founder who loaned his name to the company", resigned in November 1986, and Paula Byrne, who had joined as marketing manager that April, was by early 1987 managing Rainbird jointly with a colleague. By 1989 *ACE* described her as "at the helm" of Telecomsoft.

In September 1987 [Telecomsoft](/vault/companies/telecomsoft) signed [Graftgold](/vault/companies/graftgold), the team of [Steve Turner](/vault/people/steve-turner) and [Andrew Braybrook](/vault/people/andrew-braybrook), away from [Hewson Consultants](/vault/companies/hewson-consultants). Hewson had been preparing to publish Braybrook's *Morpheus* and Turner's *Magnetron*. *ACE* reported that a High Court injunction had settled the dispute for the time being, restraining Hewson from publishing the games "until the case can be tried", and that "Firebird will get *Magnetron* while *Morpheus* goes to sister label Rainbird".

## Sold to MicroProse

In 1989 the American publisher [MicroProse](/vault/companies/microprose) bought Telecomsoft. Its UK managing director, Stuart Bell, told *ACE* he was confident that Firebird and Rainbird would keep releasing games, but was less sure what to do with Silverbird: "It's a big jump from there down to the £1.99 category." *ACE* repeated a rumour that MicroProse had paid only £400,000 for the whole business, and that rumours of heavy losses had been denied. Games continued to appear with "Firebird/MicroProse" on the label, among them *Rick Dangerous* and *Rainbow Islands*, reviewed by *ACE* in November 1989.
