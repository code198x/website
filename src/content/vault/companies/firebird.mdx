Firebird was British Telecom's software label, set up in the spring of 1984 by
**Dr Ederyn Williams**, General Manager of BT's New Information Services
Division. It is a stranger thing than it now sounds — a nationalised telecoms
monopoly selling £2.50 games in newsagents — and *CRASH*, profiling them in May
1985 under the headline "The Boys from Buzby", said as much: it was not
immediately obvious why BT should want to be "The Power Behind the Joystick".

The magazine's own answer was that BT was already in electronic publishing
through Prestel, Micronet and Gamestar, so selling software in shops was not as
illogical a move as it first appeared.

## Fast facts
- **Founded:** spring 1984, by BT's New Information Services Division.
- **Launch price:** £2.50 — twenty titles available by November 1984.
- **Catchphrase:** "Seeing is believing".
- **Labels:** Firebird Silver (later Silverbird) for budget, [Rainbird](/vault/companies/rainbird) for premium.
- **Breakout release:** *[Elite](/vault/games/elite)*, published under the Firebird label.

**97% to 12%, under one owner.** No other publisher of the period had that range, because
no other publisher was running a rotating budget shelf and a premium adventure imprint out
of the same building.

## Open submissions, at scale
Firebird advertised for programs in May 1984 and, by its own account to *CRASH*,
received **nearly two thousand** in the first three months. That is the budget
model working as designed: no development studio, no advances, a wide net cast
across the bedroom-coder population, and an editorial filter applied afterwards.

**Tony Rainbird** was persuaded to leave his own label, Micro-Gold, and join in
June 1984 to get the operation moving. The premium **Rainbird** imprint carries
his name.

## Managing a budget range
Firebird treated the Silver range as inventory rather than a catalogue. Every
few months it planned to replace eight or twelve of the titles — dropping
whatever sold least and introducing better games to lift the average. It also
told *CRASH* it might take on games from other houses that had "exhausted the
market" at full price, which is budget republishing described plainly a decade
before the practice became routine.

By May 1985 the label had taken **three CRASH Smashes** in the few months it had
been operating, which is what prompted the profile.

## Three labels, one owner
Firebird was not BT's only games label. By 1986 the software operation ran three
imprints, and *CRASH*'s report from that April's Consumer Electronics Show
catches all of them in one paragraph: **Rainbird** used the show to announce
tie-ins with [Level 9](/vault/companies/level-9) and
[Magnetic Scrolls](/vault/companies/magnetic-scrolls), and
[Beyond](/vault/companies/beyond-software) was "the only other wing of British
Telecom present" — Firebird itself having "stayed at home on the nest".

| Label | Role |
|---|---|
| **Firebird** | Budget, from £2.50; Silver, later Silverbird |
| **Rainbird** | Premium — simulations and adventures at full price |
| **Beyond** | An established publisher absorbed into the group |

The consequence is worth stating plainly: for a stretch of the mid-1980s, the
two most respected British adventure houses and the publisher of *Lords of
Midnight* were all, corporately, the phone company.

## Don't Buy This

The open-submission pipeline has a punchline, and Firebird published it.

***Don't Buy This*** was a compilation of the **worst** games Firebird had been sent —
released deliberately, under that title, at budget price. *CRASH* gave it **39%**, which
is arguably generous. *ACE*, looking back in December 1988 in a feature on the industry's
great marketing disasters, described it with real affection:

> Released as a **deliberate laugh** in the early days of Firebird, when all they published
> was budget software, this was a collection of 'Sunday Sport' games — **games that were so
> bad, they were almost good**. There was a dog, that had to duck flying obstacles, and to
> duck it had to stand up because it was tallest when it sat down. Then there was the
> go-kart simulation… where the controls reversed as you went round a bend on the track. **A
> delicious set of awful games, sold under an honest title for a fair price.**

The go-kart game with the inverted steering was written by **Tony Rainbird** — the same
Tony Rainbird recruited to get the operation moving, and after whom the premium imprint
that published *The Pawn* and *Starglider* was named.

That is the two-thousand-submissions model laid bare. A label with an editorial filter has,
by definition, a pile of rejects. Firebird is the only publisher of the era that looked at
the pile, priced it, labelled it honestly, and put it on the shelf.

## Legacy
Firebird's output ran from £2.50 tapes to *Elite*, and the Rainbird imprint
carried simulations and adventures at full price. Telecomsoft, BT's software
arm, was sold in 1989 and the catalogue passed to MicroProse.

One acquisition is worth recording, because it took the top of a rival apart and ended in
court. **Graftgold** — Steve Turner and Andrew Braybrook, the authors on whom
[Hewson Consultants](/vault/companies/hewson-consultants) had built its reputation — signed
to Telecomsoft at the **PCW Show in September 1987**. *ACE* reported the aftermath in
February 1988 under the headline "BT GET GRAFTGOLD GAMES": rights to *Morpheus* and
*Magnetron* were "disputed fiercely" until **BT successfully restrained Hewson in the High
Court** from publishing games it had already geared up to produce. The spoils were split
between the labels — "**Firebird will get *Magnetron* while *Morpheus* goes to sister label
[Rainbird](/vault/companies/rainbird)**".

⚠ *The One*'s October 1991 retrospective dates this to 1989. It is about two years out:
*ACE* and *CRASH* both report the signing and the resulting dispute contemporaneously, in
**1987–88**, and the contemporary reporting is the better record.

"Defection" is *The One*'s word, and it reads cleaner than the record. Braybrook's own
development diary, printed in *Amiga Action*, shows Graftgold contracted to Telecomsoft for
*Rainbow Islands* from September 1988 to June 1989 **and** contracted by Hewson to produce
*Paradroid* on ST and Amiga immediately afterwards. The studio was working for both.

*The One*'s verdict on the consequences still stands, whatever its dating: Hewson "never
really recovered", and "**Telecomsoft never really recovered either — its labels were sold to
MicroProse not long afterwards**.

The label matters here because it is the clearest case of a large institution
running the bedroom-coder pipeline deliberately: an advert, two thousand
submissions, a filter, and a rotating shelf.
