"How do you explain your incredible success the last few years?" *Computer Gaming World* asked Feargus Urquhart in November 2000. By then Interplay's role-playing division had made or published *Fallout 2*, *Baldur's Gate*, *Planescape: Torment* and *Icewind Dale* in three years, and was about to release *Baldur's Gate II*. Urquhart's answer was organisation: in 1996 Interplay had split its development into divisions, and "with the founding of the Black Isle division, we were able to focus on making RPGs and only RPGs". *PC Gamer* later judged that the studio "utterly dominated the RPG genre for almost five years". Three years after that interview it was gone.

## Fast facts

- **Parent:** [Interplay](/vault/companies/interplay), Irvine, California.
- **Set up:** 1996, as Interplay's role-playing division.
- **Division director:** Feargus Urquhart, until he left in 2003.
- **Developed:** *Fallout 2* (1998), *[Planescape: Torment](/vault/games/planescape-torment)* (1999), *[Icewind Dale](/vault/games/icewind-dale)* (2000) and its sequel, *Baldur's Gate: Dark Alliance II* (2004).
- **Published:** [BioWare](/vault/companies/bioware)'s *[Baldur's Gate](/vault/games/baldurs-gate)* (1998) and *Baldur's Gate II* (2000), and games by other developers.
- **Closed:** December 2003, when Interplay laid off its staff.

## Fallout and the new label

Interplay had made role-playing games since *The Bard's Tale* and *[Wasteland](/vault/games/wasteland)* in the 1980s, but by the mid-1990s, *PC Gamer*'s Desslock wrote, it had "lost its way", and a reorganisation "created its Black Isle Studios division to focus on RPG development". The programmer Dan Spitzley told *Retro Gamer* that "Black Isle didn't exist yet" when he joined Interplay, and that Urquhart was head of the "then-fledgling" division when he offered him the lead programmer's job on *Planescape: Torment*.

*[Fallout](/vault/games/fallout)* (1997) was developed and published by Interplay and sold under Interplay's name. Urquhart later counted its team among Black Isle's staff: "after *Fallout*", he said, a number of Black Isle employees left for Troika Games, founded by key members of the *Fallout* team (see [Tim Cain](/vault/people/tim-cain)). The Black Isle name went on the box with *Fallout 2*, advertised in December 1998 as a Black Isle Studios production, a year after the first game.

## The Infinity Engine games

The partnership that made the name came from outside. Interplay published *Baldur's Gate* (1998), developed by BioWare in Edmonton, under the Black Isle label, and its success, more than a million copies, gave Black Isle an engine as well as a hit. Urquhart told *Computer Gaming World* that the studio had "been very lucky in our relationship with BioWare, in that we've been able to work very closely with them and with the engine they created for the original *Baldur's Gate*, the BioWare Infinity Engine". His other explanation was restraint: the studio had been "relatively careful about the technological scope of our products", putting its effort into content "in engines that are as new as possible—but are not on the bleeding edge".

The first Black Isle game on that engine was *Planescape: Torment* (1999). Interplay held a licence for TSR's Planescape setting that it had not used, and Chris Avellone, a writer and designer who had been hired into the new division, said that at his interview he had been asked how he would begin a Planescape game: "I'd start with the player waking from the dead in the Mortuary." That is how the finished game opens. *Computer Gaming World*'s Thierry Nguyen called it "half game, half mytho-poetic meditation" and "a role-playing game without peer": the player's alignment was set by what the character did, not chosen at the start, and statistics such as intelligence changed what could be said in conversations.

*Icewind Dale* (2000) used the same engine for an adventure in the frozen north of the Forgotten Realms, starting in the Ten Towns. There were no ready-made heroes: the player created the whole party from scratch, and David Ogden Stiers narrated. *Computer Gaming World*'s reviewer called it "a cup of refreshingly cold ice water in the vast desert of the RPG genre". An expansion, *Heart of Winter*, followed in 2001, and *Icewind Dale II* in 2002. On consoles, the Black Isle label went on Snowblind Studios' *Baldur's Gate: Dark Alliance* (2001), and the division developed its sequel itself.

## Decline

Black Isle's games sold, but Interplay did not. *PC Gamer* traced the decline through Interplay's debts: expensive 3D technology licensed for games that were cancelled, including *Stonekeep 2* and *Torn*; the loss of the publishing rights to BioWare's *Neverwinter Nights*, which Black Isle had been due to publish; and a new Dungeons & Dragons game shelved after months of work. *Fallout 3*, code-named Van Buren, had been in sporadic development since 1999. Urquhart and several senior developers left in 2003 to found [Obsidian Entertainment](/vault/companies/obsidian-entertainment), and in November J. E. Sawyer, lead designer of the *Icewind Dale* games and reportedly of Van Buren, resigned. *Lionheart: Legacy of the Crusader* (2003), a Black Isle-published role-playing game developed by Reflexive Entertainment, was the division's only PC release that year. *Computer Gaming World* reviewed it harshly and wrote that "Black Isle Studios, as we knew it, is done".

In December 2003 Interplay laid off the whole division. *Computer Gaming World* reported that members of Black Isle "said the entire Black Isle staff has been laid off and the division has been dissolved", while it was working on *Fallout 3*; an anonymous source told the magazine that Interplay was likely to sell the *Fallout* licence.

## Later history

Black Isle's people carried on elsewhere. Obsidian made *[Fallout: New Vegas](/vault/games/fallout-new-vegas)* (2010) for Bethesda Softworks, which had taken over the series (see [Fallout](/vault/games/fallout)). Troika, founded earlier by *Fallout*'s developers, made *Arcanum* and *The Temple of Elemental Evil*. When it became clear that Black Isle would never make *Fallout 3*, *Edge* noted, a 208-page "Fallout Bible" of the series' background was released. Interplay revived the Black Isle name in 2012 for a new role-playing project, *Project V13*, but its crowdfunding campaign did not raise enough to make the game.
