## Overview

**Dragon Data Ltd** was a Welsh computer manufacturer that produced the Dragon 32 and 64 home computers from 1982-1984. Based in south Wales, the company briefly challenged the Spectrum and C64 before competition and underfunding forced its closure.

## Fast Facts

- **Founded:** 1982
- **Location:** Swansea Industrial Estate, Swansea (the address printed in 1982 advertising); later Port Talbot
- **Products:** Dragon 32, Dragon 64
- **Closed:** 1984
- **Legacy:** Welsh computing heritage

## The toy company behind it

The Dragon's origins are missing from most short accounts of it, and the period advertising
states them outright. Every 1982 Dragon 32 advertisement in *Computer & Video Games* ends
with the same line of small print:

> **A member of the Mettoy Group of Companies.**

*Mettoy* — the Northampton toy manufacturer behind Corgi die-cast cars. *CVG*'s own review
that November puts it plainly: "**With a toy company behind it, Wales' very own Dragon 32
microcomputer should be a games-player's machine. It is!**" — before adding, in the next
breath, "But despite all the careful thought that has gone into it, there are reservations."

Those same advertisements give the company's address as **Queensway, Swansea Industrial
Estate, Swansea, Glamorgan SA5 4EH** — worth noting, because Dragon Data is usually placed
at Port Talbot. Both are the company's, but Swansea is the address the 1982 press carries.

## Business history, as the press recorded it

*Your Computer*, February 1988, ran a retrospective on the machines that "broke their
companies' backs" and gave the Dragon the fullest account of the sequence:

> The Dragon 32 was **launched by Mettoy in July, 1982**… Towards the end of 1982, **Mettoy
> had to sell the rights to the Dragon as its toy business slid downhill.** Dragon Data,
> established originally as a Mettoy subsidiary, **bought the machines and set up in Wales as
> an independent company.** Poor sales in the summer of 1983 led to a rescue package being
> put together by **Prutech** and then, in early 1984, **GEC took over sales and marketing**
> of the 32… **Dragon Data went into receivership in June, 1984 and its rights were bought a
> month later by Spanish company Eurohard.**

| Date | Event |
|------|-------|
| **July 1982** | Dragon 32 launched — by **Mettoy**, not Dragon Data |
| **Late 1982** | Mettoy's toy business fails; Dragon Data buys the machines and goes independent |
| **1983** | Dragon 64 launched; poor summer sales bring a **Prutech** rescue package |
| **Early 1984** | **GEC** takes over sales and marketing of the 32 |
| **June 1984** | Receivership |
| **July 1984** | Rights bought by **Eurohard** (Spain) |

The same piece names the machine that was announced and never shipped: alongside the Dragon
64 the company promised **an MSX-specification machine, "which did not" appear**.

## How close it came

The retrospective's verdict is more generous than the entry's summary, and worth preserving
because it is contemporary:

> Of all the machines which broke their companies' backs, **the Dragon 32 was probably
> closest to being an outright success**, with **sales apparently approaching 100,000** and a
> respectable software catalogue in its heyday.

⚠ That figure is the magazine's, hedged with "apparently", written four years after the
fact. Treat it as a period estimate, not a verified total.

## The receivership, reported live

*Personal Computer News* covered the collapse in its issue of **16 June 1984**, and the
detail that survives best is the size of the unsold stock — and who came sniffing:

> There was restrained optimism over the fate of Dragon Data last week following its
> announcement that it had **called in a receiver.** Several companies, **including Tandy**,
> have expressed an interest in buying some or all of the company and it looks unlikely that
> users will end up unsupported.

The report's headline figure: Dragon 32s and 64s to the value of "**£4 to £6 million's worth
unsold**".

[Tandy](/vault/companies/tandy)'s interest is the joke of the whole affair. The Dragon was
built around the same Motorola 6809 and Microsoft BASIC as Tandy's TRS-80 Color Computer,
close enough that software crossed between them. The British machine built in the CoCo's
image ended its life with the CoCo's manufacturer weighing up whether to buy the wreckage.

## Dragon Data as a software publisher

The entry treats Dragon Data purely as a hardware manufacturer. It also published games —
badged **DRAGON DATA** in the review columns of the day — and *Dragon User* was not soft on
them. In March 1984 it reviewed the graphic adventure *White Crystal* and turned most of the
review into a complaint about the box:

> Dragon Data has produced the **worst example of over-packaging** I have encountered. The
> single cassette is contained in a **full-sized video-cassette box**, more than nine inches
> in size, and over one and a half inches thick. The instruction booklet is A5 in size, yet
> the text could be fitted on one page.

The charge underneath it was **£12.95**, "which is rather excessive" — for a game written in
BASIC. *Big K*'s verdict on the company's *Starfighter* that April was similar in shape:
"**Like a lot of Dragon software, it's a compromise. The ingredients for a great game are
there but none is fully exploited.**"

## Why Dragon Data failed

| Factor | Evidence |
|--------|----------|
| **A parent in trouble** | Mettoy's toy business slid before the computer had a chance; the rights were sold within months of launch |
| **Serial rescues** | Prutech in 1983, GEC in early 1984 — two changes of backer in under a year |
| **Unsold inventory** | £4–6m of stock in the warehouse at receivership |
| **Competition** | Spectrum cheaper; C64 better equipped for games |
| **Graphics** | The 6847 VDG's palette drew complaints from the first reviews — *CVG* found the 16 colours "have a slightly muddy quality", and *Your Computer* remembered "that ubiquitous shade of green" |

## Legacy

Dragon Data represented Welsh ambition in the computer industry during the home computer boom. Though brief, the company introduced the superior 6809 architecture to UK hobbyists and created machines that maintain devoted followings today.
