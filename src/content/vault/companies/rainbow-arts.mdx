In the late 1980s Rainbow Arts was the German label whose games British magazines kept giving top marks. Two of them never made it to British shops. *The Great Giana Sisters* was withdrawn in 1988 after Nintendo objected to its likeness to *Super Mario Bros.*, and *Katakis* was stopped weeks later because Activision thought it looked too much like *R-Type*. The same team came back with *Turrican*, which *ZZAP!* gave 97% in 1990. Rainbow Arts also gave the composer [Chris Hülsbeck](/vault/people/chris-huelsbeck) his first job, and it launched programmers who went on to found [Factor 5](/vault/companies/factor-5) and Blue Byte.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1984 or 1985 (accounts differ), in Gütersloh, by Marc Alexander Ullrich.
- **Moved:** to Düsseldorf in 1988, with 20 permanent staff.
- **Owner:** one of several labels sold through the Softgold group, alongside Time Warp, Golden Goblins and Reline.
- **UK distribution:** U.S. Gold, from 1988.
- **People:** [Manfred Trenz](/vault/people/manfred-trenz), Andreas Escher, [Chris Hülsbeck](/vault/people/chris-huelsbeck), Ramiro Vaca, Hans Ippisch, Armin Gessert, Thomas Hertzler.
- **Notable games:** *The Great Giana Sisters* (1987), *Katakis* (1988), *X-Out* (1990), *[Turrican](/vault/games/turrican)* (1990), *Turrican II* (1991), *Mad TV* (1991).
- **End:** a label of Softgold under THQ by 1999.

## Gütersloh and the C64

The German magazine *GO64!* told the company's history in 2000. Marc Alexander Ullrich started the label with application programs for the Commodore 64 and Amstrad CPC, such as a vocabulary trainer and a disk-drive alignment program, before it became a limited company (GmbH) in Gütersloh and moved into games. The magazine's reasoning was that Germany had programmers, artists, musicians and plenty of computer owners, "but no software company that could be taken serious"; British imports ruled the market.

The early staff became the company's best-known names. Hülsbeck wrote the music for almost all the first games. Hans Ippisch wrote *Bad Cat*, whose PC version *GO64!* called the first game for IBM compatibles from a German company. [Manfred Trenz](/vault/people/manfred-trenz) from Saarbrücken joined in 1987 as a graphic artist and was soon programming. Thomas Hertzler, a project manager, programmed the Amiga *Giana Sisters*; he left in 1988 and co-founded Blue Byte.

Rainbow Arts was never quite independent. *CRASH* explained to its readers in December 1988 that it was "one of a group of labels marketed by German trade agency Softgold", and that U.S. Gold distributed its software in Britain. The Softgold family also included Time Warp Productions, Golden Goblins and Reline Software. In 1988 Rainbow Arts moved to Düsseldorf and grew to 20 permanent employees.

## The Great Giana Sisters

*The Great Giana Sisters* (1987) was the company's first big hit in Germany. Armin Gessert programmed the C64 version with graphics by Trenz, and Hülsbeck wrote the music. Nobody pretended it was original. *Commodore Computing International* previewed it in May 1988 as "a version of the superb Nintendo game 'Super Mario Brothers'". Giana jumped on monsters and headbutted bricks for bonuses, and *ACE* noted in August 1988 that she looked "more than a little like *Super Mario Brothers*". *ACE* still rated it 701 on the C64 and 713 on the Atari ST and Amiga. U.S. Gold released it in Britain on its GO! label, and *CRASH* made the Spectrum version a CRASH Smash in August 1988.

By October it was gone. *CRASH* told readers who were "scouring the streets" for the game to stop, and quoted Dave Baxter of Rainbow Arts' Birmingham office: "Nintendo are getting heavy." In December *ACE* reported that "US Gold withdrew *Great Giana Sisters* following pressure from Nintendo who felt it looked a bit too much like *Super Mario Brothers*." *Computer and Video Games* added that a sequel, *Giana Sisters in the Future World*, would not be released after Nintendo objected with the threat of legal action. It wondered why Nintendo had not reacted as strongly to the first game.

The Spectrum version never went on sale. *CRASH* said so in December 1988 and again in its review of the year: "legal action by Nintendo ensured the game would sadly never be released." Copies of the C64 and Amiga versions that had reached the shops, and pirated copies, kept it in circulation. The next platform game from the Softgold stable, *Hard'n'Heavy* (1989, on the Reline label), came from the same programming team, and *GO64!* noted that the credits carried only pseudonyms "to avoid any trouble with Nintendo this time".

## Katakis and R-Type

*Katakis* was Manfred Trenz and Andreas Escher's first game together, a horizontally scrolling shoot-'em-up for the C64 that both of them had based on their favourite arcade games. "Back then – *R-Type* and *Darius*, no doubt about it," Escher told *GO64!*. It left the building before it was finished. *Commodore User* reported in June 1988 that the German cracking group [Triad](/vault/groups/triad) was spreading a pre-release copy. *ACE* reported that one demo "apparently featured a message from the programmers to the effect of thank you to *R-Type* and the inspiration it has given us".

[Activision](/vault/companies/activision) held the British home licence for Irem's *[R-Type](/vault/games/r-type)*. In October 1988 *CRASH* reported that *Katakis* "has also had the brakes put on it and will never see the light of day", because Activision thought it looked "a bit too much like R-Type". U.S. Gold withdrew the game, as the [Irem entry](/vault/companies/irem#r-type-at-home) describes. Escher's version is that "various minor similarities were more or less imminent", and that Rainbow Arts made a deal: "As a compensation, we had the C64 version of 'R-Type' finished in only six weeks." *GO64!* adds that Activision objected mainly to the second level. *Katakis* was reissued in 1989 as *Denaris*, with a new second level. Factor 5, which had written the Amiga *Katakis*, went on to program the official Amiga *R-Type*.

*ACE* put both withdrawals in a longer history in December 1988. Cloning arcade games had been normal in Britain for years. "Everyone was doing versions of arcade games, so you just joined in," said the publisher Andrew Hewson. The difference in 1988, as *ACE* saw it, was that the big publishers had now paid for arcade licences and were prepared to defend them.

## Turrican and the Softgold years

Trenz's next game was *[Turrican](/vault/games/turrican)*, previewed at the start of 1990 as *Hurrican* and renamed at his request. *ZZAP!* gave the C64 version a Gold Medal and 97% in May 1990, and Factor 5's Amiga version had 94% in August. Rainbow Arts also looked outside for staff: it hired Boris Schneider and Martin Gaksch, two editors of the magazine *Power Play*, and signed a co-operation agreement with Lucasfilm. It also tried the CD early. Its *First CD Edition* put ten C64 games on an audio CD, read through an adapter plugged into the cassette port.

After *Turrican II* and the puzzle game *Logical*, the label left the C64. Its later games included *Mad TV* (1991). In 1994 advertisements still named Rainbow Arts as "a label of Softgold Computerspiele GmbH", then of Kaarst, and it launched a Rainbow Kids label for educational games. *Edge* said in 1995 that Rainbow Arts had "only recently started developing again", and credited it with "laying the foundations of the German games industry with Factor 5 and its *Turrican* series".

## Under THQ

By 1999 Softgold belonged to the American publisher THQ, and German magazines listed Rainbow Arts as "Rainbow Arts/THQ". The last game announced under the name was *Turrican 3D*, a new game by Manfred Trenz. In June 1999 *PC Player* expected it in the last quarter of that year, and it was still on the magazine's release lists in early 2001. THQ stopped its development, and it was never released.
