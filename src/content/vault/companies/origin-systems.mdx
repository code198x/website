## Overview
"We create worlds"—Origin's motto was earned. Richard Garriott's Ultima series defined computer RPGs. Wing Commander brought cinematic production to space combat. The company pushed storytelling, world-building, and production values. Then EA bought them, and the worlds stopped.

## Fast facts
- **Founded:** 1983 in Austin, Texas.
- **Founder:** Richard Garriott (Lord British).
- **Slogan:** "We create worlds."
- **EA acquisition:** 1992.
- **Closed:** 2004.
- **Notable games:** Ultima series, Wing Commander series, Ultima Online.

## Ultima's legacy
Richard Garriott's RPG series evolved over decades:
- **Early entries:** Pioneered open-world exploration.
- **Ultima IV (1985):** Ethical choices, virtue system—revolutionary.
- **Ultima VII (1992):** Living world simulation, peak of the series.
- **Ultima Online (1997):** Pioneered massively multiplayer RPGs.

Each game expanded what RPGs could be.

## Wing Commander phenomenon
1990's space opera:
- Cinematic cutscenes, branching narratives.
- Full voice acting in later entries (Mark Hamill, Malcolm McDowell).
- Production values matching Hollywood ambitions.
- Sold millions, spawned sequels and a film.

## How Britain bought Ultima

Origin was an Austin company that never had a British arm, so the *Ultima* series reached
this country through whichever local publisher held the distribution — and it changed hands.

Through the late eighties the route ran through **[MicroProse](/vault/companies/microprose)**:
full-page advertisements for *Ultima II* to *V* carry the Tetbury, Gloucestershire address
and invite readers to write there "for information on the Origin range". By 1990 the same
catalogue was being advertised by **[Mindscape](/vault/companies/mindscape) International
Limited, PO Box 1019, Lewes, East Sussex**.

A flight-simulator house in the Cotswolds, then a publisher in a Sussex market town. Neither
had anything to do with making the games, and for most British players in the period, one of
those two names was the one on the box.

## "We create worlds" — and the badge that came before Wing Commander

The motto is real and it is period: it appears in Origin's advertising from the late
eighties onward, always in the same place, under the logo.

What sits beside it is more surprising. This entry credits **Wing Commander** (1990) with
bringing cinematic production to Origin. The **"ORIGIN Cinematic Experience"** trademark was
in use before that, and it was attached to something else — *Space Rogue*, advertised in
*ACE* in March 1990 as

> the seamless integration of space flight and role playing that makes **SPACE ROGUE the
> first ORIGIN Cinematic Experience™**.

*Space Rogue* is the forgotten one — a space-combat-and-trading game with "authentic flight
dynamics, precision navigational aids", space stations and mining outposts, "swapping tales
with pirates… and goods with merchants". The cinematic ambition the company is remembered
for was branded, and shipping, on a game almost nobody names.

## The software house of 1990

Origin's peak was recognised at the time and stated plainly. *ACE*, February 1991:

> With two Ultima titles and Chris Roberts's outstanding Wing Commander, Origin was on a
> roll. **Many believe this Texas-based company to be the software house of 1990.**

Its December 1990 issue had already said Origin "must be developing a strong claim" to
Software House of the Year, on the grounds that "**Ultima VI transformed the Ultima series
with yet another quantum leap in playability**" and then Roberts "brought us the superlative
Wing Commander".

The visiting journalist also recorded the physical fact of the company at that moment:
Origin's **16,000 square foot air-conditioned building in Austin**. For a studio that had
started with Garriott selling games in ziplock bags, that building is the measure of the
decade.

## Wing Commander was made by a Briton

Worth stating in a Vault written from this side of the Atlantic. *ACE*, December 1990,
introduces him as "**the exBrit creator of the world's first cinematic space combat
simulator**" — [Chris Roberts](/vault/people/chris-roberts), who had cut his teeth on
British 8-bit machines before going to Texas.

The same feature breaks down how the game was staffed, which is a useful corrective
to "cinematic" as a vague compliment:

| Role | People |
|---|---|
| Programming | **Chris Roberts plus four other programmers** |
| Graphics | **Four artists** on the bitmapped screens |
| Audio | **One** sound-effects specialist |
| Scenario | **Three writers** |

Roberts on how it was meant to be played: "**You can develop your own style and tactics in
Wing Commander… Afterburners are the key to successfully winning the fights.**" And the
magazine's note on length — "the absolute minimum time to complete the game, **if you don't
screw up, is eight hours solid playing**".

## The designer this entry leaves out

Origin's entry cannot reasonably run without **[Warren Spector](/vault/people/warren-spector)**,
who is not mentioned anywhere above. *ACE* found him in February 1991 producing *Martian
Dreams*, the second of the *Worlds of Ultima* games built on the *Ultima VI* engine, and
described him as "**one of the most experienced role-playing game designers and writers in
America**" — a reputation he had before Origin, having "**worked for Steve Jackson Games and
TSR**, the company responsible for the Dungeons & Dragons series of products".

His own description of the job, delivered from the most comfortable chair in the building:
"**I'm head whip-cracker.**"

⚠ Note the lineage. The tabletop houses trained the designers; Origin hired them; the
studio's reputation for worlds with "history, ethics, and consequence" is in large part
imported expertise, not spontaneous invention.

## EA acquisition
1992's buyout brought resources and constraints:
- More money for production.
- More pressure for returns.
- Creative freedom gradually eroded.
- Ultima VIII and IX disappointed fans.

## Ultima Online revolution
1997's MMO pioneered a genre:
- Persistent online world.
- Player economy, housing, guilds.
- Problems (griefing, exploits) that every MMO would face.
- Proved the model could work at scale.

## Closure
EA shuttered Origin in 2004:
- Ultima X cancelled.
- Staff dispersed.
- Properties remained with EA, largely dormant.
- Richard Garriott had already departed.

## Legacy
Origin proved games could be more than entertainment—they could be worlds with history, ethics, and consequence. Their ambitions exceeded what the industry could sustain, but they showed what to aim for.
