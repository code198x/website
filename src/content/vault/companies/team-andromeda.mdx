Team Andromeda was the [Sega](/vault/companies/sega) team behind the [Panzer Dragoon](/vault/games/panzer-dragoon) games on the [Saturn](/vault/systems/sega-saturn), and it made nothing else. In four years it built one world, a desert planet a thousand years past its golden age, and told three stories in it, each in a different kind of game. The first was a rail shooter for the Saturn's launch. The last was a role-playing game on four discs that came out in the West as the Saturn was dying, in such small numbers that copies later sold for over £100. *Edge* put its finger on what joined them: "It's a testament to Team Andromeda's versatility that the Panzer Dragoon series has shed its shallow-but-lavish shoot 'em up brief in favour of a complex RPG mandate."

## Fast facts

- **Part of:** [Sega](/vault/companies/sega)'s consumer development division in Japan.
- **Active:** 1994 to 1998.
- **Games:** *Panzer Dragoon* (Japan, March 1995), *Panzer Dragoon II Zwei* (Japan, March 1996), *Panzer Dragoon Saga* (Japan, January 1998; Europe and America, spring 1998), all for the Saturn.
- **People:** Yukio Futatsugi (director of *Panzer Dragoon*, main writer of *Saga*); Manabu Kusunoki (art director of *Panzer Dragoon*, general director of *Saga*); Tomohiro Kondo (director of *Zwei*); Kentaro Yoshida (art director of *Zwei*).
- **Size:** about 15 people for the first game, about 20 for *Zwei* and 40 for *Saga*.
- **Afterwards:** members went on to [Smilebit](/vault/companies/smilebit), which made *Panzer Dragoon Orta* (2002), and to [United Game Artists](/vault/companies/united-game-artists), which made *[Rez](/vault/games/rez)* (2001).

## A showpiece for the Saturn

Sega formed Team Andromeda in 1994 for one job: a game that would show what its new console could do. *Retro* magazine's history of the series puts the first team at about 15 people, and says they took 12 months to finish once they had settled on the idea. *Panzer Dragoon* came out in Japan in March 1995. *Edge* had heard it was "the most expensive console project Sega has ever undertaken".

The game put the player on the back of a flying dragon, moving through each level on a fixed path, and asked them to shoot in every direction at once, turning the view with the shoulder buttons. Its look came from a team that, *Retro* says, were "huge fans" of the French comic artist Moebius. Sega commissioned illustrations from him for the Japanese packaging. Futatsugi directed it, and Kusunoki was its art director and built its dragon models. For a fuller account of the game, see [the Panzer Dragoon entry](/vault/games/panzer-dragoon).

## Zwei

The second game was a prequel. *Retro* reports that the team grew to 20 and again took a full year; *Zwei* came out in Japan in March 1996, a little after the first game's anniversary. This time the dragon began as a flightless youngster and grew between levels, taking different forms depending on how well the player did, and a new Berserk attack helped in the busiest moments. *Edge* thought it "an unequivocal triumph of design over content", and credited its look to "a small group of Japanese CGI artists collectively working under Sega's Team Andromeda banner". Tomohiro Kondo directed it, with Kentaro Yoshida as art director; Futatsugi took the credit for its original design and setting.

## Saga

In September 1997 *Edge* reported Team Andromeda's "surprise project", *Azel: Panzer Dragoon RPG*. Work on it had started alongside *Zwei*, *Retro Gamer* later learned from Sega's own account, which explains why *Zwei* can recognise save data from a game released two years after it. The team doubled to 40. It also had a hard time: a team member died during development, and things went so badly that the whole team visited a shrine for a "purification".

*Panzer Dragoon Saga*, as it was called in the West, came out in Japan on 29 January 1998, on four CDs. The player could now fly anywhere in each area, with control of height, and land to explore villages on foot. Battles were turn-based, run on charging gauges like *Final Fantasy VII*'s, but the player could move the dragon around an enemy to reach its weak points, and change the dragon's shape between attack, defence, agility and spiritual strength. *Sega Saturn Magazine* was struck that the team told so much of its story with the game's own 3D engine rather than video. The credits put Kusunoki at the top as general director, with Futatsugi as main writer.

*Edge* gave it nine out of ten in April 1998, calling Team Andromeda's work "simply breathtaking" at its best, with "a creative depth only SquareSoft games can currently rival". Sega added English subtitles instead of re-recording the speech, which *Edge* recognised as saving money but preferred to "B-list actors reciting words they have little feeling for". It also saw what was coming: "It's a tragedy that the Saturn's standing will ensure Team Andromeda's adventure... will enjoy a fraction of its rival's success." *Retro* reports that only about 5,000 English-language copies went to each Western territory. *Retro Gamer* gives estimates of 5,000 to 30,000 for the English print run as a whole, and in 2013 it reported that the original source code had been lost.

## After Andromeda

The team broke up after *Saga*. Sega Retro, a fan-run wiki that cites Sega's records, describes Team Andromeda as a group inside Sega's consumer division CS1, dissolved after *Saga*'s release. Its people spread out. *Retro* reports that former members were among the founders of Smilebit, one of the development companies Sega set up in 2000, and Smilebit's *Jet Set Radio* producer told *Edge* that his team had worked on *Zwei* and *Azel*. Masayoshi Kikuchi, who co-designed *Saga*'s battles, became *Jet Set Radio*'s director. Smilebit returned to the series with *Panzer Dragoon Orta* for the Xbox in 2002. According to Sega Retro, many former members also worked on *Rez* at Tetsuya Mizuguchi's United Game Artists, among them the artist Katsumi Yokota, and Futatsugi left Sega in 1998.
