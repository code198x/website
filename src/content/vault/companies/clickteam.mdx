In the autumn of 1994 *PC Gamer* gave the games designer Gary Penn a preview copy of a new program and a week to make something with it. Half an hour after he started, he had a working game of *[Pong](/vault/games/pong)*. By the end of the week he had *[Space Invaders](/vault/games/space-invaders)* and was wrestling with *[Pac-Man](/vault/games/pac-man)*. The program was *Klik & Play*, and it made games without a line of code: you placed objects on a screen, played the game, and told it what should happen each time something collided. It was written by [François Lionet](/vault/people/francois-lionet), who had already given Atari ST and Amiga owners [STOS](/vault/tools/stos) and [AMOS](/vault/tools/amos), and Yves Lamoureux. The company they formed, Clickteam, has been making tools built on the same idea ever since.

## Fast facts

- **Founders:** François Lionet and Yves Lamoureux. Some modern accounts add a third founder, Francis Poulain.
- **Founded:** the public sources disagree, giving 1993 or 1994. *Klik & Play*, released in 1994, is credited to "Francois Lionet/Yves Lamoureux"; the Clickteam name is in print by 1999.
- **Country:** France.
- **First product:** *Klik & Play* (1994), published by [Europress](/vault/companies/europress) in Britain and [Maxis](/vault/companies/maxis) in America.
- **Later tools:** *The Games Factory* (1996), *Click & Create* (1996), *Multimedia Fusion* (1998), *Jamagic* (2001) and, much later, Clickteam Fusion.

## From AMOS to the PC

By 1994 the Amiga market was shrinking, and Lionet had moved on. In August 1994 *Amiga Shopper* reported that there was "currently no development being carried out on any AMOS products" at Europress, and that Lionet was "busy working on an as yet unnamed games-creating product for the PC". Europress described it as "object-oriented", needing no programming: "Even a novice will be able to create a complete game in a couple of hours just by pointing and clicking." The columnist thought it sounded "a bit like the Shoot-em-up Construction Kit".

It was *Klik & Play*, a program for Windows. AMOS and STOS had been easier forms of BASIC; *Klik & Play*, as *PC Gamer* put it, was "icon-driven". Europress published it in Britain. In America Maxis sold it as "The Revolutionary Instant Game Creator" for $54.95, with a copyright line that read "© 1994 Europress Software and Francois Lionet/Yves Lamoureux".

## How Klik & Play worked

A *Klik & Play* game had three parts. The **storyboard** listed the screens in order, from title screen to game over. The **level editor** placed backgrounds and objects on each screen and set how each object moved: under the player's control in eight directions, like a racing car or like a platform character, or on its own as a bouncing ball or along a fixed path. The **event editor** held the rules, each one a condition and an action.

The clever part was the step-through mode. You played the unfinished game, and whenever something happened that had no rule yet, such as an alien entering the play area, *Klik & Play* stopped and asked what should happen. Your answer became a rule, applied automatically from then on. The German magazine *PC Player* explained it with a collision between "Billy Buchhalter" (Billy the Bookkeeper) and "Mecki Monster": the program pauses and asks whether to play a scream, take away a life or ignore it.

Finished games could be saved as stand-alone Windows programs and given away freely. Anyone who wanted to sell one, even as shareware, had to join the "Klik & Play Licenceware Club", *PC Player* reported. The package came with 14 ready-made games to play and modify, and libraries of graphics and sounds.

## What the magazines made of it

The reviewers enjoyed how quickly it produced results and were clear about its limits. Penn's diary in *PC Gamer* recorded the high: "Half an hour later I am a god and the natives are sacrificing their young virgins in my honour." It also recorded the frustrations. Scrolling was "not impossible, but not viable", and his *Pac-Man* ghosts would not chase the player, because on his preview copy objects moving along a path could not home in on another object. "Learning the essence of *Klik & Play* doesn't take long," he wrote, "but learning how to actually make things work is time-consuming (and surprisingly enjoyable)."

*PC Player* found it "verblüffend einfach", astonishingly easy, to get visible results, but its biggest complaint was that enemies could not be given any intelligence: they could only follow fixed paths, which ruled out racing games against computer drivers, board games and sports games. In America, *Computer Gaming World*'s Paul Schuytema recommended it as one of the best tools he had found "to experiment, not with game programming, but game design", though he gave "a major thumbs down" to its name.

Two years later *PC Gamer* ran a contest in which staff members each made a game. Steve Owen used *Klik & Play* and was less charmed: "as limited a game creation program as you could reasonably expect", with "awkward and unforgiving options" and an "intense desire to crash".

There was even an international *Klik & Play* competition. In March 1995 *PC Zone* invited readers to send in games on floppy disk, with the magazine's best entry going to a UK final and the winner going on to face entries from France, Greece, Germany and the USA. Prizes included *Klik & Play* baseball jackets.

## The Games Factory, Click & Create and Multimedia Fusion

*Klik & Play* was followed in 1996 by *The Games Factory*, which kept the storyboard, level and event editors. Europress sold it in Britain, where mail-order advertisements in 1998 listed "Europress Games Factory" at £26.99 alongside Europress's GCSE revision titles. Also in 1996 came *Click & Create*, which took the game-making parts of *The Games Factory* and added features for multimedia applications.

The next version was going to be called *Click & Create 2*. According to *Retro Gamer*, its distributor, Corel, "ran into difficulties and sold a number of their titles to IMSI", and IMSI renamed it *Multimedia Fusion*, released in 1998. *Retro Gamer* called it "the true successor to *Klik & Play*". In 2004 the same magazine gave away the full Home Edition of *The Games Factory* on its cover disc, and reported that Clickteam was working on *Multimedia Fusion 2* and *The Games Factory 2*.

## Jamagic and after

By 1999 *Edge* was calling a new Clickteam 3D tool, JAMA3D, "the latest addition to ClickTeam's legendary stable". Clickteam's first scripting language since AMOS came with *Jamagic*, a 2D and 3D game-creation system. In September 2001 *Edge* listed it as "Publisher: ClickTeam", with Lionet and Lamoureux as its creators and a scripting language "broadly equivalent to JavaScript".

The event-editor line continued. Clickteam Fusion 2.5 followed *Multimedia Fusion 2* in 2013, and in 2014 Scott Cawthon used it to write *Five Nights at Freddy's*.

Lionet himself has mixed feelings about what he started. In 2021 he told *Wireframe* that tools teaching only logic do not teach coding: "I feel a bit guilty, because I created a script-free programming tool called *Klik & Play* in 1994. The vision of AOZ is to bring back the joy of actual, intricate coding."
