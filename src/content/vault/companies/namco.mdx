In 1974 Atari's Japanese subsidiary was losing money, and Nolan Bushnell wanted rid of it. In David Sheff's account, Sega, then a maker of jukeboxes and pinball tables, bid $50,000. Masaya Nakamura, whose company built coin-operated rides for children, offered $800,000, sixteen times as much. The bid was talked down to $500,050, and it still took Nakamura two years to pay off the debt that came with the company. Six years later his company, by then called Namco, released *[Pac-Man](/vault/games/pac-man)*.

## Fast facts
- **Founded:** 1955, by Masaya Nakamura, as Nakamura Manufacturing.
- **Early business:** coin-operated children's rides.
- **Into video games:** bought Atari Japan in 1974, which gave it the right to sell Atari's games in Japan.
- **Arcade hits:** *[Galaxian](/vault/games/galaxian)* (1979), *[Pac-Man](/vault/games/pac-man)* (1980), *[Galaga](/vault/games/galaga)* (1981), *[Dig Dug](/vault/games/dig-dug)* and *[Pole Position](/vault/games/pole-position)* (1982), *[Xevious](/vault/games/xevious)* (1983).
- **Home consoles:** one of Nintendo's first Famicom licensees, from 1984.
- **Later:** owned [Atari Games](/vault/companies/atari-games) from 1985 to 1987; built arcade boards on Sony's PlayStation hardware from 1994.

## Rides, then Atari

Nakamura started his company in 1955, and for its first two decades it built rides. *Edge*, visiting in 1994, described the start as "small beginnings, manufacturing kiddies' rides". Sheff says Nakamura saw Atari Japan as a way to "instantly expand his small kiddie-ride company". Sheff dates the purchase to 1972, the year Atari itself was founded; *Retro Gamer* and *Wireframe* both put it in 1974, when Atari was shedding its Japanese operation.

What Nakamura bought was a distribution business. The deal made his company the exclusive seller of Atari's games in Japan for ten years. It opened arcades full of Atari machines, bought the rights to other games, and, by Sheff's account, quintupled its earnings. It also kept Hide Nakajima, the manager Atari had hired to run its Japanese office. Nakajima built up Namco's international sales and in 1978 opened its American subsidiary, Namco America, across the road from Atari's old headquarters in Sunnyvale.

The company had used "Namco", short for Nakamura Manufacturing Company, as a brand since 1971, and took it as its formal name in 1977. Its first video games came soon after. Toru Iwatani, who joined in 1977, started on what *Edge* called "video pinball games": *Gee Bee*, *Bomb Bee* and *Cutie Q*. *Edge*'s 1994 list of Namco's arcade releases begins with *Galaxian* in October 1979.

## Galaxian and Pac-Man

*Galaxian* arrived in the wake of *[Space Invaders](/vault/games/space-invaders)*, and *Edge* later credited its colourful graphics with "injecting skilful gameplay into the overpopulated space invaders market". In America [Midway](/vault/companies/midway) built and sold it under licence, as it had *Space Invaders* for Taito. *Video Games* reported in December 1982 that Midway had sold 45,000 *Galaxian* machines, after 72,000 *Space Invaders*.

The next Namco game Midway took on did far better. Namco offered it *Puck-Man* in early 1980. Bally's president, Robert Mullane, did not want it ("Anyway, who plays a maze game?"), but Midway went ahead, and the game reached America as *Pac-Man*. By December 1982 Midway had built 190,000 *Pac-Man* and *Ms. Pac-Man* machines. Namco America's lawyer, Dennis Wood, licensed the characters for merchandise and the home rights to [Atari](/vault/companies/atari). The [Pac-Man](/vault/games/pac-man) entry tells that story.

The 1980s arcade list that followed includes *Galaga* (1981), *Dig Dug* (1982), *Pole Position* (1982), *Xevious* (1983), *Pac-Land* (1984), *Metro-Cross* (1985) and *[Rolling Thunder](/vault/games/rolling-thunder)* (1986). In America several arrived under other companies' names. Atari took *Dig Dug*, its first licence of another company's coin-op, and manufactured *Pole Position*: *Electronic Games* listed *Pole Position* under Atari at the top of its arcade chart through the autumn of 1983, while Atari's own advertisements added the small print that the game was "created and licensed by Namco".

## The Famicom

When Nintendo opened its Famicom to other companies, Sheff writes, Namco was the first company it contacted. With [Hudson](/vault/companies/hudson-soft) it became one of the first two licensees, and it was one of six early licensees allowed to manufacture their own cartridges. *Edge* put the date at 1984. The Famicom *Xevious* sold 1.5 million copies, and both Sheff and *Edge* report that it paid for a new office building, nicknamed the Xevious Building.

## Atari Games

After the 1983 crash, Warner split Atari. It sold the home computer and console business to [Jack Tramiel](/vault/people/jack-tramiel) and in 1985 sold control of the arcade business, Atari Games, to Namco for a little over $10 million, according to Sheff. Namco got the makers of *Marble Madness* and *[Gauntlet](/vault/games/gauntlet)*, a factory in Ireland and about 230 staff. In 1987 it sold the company on to Time Warner and a group led by Nakajima, who then left Namco's board. The [Atari](/vault/companies/atari) entry covers what happened next.

## How Namco reached Britain

British players met Namco's games through licensees, and for most of the 1980s the name on the box was the licensee's. Atari's software label, Atarisoft, advertised *Pole Position* for the Commodore 64, BBC Micro and ZX Spectrum in *CRASH* in January 1985, alongside *Galaxian* and *Ms. Pac-Man*. Namco appeared only in the trademark notice at the foot of the page.

By 1988 the originator was being named alongside the publisher. In January that year a [U.S. Gold](/vault/companies/us-gold) advertisement promised, for early 1988, "HALLS OF KYROS (SEGA), ROLLING THUNDER (NAMCO), ROADBLASTERS (ATARI GAMES)". The same month *CRASH*'s index credited *Metrocross* to "US Gold/Namco", beside *Pole Position* under Atarisoft alone, and its news pages announced Argus Press Software's *Pac-Land* as an "Atari/Namco coin-op conversion". *Pacmania* came from [Grandslam](/vault/companies/grandslam).

## PlayStation arcades

Namco never sold a console of its own. *Edge* reported in 1994 that it had signed with Sony in November 1993 to write games for the PlayStation, and in return would use PlayStation hardware in its arcade machines. The deal, *Edge* wrote, "allows the coin-op specialist to produce product for the home machine as well as develop arcade machines using the PlayStation technology". Namco was then one of only two companies building coin-ops on the PlayStation chipset; the other was [Konami](/vault/companies/konami).

The board that came out of it was the System 11, "jointly created by Sony and Namco", and its first game was [*Tekken*](/vault/games/tekken), which *Edge* previewed in January 1995 as an arcade game to be "eventually ... ported to the PlayStation". *Ridge Racer*, which ran on Namco's own System 22 arcade board, was converted for the home machine; in November 1994 *Edge* reported Namco's confirmation that it would be ready for the PlayStation's launch.

In 2005 Namco merged with the toy company Bandai.
