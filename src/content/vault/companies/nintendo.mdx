For most of its first century, Nintendo made playing cards. Fusajiro Yamauchi started the business in Kyoto in 1889, selling *hanafuda*, the Japanese flower cards, and as late as the 1960s cards were still what the company was known for. Its first home video game machines did not appear until 1977. Six years after that, its Family Computer went on sale in Japan, and it became the machine that brought home video games back to America after the [1983 crash](/vault/phenomena/1983-crash). By 1995 *Edge* could open a profile by pointing out that in 1991 Nintendo had "supplanted Toyota as Japan's most successful company", which it called "incredible for a firm formed in 1889 to produce hanafuda playing cards".

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1889, Kyoto, by Fusajiro Yamauchi, making *hanafuda* cards.
- **Names:** Marufuku Co., Ltd. (1947); Nintendo Playing Card Co., Ltd. (1951); Nintendo Co., Ltd. (1963).
- **President 1949–2002:** [Hiroshi Yamauchi](/vault/people/hiroshi-yamauchi), Fusajiro's great-grandson. [Satoru Iwata](/vault/people/satoru-iwata) followed him in 2002.
- **Milestones:** first home video game machines, TV Game 6 and TV Game 15 (1977); [Game & Watch](/vault/systems/nintendo-game-and-watch) (1980); *[Donkey Kong](/vault/games/donkey-kong)* in the arcades (1981); the Family Computer (1983); [Game Boy](/vault/systems/game-boy) (1989); [Super Famicom](/vault/systems/super-nintendo) (1990).
- **Sales (Nintendo's figures):** Famicom and NES 61.91 million; Game Boy 118.69 million; Super Famicom and SNES 49.10 million; [Nintendo 64](/vault/systems/nintendo-64) 32.93 million.

## A century of cards

The cards were made by hand at first. Nintendo later added Western playing cards (from 1902), and in 1953 it became the first company in Japan to mass-produce plastic ones. In 1959 it began printing Disney characters on cards for children. *Edge* wrote in 1997 that putting Mickey Mouse on the backs of the cards "virtually doubled the company profits over night". Nintendo listed its shares on the Osaka and Kyoto stock exchanges in 1962 and took its present name in 1963.

Hiroshi Yamauchi had become president in 1949, in his early twenties. Magazine profiles of the company describe his first attempts to find something beyond cards: *Edge* in 1995 listed "individually portioned instant rice, a 'love hotel' and a taxi firm", and *N64 Magazine* in 1997 said all three failed. Nintendo's own published history does not mention them. What did work, according to the same accounts, was Nintendo's distribution: the network that already put its cards into shops all over Japan could carry toys and games too.

## Gunpei Yokoi and the toys

The first successful new line came from a maintenance engineer. [Gunpei Yokoi](/vault/people/gunpei-yokoi), an electronics graduate from Kyoto, was taken on to look after the card factory's machinery. *Edge* described him in 1995 as "an electronics tinkerer who was forever knocking up gadgets out of spare parts", and said Yamauchi moved him into a new division to make games and toys. Asked what he should make, Yokoi was told, according to David Sheff's book *Game Over* as quoted by *Edge*: "Something great!"

His first hit was the Ultra Hand, an extending plastic arm that grabbed things. *Edge* reported that it sold 1.2 million on launch; the magazines dated it to 1970, although later histories put it in 1966. Other toys followed, including a baseball pitching machine, the Ultra Machine, and a "Love Tester" for young couples. *Nintendo Power* told its readers in 1989 that Nintendo's Beam Gun series of 1970 had been "the first electronic toy in Japan".

## Game & Watch

The [Game & Watch](/vault/systems/nintendo-game-and-watch) handhelds came from Yokoi's team and used the liquid-crystal displays found in pocket calculators. Each played one game on a fixed LCD screen and also kept the time, hence the name. The first, *Ball*, went on sale in April 1980. *Retro Gamer*'s history of the series says the early models "did not sell in great numbers" and that sales took off with *Fire* in July 1980.

The series introduced a control that Nintendo kept. The multi-screen *Donkey Kong* Game & Watch of 1982 had a cross-shaped directional pad in place of a joystick, the same pad that later appeared on the NES controller and the Game Boy. *N64 Magazine* called it the company's "first major contribution" to game design, and noted that other console makers followed.

Yokoi later described his approach as "lateral thinking of withered technology": taking cheap, well-understood parts and finding new uses for them. Nintendo still quotes the phrase when it explains its own history.

## Into the arcades

Nintendo's first arcade games of the late 1970s, including *Radar Scope*, *Sheriff* and *Sky Skipper*, did reasonably well without making much of a mark. Then Yamauchi asked a young designer from the planning department, [Shigeru Miyamoto](/vault/people/shigeru-miyamoto), for a new game. Miyamoto had joined the company in 1977. *[Donkey Kong](/vault/games/donkey-kong)* (1981) was built around a story and characters instead of shooting, and it introduced the carpenter who became Mario. *N64 Magazine* reported that more than 60,000 *Donkey Kong* arcade machines sold in the United States.

## The Famicom and the NES

The Family Computer, or Famicom, was designed by a team led by [Masayuki Uemura](/vault/people/masayuki-uemura), a former Sharp engineer. It went on sale in Japan on 15 July 1983 at ¥14,800. According to *N64 Magazine*, it sold 500,000 units in its first two months and then sold "as fast as it could be made". The [Nintendo Entertainment System](/vault/systems/nintendo-entertainment-system) entry covers the hardware.

America was harder. Its home video game market had collapsed in the [1983 crash](/vault/phenomena/1983-crash), and shops no longer wanted consoles. Nintendo of America redesigned the Famicom as a grey box and sold it as a toy, packed with a light gun and a robot, [R.O.B.](/vault/hardware/rob) After a test market in the New York area in autumn 1985 and another in Los Angeles in February 1986, the NES went on sale across the United States during 1986. *[Super Mario Bros.](/vault/games/super-mario-bros)*, in *N64 Magazine*'s words, "was the game that sold the NES in the west".

Nintendo also decided which games could run on the machine. A lockout chip in every NES had to find a matching chip in the cartridge, and Nintendo made the cartridges. Its advertising told buyers to look for the [Nintendo Seal of Quality](/vault/phenomena/nintendo-seal), "your guarantee". *Edge* later put the arrangement less politely: Nintendo "sewed up the market with brutal efficiency, then sucked thirdparty developers dry with draconian licensing terms".

## What dominance looked like, in numbers

*ACE* reported from the Consumer Electronics Show in its March 1990 issue and led with the summary: "Nintendo rules, OK? That was the message that was brutally rammed home at this show." Then it gave Nintendo's own figures:

- around **26 million NES machines** sold in the US "in just over three years";
- the console in **22% of US households**, with Nintendo aiming for **29%** by the end of 1990;
- **$30 million** on advertising in the first half of 1990 alone.

The same report singled out the British developer [Rare](/vault/companies/rare), "the company that was Ultimate Play the Game", which had by then programmed around 40 Nintendo titles, all for other publishers. And it gave the new handheld's first figures:

> In the last quarter of 1989, the Gameboy produced more revenue than the whole of the videogame industry in 1985. One million hardware units sold, three million carts sold. 5 million Gameboys are expected to be sold in 1990.

The comparison with 1985 is a measure of how far the American market had fallen, as well as of how fast Nintendo had rebuilt it.

## Tengen and the courts

Enforcing the closed platform meant lawsuits. *ACE* reported in January 1990:

> Nintendo has just won its long court battle with Tengen over the classic [*Tetris*]. Nintendo now has exclusive rights to this addictive game – after some confusion over what company had what rights. Although this Nintendo vs Tengen court battle is over, there is still another $100 million lawsuit between the two companies – this time over alleged patent infringement.

Tengen was the console label of [Atari Games](/vault/companies/atari-games), the arcade half of the Atari that Warner had broken up in 1984. It had found a way round the NES lockout chip, and Atari Games had accused Nintendo of running a monopoly. By October 1990 *ACE* was describing Tengen as "the only software developer really supporting the Lynx", Atari's colour handheld.

Nintendo's lawyers reached British companies as well. In December 1988 *ACE* reported that US Gold had withdrawn *The Great Giana Sisters* "following pressure from Nintendo who felt it looked a bit too much like *Super Mario Brothers*". And in 1992 *Amiga Format* reported that Nintendo had "sued Codemasters and lost" over the [Game Genie](/vault/hardware/game-genie), the cheat cartridge [Codemasters](/vault/companies/codemasters) sold for consoles.

## Nintendo in Britain

Nintendo reached British shops late. Mattel launched the NES in the UK in 1987, four years after the Famicom, at £129.99 with *Super Mario Bros.*, and the price was soon cut to £99.99. [Roger Kean](/vault/people/roger-kean), reviewing it for *CRASH*, thought it worth buying if "virtually flawless arcade conversions" were what you wanted.

The Game Boy's British debut was planned for the Earls Court Toy Fair in January 1990, a toy fair and not a computer show, alongside the [Atari Lynx](/vault/systems/atari-lynx). *ACE* noted that the Game Boy was "doing a storm in Japan and USA with demand easily outstripping supply". By October 1990 *ACE*'s console guide listed the Game Boy at £69.99 with *[Tetris](/vault/games/tetris)*, and the NES at £79.99 with a *Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles* cartridge for a "limited period", against the Lynx at £179.99.

Nintendo also published its own magazine in Europe. [*Club Nintendo*](/vault/magazines/club-nintendo) appeared from 1989 in English, German and Dutch editions. Its "Scores of Fun" page set British players against French, Dutch and Belgian ones on *Gradius*, *Ghosts 'n Goblins*, *Castlevania* and *Kid Icarus*, with a photograph of the screen required as proof.

## Later consoles

The 16-bit Super Famicom arrived in Japan in 1990, the Nintendo 64 in 1996, the Game Boy Advance in 2001 and the Nintendo DS in 2004. In 1997 *Edge* reported that Yamauchi, then 69, had said he would retire; he handed over to Satoru Iwata in 2002. Nintendo's own life-to-date figures put the Game Boy, at 118.69 million, well ahead of any of its home consoles of the period.
