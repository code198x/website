**Tandy Corporation** was the American electronics company that owned the Radio Shack retail chain and produced the TRS-80 computer line. The company played a significant role in early personal computing, offering affordable systems through its extensive retail network.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1919 (as leather goods)
- **Radio Shack acquired:** 1963
- **Computer era:** 1977-1993
- **Notable lines:** [TRS-80](/vault/systems/trs-80), Color Computer

## Legacy

Tandy democratised computer access through Radio Shack's retail presence. While not the most powerful systems, TRS-80 and CoCo computers were accessible, available, and supported—qualities that introduced millions to computing.

## "Tandy Tower" and its own users

The entry above describes what Tandy made. The more interesting record in this library is how
Tandy behaved toward the people who bought it — and *80 Micro*'s correspondence pages in 1982
are unusually direct about it.

On Tandy's line that third-party hardware would damage your machine:

> we get weary of **Tandy Tower's suggestions that non-Shack peripherals, upgrades,
> modifications, and enhancements are going to screw up users' machines**.

And on the premise underneath it:

> [Radio Shack] — a relative latecomer to digital equipment — **isn't the only font of
> hardware and software wisdom**, nor necessarily of philosophical validity.

The retail model that gave Tandy its enormous advantage — thousands of Radio Shack stores
selling a computer to people who had never seen one — came with a posture toward outside
suppliers that its own enthusiast base resented. Every issue of *80 Micro* in this library
carries third-party advertising with the same disclaimer: "*TRS-80 is a trademark of Tandy
Corporation, which has no relationship to [us]*". An entire aftermarket, obliged to state in
print that the manufacturer wanted nothing to do with it.

## The Color Computer, from inside the user base

The same 1982 correspondence catches a community arguing about whether its machine had a
future, and making the case on evidence:

> maybe Wayne [Green] is right when he says that **Tandy Corporation might phase out the Color
> Computer; eventually, that's inevitable**… [But] the walls are covered with Color Computer
> software — **most of it far more sophisticated than what was available for the Model I at an
> equivalent stage of its existence**. Furthermore, Radio Shack's disk interface is here. It
> works.

The conclusion is the one worth keeping: if Radio Shack were to drop the Color Computer,
"**it isn't because the user base was missing**" — the suspicion being that it would go to
clear room for a new 16-bit machine.

Users of a mass-market home computer, in 1982, correctly identifying that the threat to their
platform was their own manufacturer's product strategy rather than any failure of the
platform.
