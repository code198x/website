In 1985 a British Commodore 64 owner who bought *Lode Runner*, *Archon* or *Racing Destruction Set* found the name of a record company on the box. Ariolasoft was the software house of Ariola, the German record company owned by Bertelsmann, and it sold the games of two leading American publishers, [Electronic Arts](/vault/companies/electronic-arts) and [Broderbund](/vault/companies/broderbund), in Europe. Its British arm hired the Hippodrome in London's West End for its launch, took journalists racing at Brands Hatch and to the golf course at Wentworth, and within two years was paying a team in Budapest to program a golf game from videotapes.

## Fast facts

- **Parent:** Ariola, the record company of the German media group Bertelsmann. *Your Commodore* described Ariolasoft in 1985 as "connected to Arista Records".
- **British arm:** Ariolasoft U.K. Ltd, launched at the end of 1984 at Asphalte House, Palace Street, London SW1; by 1986 its mail-order address was PO Box 461, London NW1.
- **Headquarters:** in Germany, by 1990 at Rietberg, near Gütersloh.
- **American games it sold in Britain:** Electronic Arts' *Archon*, *M.U.L.E.*, *One on One*, *Racing Destruction Set*, *[Marble Madness](/vault/games/marble-madness)* and *Arcticfox*; Broderbund's *[Lode Runner](/vault/games/lode-runner)*, *Choplifter*, *Spelunker* and *Karateka*.
- **Games it commissioned:** *Golf* (1986), *The Deactivators* (1986) and games on its own labels, Reaktor, Starlight and 39 Steps. It also sold the Spanish studio Dinamic's *Camelot Warriors* in Britain.
- **End:** Electronic Arts set up its own British operation in 1987. In 1990 the German company was sold to its manager, Hans-Joachim Krusche, and renamed United Software.

## A record company launch

Ariolasoft arrived in Britain in the winter of 1984–85, and *Your Commodore* filed the story under "Off the record". Two record companies were "making a song and dance about entering the software market", it reported: CBS and "Ariolasoft which is connected to Arista Records". CBS had started "rather modestly". Ariolasoft "hired the Hippodrome in London's West End for the night to tell the world about their first twelve titles". All twelve were imports, and they included at least two American number ones. "Some are now getting a bit long in the tooth, like Lode Runner," the magazine added, "but the selection does include some brilliant games."

*Commodore Computing International*'s release list for New Year 1985 shows what those games cost. Broderbund's *Choplifter*, *Lode Runner*, *Spelunker* and *Raid on Bungeling Bay* and Electronic Arts' *One on One* and *Realm of Impossibility* were £9.95 on cassette and £12.95 on disk. *Archon*, *M.U.L.E.*, *Murder on the Zinderneuf* and *Operation Whirlwind* were £11.95 and £14.95. Spectrum and Amstrad owners paid less: Ariolasoft's July 1985 advertisement in *CRASH* priced *Realm of Impossibility* at £9.95 on Commodore or Atari cassette and £8.95 on Spectrum or Amstrad cassette. By 1986 its Commodore 64 games settled at £9.95 on cassette and £14.95 on disk.

The Spectrum versions were reviewed on their merits. *CRASH* gave Paul Reiche III's *Archon* 83% in July 1985, and called it "very good, though pricey" at £10.95.

Ariolasoft spent money on being noticed. For the launch of *Racing Destruction Set* in 1985 its marketing and sales director, Frank Brunger, sent reviewers and distributors on a one-day "taster" course at Brands Hatch, where they each drove three laps in a Ford XR3i. In 1986 a coach took *Zzap!64* to Wentworth, where Ariolasoft was previewing its golf game.

## From importer to publisher

The American catalogue came ready-made, but Ariolasoft soon wanted games of its own. In July 1985 *Your Commodore* reported that the RamJam Corporation, the team behind the Commodore 64 adventure *Valkyrie 17*, had signed an exclusive three-year licensing agreement with Ariolasoft, "following in the footsteps of American companies Electronic Arts, Broderbund and Batteries Included", but the first British company to do so. RamJam's *The Terrors of Trantoss* came out under the Ariolasoft name in 1986. At the end of 1985 the trade press reported that Ariolasoft had bought [Llamasoft](/vault/companies/llamasoft). Jeff Minter told *Zzap!64* that it had not: Ariolasoft was marketing one game, *Batalyx*, as "an experimental deal".

### Golf, programmed in Budapest

The biggest of these projects was *Golf*, released as *Golf Construction Set*. According to *Computer & Video Games*, it was the brainchild of the games designer David Bishop, who ran the Tigress Marketing company, and Ariolasoft's Ashley Grey. The programming was done in Budapest by one of the teams working for Andromeda Software, the company that had programmed [Domark](/vault/companies/domark)'s *Eureka!*. None of the Hungarian programmers had seen or played golf. Bishop supplied videotapes of the classic tournaments and details of the courses, and "even took a golf club and golf balls with him on one of his numerous trips to the Hungarian capital". The videos solved a problem: in early versions the ball "flew for miles with just a tap of the club" and bounced "like one of those compressed rubber Superballs".

The game offered four championship courses, The Belfry, Wentworth Old Course, Sunningdale and Royal St George's, and a construction program for designing your own. *Zzap!64* reviewed it in July 1986 at £12.95 for two cassettes or £14.95 on disk and gave it 79%. Looking back that Christmas, the magazine judged that U.S. Gold's *Leader Board* was "much the better and most enjoyable".

Bishop's team worked for Ariolasoft again. Tigress was credited, with System Software, on *The Deactivators*, which *CRASH* scored 85% in November 1986. In 1987 *Computer Gamer* described "Dave Bishop's team" as major contributors to Ariolasoft's Reaktor label.

### Labels and a Spanish import

By 1987 Ariolasoft was releasing games under several labels. Reaktor published *Zarjaz*, *Centurions*, *Triaxos* and the Commodore 64 shoot 'em up *Starfox*, whose name later made Nintendo sell its own [*Star Fox*](/vault/games/star-fox) in Europe as *Starwing*; Starlight, set up by Francis Lee, formerly of Beyond, published *Deathscape* and *Hybrid*; and 39 Steps published *Bride of Frankenstein*. In October 1987 *The Games Machine* reported that Ariolasoft had "changed direction, moving towards being a publishing house rather than a software label", with teams of programmers working fairly independently on games for labels that Ariolasoft then published. [Tony Crowther](/vault/people/tony-crowther) joined in early 1987 to turn his unfinished *Moebius* into the official *Gobots* game.

Ariolasoft also carried European games the other way. *Camelot Warriors*, by Victor Ruiz of the Madrid studio Dinamic, came out in Britain under the Ariolasoft name, and *CRASH* scored it 82% in December 1986.

## Electronic Arts goes it alone

Ariolasoft's British business rested on Electronic Arts, and in 1987 EA left. *Commodore Computing International* reported that September that EA had timed "its major launch in Europe" to coincide with the Personal Computer World Show, bringing *Marble Madness*, *The Bard's Tale* and the *Archon* games, "all previously available through Ariolasoft". EA's Mark Lewis explained the decision to *Amiga Computing* the following year: the Ariolasoft years had been "a good experience for a couple of years but we weren't in touch with the consumers".

The British arm did not survive long without it. Looking back in January 1990, *The One* called Ariolasoft "the German distributor's English software appendage" and said it "didn't last too long over here".

## The German company

In Germany Ariolasoft carried on as a distributor. In 1989 the British publisher Mandarin Software made it its German partner, and *Amiga Computing* called it a "giant German distribution house" that would market Mandarin's games through "its 2,000 strong dealer base". In March 1990 *Amiga Joker* visited Ariolasoft's headquarters at Rietberg, near Gütersloh, for a press day with the German developer [Thalion](/vault/companies/thalion) and U.S. Gold, whose games it handled.

The Heinz Nixdorf MuseumsForum's account of the company says that in 1990 Bertelsmann sold Ariolasoft to its manager, Hans-Joachim Krusche, and that it was renamed United Software. *Amiga Joker* noted in October 1990 that a Dracula adventure was one of the last programs United Software would publish "under the old name Ariola Soft". The same account says the German branch of MicroProse took United Software over in 1993.

Ariola was one of several record companies that tried software in the mid-1980s; see [When the Record Companies Sold Software](/vault/phenomena/record-companies-in-software).
