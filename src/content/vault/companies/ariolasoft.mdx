If you bought *Marble Madness*, *Archon*, *Racing Destruction Set*, *Arcticfox* or *Lode Runner* in Britain in 1986, the name on the box was Ariolasoft. None of those games is Ariolasoft's. They are Electronic Arts' and Broderbund's, and Ariolasoft was **the company that held the British rights to sell them**.

## Fast facts

- **Press evidence in this library:** reviewed in *[CRASH](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine)* and *[ZZAP!64](/vault/magazines/zzap-64)* through **1985–1987**, at **£9.95 cassette / £14.95 disk** — a consistent, distinctly American two-tier price.
- **Published in the UK:** *[Marble Madness](/vault/games/marble-madness)*, *Archon* (83% in *CRASH*, £10.95), *Racing Destruction Set*, *Arcticfox*, *Touchdown Football*, *[Lode Runner](/vault/games/lode-runner)*, *Camelot Warriors*, *Deathscape*, *Triaxos*.
- **Parent:** Ariola, the record company — Ariolasoft was its software arm, and mail orders went to Ariolasoft UK Ltd, PO Box 461, London NW1.

## Two industries meeting

Two things about that list are worth separating out.

⚠ That two-tier reading is too simple. Ariolasoft's own advertisement in *CRASH* 18, July 1985, prices *Realm of Impossibility* as a **matrix by platform**, not a pair:

> "**Commodore or Atari** Cassette £9.95, Disk £12.95 · **Spectrum or Amstrad** Cassette £8.95. All prices include VAT & p&p."

So the disk tier was £12.95 on that title rather than £14.95, and there was a **separate, lower cassette price for the Spectrum and Amstrad** — the machines whose owners would not pay Commodore money. The same advertisement gives the trading address as **Ariolasoft U.K. Ltd., Suite 105/106, Asphalte House, Palace Street, London SW1E 5HS**.

The first thing to draw out is the **cassette-and-disk pricing**. £9.95 for tape and £14.95 for disk is an American publisher's price structure landing in a market where the Commodore 64 was overwhelmingly a cassette machine and £9.95 was already at the top end. American software cost more here, and part of the reason is that another company had to make a living in between.

The second is who owned it. A record label deciding that software was adjacent to its business is a judgement of its moment, and not a stupid one: both are **content stamped onto cheap physical media, sold through the same shops, to overlapping customers, with distribution as the hard part**. Record companies already had the warehouses, the sales force and the relationships with retail. Software did not.

## The other direction

*Camelot Warriors* is the quiet surprise in the catalogue. It is Spanish — a Dinamic game, from Madrid — reviewed in *CRASH* in December 1986 under the Ariolasoft name.

The same machinery that carried American software into Britain carried European software too, and it worked because the barrier was never technical. A C64 in Madrid ran the same code as a C64 in Manchester. What stopped a Spanish game reaching British shops was **paperwork, boxes, lorries and a sales rep**, and a licensee solved all four.

The American imports cluster in the eighties and the British originals spread wider — the
same pattern visible at [US Gold](/vault/companies/us-gold), and for the same reason. A
distributor handling finished, tested product from Electronic Arts is buying a known
quantity; a distributor commissioning its own is taking the risk itself.

Ariolasoft was still operating at scale into the sixteen-bit years: *Amiga Computing*
records **Mandarin Software** completing a distribution deal with "**giant German
distribution house Ariolasoft**" in 1989, covering *Lombard RAC Rally*, *Time and Magik* and
*Pioneer Plague*. The record-company parentage produced a distribution business that
outlived most of the publishers it carried.

## Why it mattered

Ariolasoft ran on the **territory licence** — the arrangement that made the eighties software market international while leaving no trace on the games themselves. It explains a puzzle anyone browsing old British magazines runs into: why celebrated American games carry a publisher's name that means nothing to American readers, and vice versa.

It also marks a moment when it was not obvious that software would become its own industry instead of a branch of the record business. That question stayed open for several years, and one of the possible answers had a record company's name on it.

Ariolasoft was not alone in asking it. Virgin, Thorn EMI and K-Tel all opened software labels between 1983 and 1985, and by 1989 only Virgin remained — see [When the Record Companies Sold Software](/vault/phenomena/record-companies-in-software).
