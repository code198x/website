## Overview

If you bought *Marble Madness*, *Archon*, *Racing Destruction Set*, *Arcticfox* or *Lode Runner* in Britain in 1986, the name on the box was Ariolasoft. None of those games is Ariolasoft's. They are Electronic Arts' and Broderbund's, and Ariolasoft was **the company that held the British rights to sell them**.

## Fast facts

- **Seen in the press:** reviewed in *CRASH* and *ZZAP!64* through **1985–1987**, at **£9.95 cassette / £14.95 disk** — a consistent, distinctly American two-tier price.
- **Published in the UK:** *Marble Madness*, *Archon* (83% in *CRASH*, £10.95), *Racing Destruction Set*, *Arcticfox*, *Touchdown Football*, *Lode Runner*, *Camelot Warriors*, *Deathscape*, *Triaxos*.
- **Parent:** Ariola, the record company — Ariolasoft was its software arm, and mail orders went to Ariolasoft UK Ltd, PO Box 461, London NW1.

## Two industries meeting

Two things about that list are worth separating out.

The first is the **cassette-and-disk pricing**. £9.95 for tape and £14.95 for disk is an American publisher's price structure landing in a market where the Commodore 64 was overwhelmingly a cassette machine and £9.95 was already at the top end. American software cost more here, and part of the reason is simply that another company had to make a living in between.

The second is who owned it. A record label deciding that software was adjacent to its business is a very 1984 judgement, and not a stupid one: both are **content stamped onto cheap physical media, sold through the same shops, to overlapping customers, with distribution as the hard part**. Record companies already had the warehouses, the sales force and the relationships with retail. Software did not.

## The other direction

*Camelot Warriors* is the entry's quiet surprise. It is Spanish — a Dinamic game, from Madrid — reviewed in *CRASH* in December 1986 under the Ariolasoft name.

The same machinery that carried American software into Britain carried European software too, and it worked because the barrier was never technical. A C64 in Madrid ran the same code as a C64 in Manchester. What stopped a Spanish game reaching British shops was **paperwork, boxes, lorries and a sales rep**, and a licensee solved all four.

## Why it mattered

Ariolasoft is the entry for the **territory licence** — the arrangement that made the eighties software market international while leaving no trace on the games themselves. It explains a puzzle anyone browsing old British magazines runs into: why celebrated American games carry a publisher's name that means nothing to American readers, and vice versa.

It also marks a moment when it was not obvious that software would become its own industry rather than a branch of the record business. That question stayed open for several years, and one of the possible answers had a record company's name on it.
