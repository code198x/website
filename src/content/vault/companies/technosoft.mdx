When Westwood's lead programmer Joe Bostic described what went into *[Dune II](/vault/games/dune-ii)*, the game that set the pattern for real-time strategy, he named three influences. *Populous* supplied the real-time play and *Civilization* the strategy. The third was a cartridge for Sega's Mega Drive: *Herzog Zwei*, "for the fast pacing". His Westwood colleague Mike Legg remembered the same thing: "We were also playing Herzog Zwei on the Mega Drive at the time." It came from **Technosoft**, a company in Sasebo, in Nagasaki prefecture, better known in Britain for a series of shoot-'em-ups called Thunder Force.

## Fast facts

- **Based:** Sasebo, Nagasaki prefecture, Japan.
- **Founded:** February 1980, as Sasebo Microcomputer Center; renamed Technosoft in 1982.
- **Name:** written "Tecno Soft" on many of its games and in the period press, and "Technosoft" in others.
- **Known for:** the [Thunder Force](/vault/games/thunder-force) shoot-'em-ups (1983–1998), *Herzog* (MSX, 1988) and *Herzog Zwei* (Mega Drive, 1989), *Elemental Master* (1990), *Hyper Duel* (arcade 1993, Saturn).
- **End:** stopped making games at the end of the 1990s; its rights passed to Twenty-One Company by 2001 and to [Sega](/vault/companies/sega) in 2016.

## Thunder Force

The first *Thunder Force* was a shooter for Japanese home computers, viewed from overhead and scrolling in every direction. It began on Sharp's X1, dated December 1983 in public sources (one puts it in 1984), and was converted to other machines; *Retro Gamer* pointed to it as an example of a game where the X1 version was better than the one for NEC's dominant PC-88. It never left Japan.

The series found its audience on the Mega Drive. *Thunder Force II* appeared first on Sharp's X68000 in 1988 and on the Mega Drive in 1989, published there by Sega. It already let the player flick between forward, backward and multi-directional weapons, and it kept the first game's overhead stages, which "scrolled in eight directions", and alternated them with side-on stages in the manner of *[Gradius](/vault/games/gradius)* and *[R-Type](/vault/games/r-type)*. *Mean Machines* gave it 82% in its first issue. *Thunder Force III* (1990) dropped the overhead stages and became, in *Mean Machines*' words, "a more traditional R-Type style horizontally scrolling shoot 'em up", keeping the switchable weapons: the ship carried five at once and the player could change between them at will. Sega did not release it in Britain. *Mean Machines* reviewed it in October 1991, after it had been available on import for more than a year, and ranked it with *Hellfire* and *Gynoug* as one of the best blasters on the Mega Drive.

*Thunder Force IV* (1992) widened the playfield. The screen scrolled up and down as well as along, so that power-ups were not always in view, and the player chose the order in which to attack the first four areas. *Mean Machines*' import review called the graphics "state of the art", with "some breathtaking backdrops". Its verdict caught the series' reputation: "The best shoot 'em up you'll get on the Megadrive, but suffers from a complete lack of originality." In America it was sold as *Lightening Force*, and Sega released it in Britain in 1993.

*Thunder Force V* came out on the Saturn in Japan in 1997 and on the PlayStation in 1998, when Working Designs published it in America. *Sega Saturn Magazine* reported in 1997 that the enemies would be "full 3D polygon constructions" while the play stayed side-on, and that Technosoft had been impressed by Taito's *RayStorm* but thought it could do better. *Edge* gave the game six out of ten in 1998. It found the "3D" only cosmetic and the game, "like so many Japanese releases, far too short", though its "2D appeal to old school gamers is undeniable".

## Herzog Zwei

Technosoft's other lasting idea began on the MSX. *Herzog* (1988) was billed as a "real time combat simulation": each side had a base, and the player flew a fighter that could turn into a robot while sending units against the enemy base. *Herzog Zwei* took the idea to the Mega Drive in 1989. *The Games Machine* found it an odd thing to see on a console, "a strategy game", and decided that it was "really a multi-direction shoot-'em-up with one or two tactical possibilities", but "quite addictive in two-player mode, fierce rivalry developing between friends". It gave it 75%. The [real-time strategy](/vault/genres/rts-genre) entry tells how *Herzog Zwei* fits among the genre's forerunners.

*Retro Gamer* later wrote that the game sold poorly, partly because Technosoft's reputation for shooters led buyers to judge it as one.

## Other games

*Elemental Master* (1990) was another Mega Drive shooter; *Megatech* called it "a technically superb shoot 'em up" with "excellent graphics and superb sound". Technosoft also converted *Devil Crash*, the pinball game [Compile](/vault/companies/compile) had made for the PC Engine, to the Mega Drive in 1991; it reached Britain as *Dragon's Fury*. *Hyper Duel* began in the arcades in 1993 and came to the Saturn, with a ship that could change into a flying robot, which *Retro Gamer* said gave it "a distinct Thunder Force feel".

The music was part of the reputation. Looking back at *Thunder Force III* in 2012, *Retro Gamer* called Technosoft "well-regarded for its superb chiptune scores".

## After Technosoft

Technosoft stopped making games at the end of the 1990s, and by 2001 its rights and assets had passed to Twenty-One Company. In 2008 Sega announced *Thunder Force VI*, made by Sega under the direction of Tez Okano. In September 2016, at the Tokyo Game Show, Sega announced that it had acquired the intellectual property of all Technosoft's games. Naosuke Arai, a former Technosoft development director, told the audience that the company's late president, Mr Ozono, had worried about the brand dying out and had felt that Sega was the company to take it on. *Thunder Force III* was the first game to follow, in a Japanese collection of Sega classics for the Nintendo 3DS in December 2016.
