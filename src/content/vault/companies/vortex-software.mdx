Vortex was, for practical purposes, **[Costa Panayi](/vault/people/costa-panayi)** — a
publisher whose catalogue is one programmer's sequence of ideas about **how to look at a
three-dimensional world on a machine that has none**.

*Computer & Video Games* introduced *Cyclone* in February 1985 as coming "from Costa
Panayi, the programmer who brought you the chart topping *Tornado Low Level*". Three years
later *Amstrad Action* was still framing new releases the same way: "Vortex are famous for
games like *Highway Encounter* and *Alien Highway*, **both programmed by Costa Panayi**."

## One idea, worked through

What holds the catalogue together is not a genre. It is a **camera angle**.

*TLL* (*Tornado Low Level*) and *Cyclone* both put the player above a landscape looking
down at a shallow angle — a view borrowed from neither the arcade's side-on scroller nor
the flight simulator's cockpit. *Cyclone*, in *C&VG*'s description, flies "a helicopter on
a mercy mission around a group of islands threatened by a deadly storm", searching for
inhabitants and picking them up.

*Highway Encounter* (**95%**) took the same overhead-oblique view and turned it into a
corridor: a straight road, a payload to push down it, and a squad of droids who die on the
way. *Revolution* (**91%**) put a rolling ball into a full isometric world.

That is a programmer solving the same rendering problem five times and getting a different
game out of it each time — the opposite of a publisher buying five licences.

## After the independence

By 1988 Vortex had "linked up with Gremlin", in *Amstrad Action*'s phrase, and the
partnership's first release was ***Deflektor*** — a laser-and-mirrors puzzle game across 60
levels, which *Amstrad Action* rated **90%** and called "an original concept, wonderfully
implemented".

The magazine's second reviewer put the continuity plainly: "It's great to see that Vortex
and Costa Panayi have **lost none of their flair and originality** since teaming up with
Gremlin."

Worth noting because absorption usually means the end of a house style. Here the house
style was a person, and the person carried on.

## Why it mattered

Vortex is the cleanest example in this Vault of a **one-programmer publisher that competed
on technique rather than scale**. No licences, no conversion factory, no marketing
campaign — a sequence of games that each asked the same question about perspective and
answered it differently.

Set it beside [Durell](/vault/companies/durell), which trained programmers, and
[Gargoyle](/vault/companies/gargoyle-games), which opened a second label when its genre
boxed it in. Vortex did neither. It just kept shipping Panayi.
