Costa Panayi was a mechanical engineer who worked with 3D geometry every day, and when he started writing games he could not see the point of doing it in two dimensions. "It just seems like a step backwards to return to 2D now," he told *CRASH* in 1986. "You get a feeling of space when you're working in 3D, and I don't think I could write in 2D." Every game he wrote from *Android 2* onwards was in 3D, and the company that sold them, Vortex Software of Manchester, was built around him.

Vortex released few games, about two a year, which was as many as Panayi could write on his own. *CRASH* reviewed them for its Spectrum readers from 1984 onwards, and by October 1986 it noted that the lowest overall score any of his games had collected was 79%.

## Fast facts

- **Based in:** Manchester; by late 1986 at Vortex House, Salford.
- **People:** Costa Panayi (programming), Luke Andrews (business), and at the start Costa's brother Crete Panayi.
- **Began:** late 1982, as a hobby that paid for itself; run professionally from 1983.

## From ZX81 to Android

Panayi bought a ZX81 at the end of 1982 and swapped programs with friends who owned one. "Our first games were Pontoon and Othello — small BASIC games — and we sent them off to a publishers and started earning royalties!" he recalled. With the money they bought books on machine code. When the Spectrum arrived they wrote *Cosmos* and *Android 1*. Running a business on the side took too much of their time, so they brought in Luke Andrews, then a teacher, to handle it. "I got involved on the business side to allow the lads to program," Andrews said.

*Android 1*, a robot-maze shooting game for the 16K Spectrum, reached the charts early in 1983. Its sequel was Panayi's first 3D game. *CRASH* made *Android 2* its Game of the Month in March 1984 and scored it 90%. It described a playing area that was "part massively convoluted maze, part open space", seen in "solid 3D, seen from well above and slightly to one side", and its reviewers thought its graphics "just about the best, possibly as good as any other game for the Spectrum to date". Vortex licensed Ocean, another Manchester company, to produce the Commodore 64 version.

## TLL and Cyclone

*TLL*, short for *Tornado Low Level*, put a swing-wing jet over a scrolling landscape of buildings, pylons, power lines, rivers and cliffs, about 36 times the size of the screen. The way to show height was a shadow. "The 3D effect of your shadow works extremely well, breaking up quite accurately if you are half over a building," *CRASH* wrote in August 1984, and it found the game "not quite simulation, not quite arcade, but somewhere in between". Targets scored more the lower you flew over them, so players went under bridges and power cables to get them.

*Cyclone* followed, reviewed in January 1985. *Computer & Video Games* introduced it as coming "from Costa Panayi, the programmer who brought you the chart topping Tornado Low Level": you fly a helicopter "on a mercy mission around a group of islands threatened by a deadly storm", winching up survivors and crates of medical supplies while the cyclone roams the map and throws the helicopter about. *CRASH* liked the effect but found the play too close to *TLL*, and gave it 79%, Panayi's lowest score. Panayi noticed the market following him: after *Cyclone*, he said, "there were dozens of helicopter games on the market suddenly".

## Highway Encounter

*Highway Encounter* was Panayi's highest-rated game. You control one of five droids, Vortons, pushing a bomb along a straight road through 30 zones to the enemy base, seen "from an oblique aerial angle". The other droids follow automatically and stop dead at any obstruction, so most of the game is clearing a path: shoving barrels and boxes into place to block the mines, and holding off enemy robots with a laser that pauses to recharge after every three shots. *CRASH* gave it 95% in September 1985 and compared its graphics to [Ultimate](/vault/companies/ultimate)'s, "both in design and quality".

It was not built on the earlier games' routines. "Highway Encounter meant starting from scratch again," *CRASH* reported, and Panayi explained: "I like to take a completely different approach to the way I handle the 3D in each game." Its sequel, *Alien Highway* (88% in June 1986), was a remix with a random element in its layout, and was written by Marc Haigh-Hutchinson, according to *CRASH*'s review.

## Revolution

*Revolution* was a deliberate change. "With Revolution we thought there are no bouncing ball games, so why don't we have a go?" Panayi said. The player bounces a ball around platforms hung over bottomless ravines, touching pairs of grey blocks within a time limit to solve each puzzle. Each new game shuffles the puzzles between levels, so a player who never passes level one still gets new ones. *CRASH* made it a Smash with 91% in October 1986 and thought it "still seems to have something that relates it to the Highway games".

## U.S. Gold and Gremlin

In 1986 Vortex signed a deal with [U.S. Gold](/vault/companies/us-gold) to handle its marketing. "Now that the market's moved on to Europe, and we've moved on, VORTEX has outgrown the VORTEX team," Vortex told *CRASH*, and it planned to build a team of programmers around Panayi. *Revolution*'s advertisements that November carried the line "Manufactured and distributed by U.S. Gold Limited".

From the end of 1986 Vortex's games also appeared with [Gremlin Graphics](/vault/companies/gremlin-graphics). *ZZAP!64* credited the Commodore 64 *Highway Encounter* to "Gremlin Graphics/Vortex" in December 1986, and *Amstrad Action* described *Deflektor* in 1988 as "the first Vortex title since they linked up with Gremlin". *Deflektor* was a puzzle game of 60 levels in which the player turns mirrors to guide a laser beam, while gremlins wander about turning them back. *Amstrad Action* rated it 90% and called it "an original concept, wonderfully implemented", and its second reviewer was glad that "Vortex and Costa Panayi have lost none of their flair and originality since teaming up with Gremlin". By 1989 Panayi's next game, *H.A.T.E.* (*Hostile All Terrain Encounter*), a return to his three-quarter 3D view, was reviewed as a Gremlin Graphics release.

Period sources do not record Vortex being taken over by Gremlin, or when the company stopped trading.
