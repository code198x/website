Sega's name is a short form of an English one: Service Games. The company that became one of the leading arcade makers of the 1980s, and then Nintendo's main rival in home consoles, grew out of American businesses that supplied slot machines, jukeboxes and arcade games to US servicemen in post-war Japan. An American, David Rosen, led it for most of its first twenty years, and from 1969 it belonged to an American conglomerate. Sega's own histories, and the magazines that retold them, do not agree on how it all began.

## Fast facts

- **Origins:** American-run coin-op distributors in Japan, from the early 1950s.
- **Name:** from **SE**rvice **GA**mes.
- **Sega Enterprises:** formed by a merger with David Rosen's company, Rosen Enterprises, in 1965.
- **Owners:** Gulf+Western from 1969; a management buyout backed by the Japanese company CSK in April 1984.
- **Arcade games:** *Periscope*, then the Gremlin-developed video games of the early 1980s, then Yu Suzuki's *Hang-On*, *Space Harrier*, *Out Run* and *After Burner*.
- **Home consoles:** SG-1000 (1983), Mark III and [Master System](/vault/systems/sega-master-system) (1985–86), [Mega Drive](/vault/systems/sega-mega-drive) (1988), [Game Gear](/vault/systems/sega-game-gear), [Saturn](/vault/systems/sega-saturn) (1994), [Dreamcast](/vault/systems/sega-dreamcast) (1998).
- **End of the hardware:** in January 2001 Sega announced that it would stop making the Dreamcast and publish games for other companies' machines.

## Service Games and David Rosen

Every account starts in the same place. After the Second World War, and during the Korean War, there were American servicemen in Japan and money to be made entertaining them. The details depend on who is telling it.

*Sega Visions*, Sega of America's own magazine, told its readers in February 1993 that Service Games was already selling "jockey tables" and electro-mechanical pinball machines in 1951, and that "in 1965, Service Games merged with Rosen Enterprises, Ltd. (founded in Japan by American David Rosen), to become SEGA Enterprises Japan, its new name derived from SErvice GAmes."

*[Computer and Video Games](/vault/magazines/computer-and-video-games)* gave a fuller version in November 1998. A company called **Nihon Goraku Bussan** began importing pinball machines and jukeboxes for the soldiers in the early 1950s, and traded under the brand name Service Games. Rosen, an ex-serviceman, had been importing air-rifle games and other coin-operated amusements since the late 1950s. In 1965 the two merged, "and the merger with Rosen Enterprises gave the now familiar Sega Enterprises".

*Edge*, a month later, told the story as Rosen's alone. After the Korean War he went back to Tokyo, and from 1954 imported instant photo booths, because occupied Japan wanted a photograph on every identity card. Repairing the booths led to repairing pachinko machines, and in *Edge*'s version Service Games was the name of Rosen's own pachinko operation. The other accounts give Service Games a separate history from his.

Two later sources push the story further back. The 2019 book *SEGA Master System: a visual compendium* says Sega was "established in the 1940s as Standard Games", supplying slot machines to US military bases, and moved to Japan as Service Games in 1953. The Wikipedia history, a secondary source, names the Americans involved: Bromley, Bromberg and James Humpert set up Standard Games in Honolulu in 1940, and Service Games in 1946. It also explains the date Sega itself uses. Service Games of Japan was wound up in May 1960, and two new companies, Nihon Goraku Bussan and Nihon Kikai Seizō, took over its business in June. Sega's parent company counts from 1960: it marked Sega's 60th anniversary in 2020.

The accounts measure different events. 1960 is the incorporation of the company that later became Sega, 1965 is the merger that produced Sega Enterprises, and the 1940s are the American businesses that came before either.

## Gulf+Western, Gremlin and the 1984 buyout

Once it became Sega, the compendium says, the company began making its own games instead of distributing other people's, and the submarine game *Periscope* was the first of its hits. In 1969 Gulf+Western Industries bought Sega Enterprises. In the late 1970s Sega bought Gremlin Industries of San Diego, which knew how to make video games when Sega did not, and for several years arcade cabinets in America carried the name Sega/Gremlin. Gulf+Western also owned Paramount Pictures, which is how Sega came to make *Star Trek: Strategic Operations Simulator*.

Rosen was still at the head of the company. In May 1983 *JoyStik* quoted him as chairman of the board, and reported that the company had dropped the Gremlin half of its name because the combined name was "a bit awkward". The same year he appears in a story told by Richard Stearns of Parker Brothers, who wanted the home rights to Konami's *[Frogger](/vault/games/frogger)*, which Sega distributed in America. Stearns called the game "kind of a mediocre arcade game" at the time. Sega said the rights were going elsewhere, so Parker Brothers tried again with more money: "we called up David Rosen and almost heard his chin hit the table when we made our offer."

Sega of America's 1993 history lists the "firsts" the company claimed by 1983: the first laser-disc game (*Astron Belt*) and the first 3-D video game (*Subroc-3D*). These are Sega's own claims, made a decade later.

The American arcade business then slumped, and Gulf+Western sold. The compendium records that it sold Sega's American assets to Bally Manufacturing in 1983. In April 1984, as *Sega Visions* told it, Sega Enterprises in Japan was "purchased from Gulf + Western by a partnership made up of CSK ... and Sega Enterprises Japan management". The compendium names the two men behind the buyout, Hayao Nakayama and David Rosen, with Nakayama as chief executive. Sega of America was set up the same year.

## The arcade hits

Yu Suzuki joined Sega in 1983. The compendium credits him with a run of games built into cabinets that home machines could not copy. *Hang-On* (1985) had a ride-on motorcycle that the player leaned into the corners, the first of what Sega called *taikan* ("body sensation") games. *Space Harrier* put the player in a hydraulic cabinet, and *Enduro Racer*, *Out Run*, *Thunder Blade*, *Super Hang-On* and *After Burner* followed. Their "Super Scaler" boards drew the road and the scenery by scaling sprites, not polygons.

The polygons came in the 1990s. When the [Saturn](/vault/systems/sega-saturn) went on sale in Japan, *Edge* found that the only worthwhile launch game was a conversion of the arcade polygon fighting game *Virtua Fighter*.

## Consoles

Sega's first home console, the SG-1000, went on sale in Japan on 15 July 1983, the same day as Nintendo's Famicom, according to the compendium. Its successor, the Mark III, became the [Master System](/vault/systems/sega-master-system), which lost heavily to Nintendo in Japan and North America but did well in Europe and Brazil. Its entry has the details.

The [Mega Drive](/vault/systems/sega-mega-drive) followed in Japan at the end of 1988 and, *ACE* reported, sold out its first 50,000 or so within two days. *Sega Visions* called its American version, the Genesis, "the first ... affordable 16-bit video game system" when it arrived in 1989 at $189.95, and said that by 1992 it topped the national best-seller list for all toys and games. Those were Sega's own figures. The Mega Drive's entry covers its hardware and its marketing.

The Saturn went on sale in Japan on 22 November 1994. *Edge* was in Tokyo for the launch: 200,000 machines sold out on the first day at ¥44,800, and a Sega source said the company had held back 300,000 more for when Sony's PlayStation arrived. The [Dreamcast](/vault/systems/sega-dreamcast) followed in Japan on 27 November 1998.

It was the last one. *Edge* reported the announcement in March 2001. On 24 January Sega said it would stop making the Dreamcast, and on 31 January it confirmed that it was leaving the hardware business to publish games for other companies' consoles. *Virtua Fighter 4* would appear on PlayStation 2.

## Sega on home computers

In Britain and Europe most people first played Sega's arcade games at home through licensed conversions for computers. US Gold published *Out Run* for the Spectrum at £7.99 and claimed 200,000 copies sold in its first two weeks. *CRASH*'s reviewers split over it in February 1988: one called it "an essential purchase", another "one of the worst multiloads in history". Elite published *Space Harrier*, and Activision *Afterburner*, which *CRASH* rated 86%. The compendium is blunt about these conversions, calling them "poor facsimiles of the real thing". It adds that Sega marketed the Master System in Europe as the faithful way to play the same games at home.

After 2001 Sega published its games on other companies' consoles, beginning with PlayStation 2 and Nintendo's machines. In 2004 it became part of Sega Sammy Holdings.
