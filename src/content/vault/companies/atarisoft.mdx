Atarisoft’s advertisement in the June 1984 *Ahoy!* lined up five competing computers beneath stacks of game boxes. The machines were a VIC-20, IBM PC, Apple II, TI-99/4A and Commodore 64. Above them, Atari offered games their owners might otherwise have associated with an arcade or an Atari machine. The business was selling software to those owners, whether or not they bought Atari hardware. ([Original advertisement, back cover](https://archive.org/details/ahoy-magazine-06).)

**Atarisoft** was a label of [Atari Inc.](/vault/companies/atari). John Anderson’s March 1984 *Creative Computing* article dates the announcement of the cross-platform venture to June 1983. Its history predates the later split between Atari Corporation and [Atari Games](/vault/companies/atari-games), and the latter’s [Tengen](/vault/companies/tengen) subsidiary.

## One label, several origins

The name on a box did not necessarily identify the arcade game’s creator. The *Ahoy!* advertisement offered *[Pac-Man](/vault/games/pac-man)*, *[Donkey Kong](/vault/games/donkey-kong)*, *[Defender](/vault/games/defender)*, *[Joust](/vault/games/joust)* and *[Pole Position](/vault/games/pole-position)*, among others. Its small print names Nintendo, Williams, Namco and Taito rights alongside Atari’s own.

Those credits describe a network of licences. Atarisoft could publish a version of a Nintendo or Namco game for a Commodore computer. The publishing label, original game design and particular conversion were separate contributions. An Atari logo alone cannot settle who designed or programmed the game inside.

## Different versions for different machines

The stacks in that advertisement are not identical. They present a catalogue by machine, with different titles under each platform heading. The text also mentions ColecoVision and Intellivision, with a qualification that *Donkey Kong* and *Battlezone* were not available for ColecoVision. Even the advertisement needed exceptions.

A contemporary *[COMPUTE!](/vault/magazines/compute-magazine)* Winter CES report lists new Atarisoft announcements for the C64, VIC-20, IBM PC, Apple II and TI-99/4A. It gives $34.95 for disk editions and $44.95 for cartridges. Its language records what Atari announced at the show; it does not establish that every listed game reached every listed machine. ([*COMPUTE!* 47, April 1984, p.86](https://www.atarimagazines.com/compute/issue47/086_1_The_Shakeout_Begins_A_Watershed_Winter_CES.php).)

The format mattered to the buyer. A Commodore cartridge could not be put into an Apple II and become the Apple version. Each machine needed its own program and physical edition, within its own display, memory and sound limits.

## A publishing name with a specific owner

Atarisoft’s June 1984 advertisement explicitly says its products were manufactured by Atari Inc. and were not licensed or approved by the makers of the competing hardware. The arcade-game licences and the hardware companies’ approval were different questions. Keeping those relationships separate also prevents a later Tengen cartridge or an Atari Corporation product from being folded into the same catalogue because it carries part of the Atari name.
