Durell Software Limited began in **February 1983**, when **Robert White** — in *CRASH*'s
words — "plonked a **48K Oric** and an Epson printer on a desk in **Taunton** and began
writing home computer software."

What grew out of that desk is, by the numbers, one of the strongest catalogues of the
period: **95, 93, 93, 92, 90, 88, 88, 83**, across seven years, from a company most
histories of the era do not mention. The numbers are on the record; the company is not.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** February 1983. **Based:** Castle Lodge, Castle Green, Taunton, Somerset
  TA1 4AB — an attic room in an old building facing Taunton's castle.
- **Founder:** **Robert White**.
- **Programmers named in the press:** **Mike Richardson** (*Harrier Attack*, *Scuba Dive*,
  *Combat Lynx*, *Turbo Esprit*); **Clive Townsend** (*Saboteur*); **Simon Francis**
  (*Critical Mass*); **Ron Jeffs** (Oric versions); **Nick Wilson** (conversions);
  **Dave Cummings** (graphics); **Stephen Parker**, marketing consultant;
  **Phil Dierks** and **Mike Evis**, business software.
- **Advertising slogan:** *"Software getting harder…"*
- ***CRASH* scores, from the issue 48 index:** *Fat Worm Blows A Sparky* **95%** (issue 34),
  *Saboteur* **93%** (issue 24), *Thanatos* **93%** (issue 35), *Scuba Dive* **92%**
  (issue 2), *Critical Mass* **90%** (issue 23), *Combat Lynx* **88%** (issue 10),

## The kitchen units on the ceiling

Robert White's route into software is the least likely in this Vault, and *CRASH* got the
whole of it out of him in February 1986.

He trained as an **art teacher**, finished, and worked out there was not much demand. He
retrained in **quantity surveying** and left college with a first-class degree, having met
computers on the course. He then applied — "rather cheekily", says *CRASH* — to the
**Oxford Regional Health Authority**, which was using a computer-based building design
system to model **Milton Keynes District General Hospital**:

> The model of the hospital was a fully accurate three dimensional representation of the
> building, **right down to the very last doorknob and window catch**. The whole hospital
> was being designed on computer — as you can imagine, it was a massive undertaking.

His mixture of art, computer modelling and surveying got him the job. His first task was to
find some kitchen work-units in the model, and the list came back with **exactly twice as
many as the architect knew were needed**.

> It took me quite a while to make the conceptual jump needed to find them. In the end,
> **they turned out to be on the ceilings**.

One architect had entered the units onto the ceilings rather than the floors; a second had
later noticed the floors were empty and entered them again. A man whose job was to
reconcile a three-dimensional model against physical reality, finding the discrepancy by
imagining the room upside down. It is difficult to think of better training for writing
games on a machine with 48K.

He left because he was bored:

> I really didn't want to carry on being someone else's employee and saw there was an
> opportunity to set up a business of my own producing computer software. **So I made the
> break.**

## Starting small, deliberately

The founding is unusually well documented, and unusually unromantic.

White and his wife **Veronica** moved from Oxfordshire to a house near Taunton **owned by
his mother-in-law** — "so in the early days of Durell there wasn't a mortgage to worry
about". Veronica kept working as a **drama teacher**. Robert bought an Oric and a printer,
and the printer was the hard decision: "the printer was an investment I thought long and
hard about".

**Plan A was not to write games.** It was to finish an assembler on the Oric, sell it as a
utility, and use it to write the games afterwards. That is the correct order of operations
and almost nobody followed it.

He then **advertised locally** for programmers, which is how **Mike Richardson** and
**Ron Jeffs** arrived. Richardson had left school early with a half-finished Spectrum game
— *Jungle Trouble*, which took the Game of the Month slot in the Living Guide in
**issue 1 of *CRASH***. He wrote *Harrier Attack* next.

*Harrier Attack* sold **nearly a quarter of a million copies** worldwide across formats. It
also, *CRASH* notes drily, "attracted a bit of flak for its scenario — it was the time of
the Falklands War".

## Training on games that were never released

The most interesting thing Durell did is a hiring practice, and it produced the company's
most famous game by accident.

**Clive Townsend** had been a Sinclair owner since the ZX81, where he wrote a Tarot card
program. He hung around the Durell offices learning machine code, making tea and — *CRASH*
is unkind about this — "wearing his cool-dude sunglasses". In **May 1985** Durell took him
on full time and set him to write a Spectrum game called ***Death Pit***, in *CRASH*'s
words "**mainly so he could learn how to apply the machine code he'd learnt**".

*Death Pit* was never released. Halfway through it briefly looked commercial, then didn't.

Between sessions on it, Townsend was doing graphics for other people and playing with his
own at home:

> I'm interested in Karate, and designed a **Ninja who just ran around in a building on a
> scrolling screen**. I showed what I had done to Robert in the office one day, and he
> liked the idea — only **he wanted the screen to flip rather than scroll**.

That became ***Saboteur***, **93%**, and one of the founding texts of
[stealth as a game mechanic](/vault/techniques/stealth-mechanics). *CRASH* records the
provenance exactly: *Saboteur* "rose from the ashes of *Death Pit*, which was
**cannibalised for the routines it contained**."

A publisher paying a junior a salary to write a game it expected to throw away, and getting
a 93% out of the wreckage, is a training budget most companies of the period did not have
and did not think to want. Set it beside White's stated company philosophy, which *CRASH*
squeezed out of him as a one-liner:

> **Every game we do should really be an improvement for each programmer.**

Townsend also insisted his playtesters be credited — "Rich, and Mat the Fat" — so *CRASH*
credited them.

## Simon Francis, and the Dragon that got him hired

The second recruitment story is the same shape. **Simon Francis** met computers via a lab
technician's ZX81 at middle school, learned BASIC on a Pet, and talked his father into a
**Dragon 32**. He wrote *Pit Fiend* on it, sold to **Microdeal**'s pocket-money label, and
is candid about it:

> The only clever thing about *Pit Fiend* in my opinion was the fact that I managed to get
> **four voices on the Dragon**. Otherwise it was a run-of-the-mill game really.

He showed it to Robert White, who "wasn't interested in Dragon software" — and offered him
freelance work anyway, handing him an Amstrad to learn Z80 on. A Galaxians variant and a
couple of Amstrad games later, Durell pulled him **out of college** at the start of 1985 to
write *Critical Mass*, which took **90%**.

Francis on his own 90%: "**I'm not entirely satisfied with *Critical Mass*.**"

## No storyboards, and a business software department

Two structural details worth keeping.

**How games got approved.** *CRASH*: "No-one at Durell bothers too much with storyboards.
Game ideas are bounced around the office, with everyone chipping in ideas and suggestions
until the basic idea becomes a fairly detailed plan. Then it's a matter of convincing
Robert, the Big Cheese, that the idea is worth turning into a game." A marketing consultant,
**Stephen Parker**, advised on direction, and White reports the advice plainly: "the more
High-Tech scenarios involving vehicles and futuristic equipment are what people want."

**How the rent got paid.** Durell ran a **business software section** — **Phil Dierks** and
**Mike Evis** on an accounting package — and White is explicit about why:

> the business software market represents an area of **stability** to back up the volatile
> games market. **A game tends to have quite a short life nowadays whereas good business
> software can sell and sell (and sell).**

A games publisher hedging its games business with accountancy software, in 1986, having
correctly identified that hits do not repeat. Games programmers lent a hand on business
routines "more by way of taking a break".

## The cost of getting better

Running through the profile is a note that turns out to be about the whole industry.

Mike Richardson wrote the Amstrad *Harrier Attack* in a couple of weeks. *Scuba Dive* took
six months. *Turbo Esprit* took over twelve. He says so ruefully:

> **Each successive program is taking longer to write.** I suppose it's getting more
> difficult to keep up with advances in the quality of games.

That is a programmer in 1986 describing, from the inside, the cost curve that would end the
bedroom era. Durell's response was the training pipeline above: if each game costs more,
the answer is programmers who get better each time.

*CRASH*'s own aside on *Scuba Dive* makes the same point from the reviewing side. It scored
**92%** in issue 2 — "in those days, the CRASH Smash hadn't been invented, otherwise
*Scuba Dive*'s 92% overall rating would have made it one of the first Smashes." *Combat
Lynx*, next, took **88%** and "missed the mark by a couple of percentage points". Then
*Critical Mass* and *Saboteur* both took Smashes, which is what brought *CRASH* to Taunton
in the first place.

## Why it mattered

Durell is the counter-example to almost every generalisation about British 8-bit
publishing. It was not in London, Liverpool or Manchester. It did not buy licences. It did
not have a famous founder, a scandal, or an advertising campaign anyone remembers — only a
slogan, *"Software getting harder…"*, which turned out to be a promise.

What it had was a **method**: hire locally, train on projects you can afford to bin, credit
the people who did the work, hedge the volatile business with a boring one, and treat each
game as the programmer's next lesson rather than the company's next hit. On that method a
quantity surveyor in Somerset out-scored most of the industry for four years.
