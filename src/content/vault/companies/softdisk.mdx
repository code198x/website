Softdisk sold software the way a magazine sells articles. Every month a subscriber received a disk from Shreveport, Louisiana, with a cover picture, a contents page, readers' letters, columns and a handful of new programs, all read and run on the computer itself. By 1989 the company said it was reaching "over 75,000 customers each month" with four such magazines, one for each of the popular home computers.

It is remembered now for a small department in the same building. In 1990 Softdisk's PC games subscription, Gamer's Edge, was being written by John Romero, John Carmack, Tom Hall and the artist Adrian Carmack. They made *Commander Keen* at night on the company's computers, and in February 1991 they left to found [id Software](/vault/companies/id-software).

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** 1981, by the company's own account; the earliest independent listing found is from March 1984.
- **Based:** Shreveport, Louisiana: 3811 Saint Vincent in 1984, P.O. Box 30008 through the late 1980s and 1990s, and 606 Common Street in the early 1990s.
- **Run by:** Al Vekovius, its owner when the id founders left.
- **Disk magazines:** *Softdisk* (Apple II), *Loadstar* (Commodore 64 and 128), *Big Blue Disk* (IBM PC, later *Softdisk PC*), *Diskworld* (Macintosh), *Softdisk G-S* (Apple IIGS) and *On Disk Monthly* (IBM PC).
- **Games:** Gamer's Edge, a monthly subscription of PC games, whose programmers founded id Software on 1 February 1991.

## A magazine on a disk

The first disk carried the company's name. *Softdisk* was an [Apple II](/vault/systems/apple-ii) magazine, and in September 1984 *Electronic Games* reported that "the monthly publication-on-disk for Apple pickers" was moving into shops. Each issue, the magazine said, had "programming techniques, hints, tips, a variety of useful programs, games, novelties, plus a great deal of interaction between subscribers in an almost club-like atmosphere".

*Computist*, an Apple II magazine, reviewed it in 1990, when *Softdisk* was on issue 106. It "reads much like a 'real magazine', even down to pages you can turn with a click on a screen corner". Most of the disk went to two or three programs and extras. Issue 106 had a text adventure called "Amusement Park" that the reviewer rated "near Infocom-grade", two crossword puzzles, 28 Print Shop graphics, a version of Mastermind and a checkbook balancer. A year's subscription cost $69.95. A separate magazine for the Apple IIGS, *Softdisk G-S*, had reached issue 11 and came on a 3½-inch disk.

## Four monthly disks

*Loadstar* did the same for the [Commodore 64](/vault/systems/commodore-64) from 1984, and by 1988 Softdisk was selling three magazines side by side, each at $19.95 for a three-month trial: *Softdisk* for the Apple II, *Loadstar* for the Commodore 64 and 128, and *Big Blue Disk* for the IBM PC, Tandy and MS-DOS clones. The [Disk magazines](/vault/culture/disk-magazines) entry describes what a *Loadstar* subscription bought.

In 1989 Softdisk went looking for contributors in *Amazing Computing* with an advertisement built on *The Wizard of Oz*: "We're off to seek a wizard... a computer wizard, that is." It called the company "publishers of the largest and most successful family of monthly software collections, reaching over 75,000 customers each month, including some in Kansas". It listed four monthly disks, *Softdisk*, *Diskworld* for the Macintosh, *Big Blue Disk* and *Loadstar*, and promised versions for the Apple IIGS and the Amiga. "We do not pay in emeralds or rubies," it added, "but we do pay... the highest rates in the industry!" *Compute!* reported in December 1989 that *Diskworld* had "recently started".

The PC range grew in the 1990s. *On Disk Monthly* was advertised in November 1991 at "less than $7 a month", and by June 1994 Softdisk was offering a free issue of *Softdisk PC*, "the #1 choice for home computing software! Not shareware!", with "8-10 new programs" a month for DOS and Windows.

## Gamer's Edge

Gamer's Edge was, in Softdisk's own advertising, "the first software subscription devoted to game enthusiasts": a new game for the IBM PC every month, for "LESS THAN $10 A MONTH". *Wireframe* credits John Romero with the idea. Romero had written Apple II games for magazines, including the disk magazine *Uptime*, and he arrived in 1989. "We were all at Softdisk in 1989 when I met Tom Hall," he told *Edge*. "I came to Softdisk because Jay [Wilbur] was going there." Wilbur later ran id's business side. According to *Retro Gamer*, Hall had joined as a games programmer in 1987. John Carmack came from freelance programming, and Adrian Carmack was already working at Softdisk as an artist.

In 1990 Carmack found a way to make a PC scroll a screen of scenery smoothly, which he called adaptive tile refresh. Carmack and Hall used it to rebuild the first level of *Super Mario Bros. 3* overnight, and the team spent a weekend with Softdisk's computers, taken home in their cars, turning it into a demonstration for Nintendo. Nintendo wanted Mario kept on its own machines. Softdisk, Hall recalled, "didn't want to use the smooth scrolling trick Carmack had discovered, so we thought, 'Well, if they don't want it, we could do something ourselves.'"

That something was *Commander Keen in Invasion of the Vorticons*, written at night and at weekends on Softdisk's computers and published as shareware by Scott Miller's [Apogee](/vault/companies/apogee-software) on 14 December 1990. Miller had reached Romero through the fan mail that Softdisk screened, by writing under several names from one address. After the first royalty cheque arrived, Carmack, Romero, Hall and Adrian Carmack left Softdisk and founded id Software on 1 February 1991.

Softdisk was not pleased that the game had been made with its equipment, and the four agreed to go on writing games for Gamer's Edge, one every two months until 31 January 1992, at $5,000 a game. "As part of leaving, we agreed to do games so the Gamer's Edge product could continue," Hall told *Retro Gamer*. Those contract games were where id tried out its ideas: *Commander Keen in Keen Dreams*, which Softdisk offered free to new Gamer's Edge subscribers in December 1991 as "a $14.95 value"; *Hovertank One*, with the player in a tank among flat-coloured walls; and *Catacomb 3-D*, which put textures on the walls. The last two contract games were made by other programmers so that id could start *Wolfenstein 3D*. Softdisk later issued a follow-up, *The Catacomb Abyss*, as shareware; *Computer Gaming World* still called Softdisk "best known for their Gamer's Edge monthly disk subscription service" in May 1993.

## After the disks

*Loadstar* was the longest-lived of the magazines. By 1999 it came from J & F Publishing at the same Shreveport post-office box, and it lasted into the 2000s. According to Wikipedia, Softdisk published a Windows disk magazine from 1994 to 1999 and became an internet service provider and web developer from 1995. By 2013 Softdisk and its games were in the hands of Flat Rock Software, which licensed *Keen Dreams* for a new release in 2013 and published the source code of *Hovertank* in 2014.

## Released source code

Several games that id made for Softdisk have had their source code published under the GNU General Public License version 2. In June 2014 Flat Rock Software released *Hovertank 3-D* on GitHub, and the *Catacomb* series, from *Catacomb 3-D* to *The Catacomb Abyss* and its sequels *Armageddon* and *Apocalypse*, appeared there the same month. In September 2014 *Commander Keen in Keen Dreams* followed, under the GPL version 2 or later, after a crowdfunding campaign to pay Flat Rock for the right to release it; the release credits Javier M. Chavez and Chuck Naaden.
