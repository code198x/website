If you bought a U.S. Gold arcade conversion in the late 1980s, there is a good chance it was written in a house in south Manchester. Tiertex, set up in 1987 by Donald Campbell and John Prince, converted Atari's *720°*, Sega's *Thunder Blade* and Capcom's *Street Fighter*, *Strider* and *Mercs*, usually for every home format at once and usually against a deadline. The results were uneven, and the period magazines show just how uneven. *CRASH* made *Thunder Blade* a Smash at 91%; *Zzap!64* gave *Human Killing Machine* 31% and told its readers to avoid it. The same Tiertex *Strider* scored 96% on the Amiga and 42% on the Commodore 64.

## Fast facts

- **Founders:** Donald Campbell, an electronics engineer, and John Prince, a physicist.
- **Founded:** 1987, in Manchester.
- **Main client:** [U.S. Gold](/vault/companies/us-gold), which bought a stake in the company.
- **Formats:** Spectrum, Commodore 64, Amstrad CPC, Atari ST, Amiga and PC; later Sega and Nintendo consoles and the Game Boy.
- **Size:** 15 staff by January 1990; about a hundred people passed through it over sixteen years, by Campbell's count.
- **Ended:** stopped trading in about 2003.

## Two founders from Artic

Campbell and Prince met writing Spectrum games for Artic Computing. Campbell wrote Artic's *World Cup Football* in two or three weeks of a university Easter break, and it paid his way through his degree. It came back to haunt him: in 1986 U.S. Gold repackaged it as [*World Cup Carnival*](/vault/companies/us-gold#world-cup-carnival), which *CRASH* scored 26%. After Artic folded, the two men joined [Charles Cecil](/vault/people/charles-cecil) in a company called Paragon Programming. Paragon was based in Wandsworth, and the two northerners "didn't really feel part of" it, Campbell said, so in 1987 they left and formed Tiertex.

The magazines liked the founders' credentials. *CRASH* introduced them in 1988 as "physicist John Prince and electronics engineer Donald Campbell (they should know what they're doing!)". *The One* called Prince "Dr John". Asked by *CRASH* that Christmas for his favourite Spectrum games, Campbell put *3-D Deathchase* first; the programmer he most admired was [Jon Ritman](/vault/people/jon-ritman), because "whether it's football games or isometric arcade adventures, all his stuff is very well thought out and technically competent".

## U.S. Gold's conversion house

The first job was Atari's *720°* for U.S. Gold, and Prince did most of it. *CRASH* scored the Spectrum version 81% in December 1987. More coin-op licences followed: *Rolling Thunder*, *Street Fighter*, *Last Duel*, *Thunder Blade*, *Black Tiger*, *Strider*, *Dynasty Wars*, *UN Squadron*, *Mercs* and *Alien Storm*, along with the film tie-in *Indiana Jones and the Last Crusade* and the football game *Italy 1990*. "It wasn't long before we were effectively 'US Gold's development house'," Campbell said.

What U.S. Gold bought was speed and scale. Tiertex did (mostly) all the graphics, music and code in-house, so one contract could cover up to eight formats. Geoff Brown explained why that suited U.S. Gold: "Tiertex, for instance, did *720º* and *Rolling Thunder* for us on all formats. It was cheaper that way – a hundred thousand pounds for four formats whereas it might be fifty thousand for one." Eventually U.S. Gold bought a stake in the company. Martyn Savage, who advised on the deal, said that before it Tiertex "were just a coding shop and went wherever the work was". A U.S. Gold producer, Simon Hadlington, remembered that there was then "a preference to use Tiertex whenever possible because of US Gold having a stake in them". When CentreGold floated in 1993, Tiertex became independent again.

The team grew from the two founders to eleven staff by September 1989 and fifteen by January 1990, "crammed into a very nice office in South Manchester", according to *CRASH*. *The Story of US Gold* photographs a house at 67 Palatine Road in Didsbury, "home turned office for the Tiertex team".

### How the conversions were made

Tiertex built its own tools. In November 1988 *Sinclair User* described an in-house development system: object code was assembled on an Atari ST and downloaded to the target machine along with the graphics. *CRASH* described the Spectrum side in 1990: arcade screenshots were grabbed onto the STs, which ran a Z80 editor and assembler and sprite-grabbing programs that pre-generated masks. Mark Tait, the company's musician, arranged arcade tunes on an Akai sampler and converted the MIDI data for Tiertex's own Spectrum music driver.

There was no arcade source code. For *Strider*, *The One* reported, Capcom was "understandably wary about revealing its coin-op secrets", so Prince built the game from long sessions on the arcade machine and a video of it being played through; the sprites for the ST and Amiga versions were digitised from the cabinet and retouched by hand. The whole job took just over six months. Anthony Ball, who converted *Mercs* there, remembered that the arcade machine lived in the attic, "where the main team of artists resided".

*Thunder Blade* shows the ambition. Its 3D routines were developed alongside the ST and Amiga versions, and *CRASH* reported that "full 16-bit maths is used, even on the Spectrum's 8-bit Z80 CPU", with polygon-filled skyscrapers and more than 128K of graphics and object code, loaded in sections. Tiertex also folded the arcade cabinet's separate speed control onto a single joystick.

## The reviews

The period scores do not add up to one reputation. They show a studio whose best work was very good and whose rushed work was poor, sometimes on the same game:

The best reviews were raves. *CRASH* called *Thunder Blade* "a superb conversion of the great coin-op – US Gold and Tiertex have definitely pulled off what others said couldn't be done". A *Zzap!* reviewer found Amiga *Strider* "simply one of the most playable games I've played", though the magazine later felt the Commodore 64 version had been "hacked into smaller, lesser pieces". *Mean Machines* thought "Programmers Tiertex have certainly come up with the goods" with *World Class Leaderboard*.

The worst were brutal. *Human Killing Machine*, an original fighting game Tiertex made as a follow-up to *Street Fighter*, drew this from one *Zzap!* reviewer: "The 64 version is ridiculously easy – so easy in fact, that I finished after just one go." Another ended his comment with one word: "Avoid." *Amiga Format* called *Alien Storm* in 1992 "mindless and only occasionally fun".

People who worked there are frank about why. Mick West, who converted *UN Squadron* to the ST and Amiga at Tiertex, said "there was very little in the way of quality control... We were just to copy the game, and do it as quickly as possible", and judged his own version "not a very good conversion". Campbell said the company was sometimes "slated for doing quick Atari ST to Amiga ports but we simply didn't have the budget to write an ST and an Amiga version". Hadlington, from the publisher's side, said U.S. Gold chose converters on "who was the cheapest and who could do it the quickest".

## Consoles and the Game Boy

When U.S. Gold turned to consoles, Tiertex went with it. Campbell recalled reverse-engineering an early Sega Master System, which arrived with "no documentation, tools or help", to make a demo for Geoff Brown to show Sega. Tiertex went on to program U.S. Gold's sports licences, including *Olympic Gold* for the 1992 Barcelona Olympics, *Winter Olympics: Lillehammer 94* and *World Cup USA 94*, as well as the Master System *Super Kick Off*. *PC Gamer* praised *World Cup USA 94*'s "exhaustive options system that enables you to turn just about every feature on or off". The studio also converted Delphine's *[Flashback](/vault/games/flashback)*; *Nintendo Magazine System* reported in July 1993 that Tiertex was programming it for U.S. Gold, and Campbell listed SNES, PC CD, Mega CD, Jaguar, CD-i and 3DO versions.

From 1992 Tiertex also worked for other publishers, including Domark, Grandslam and EA. In its last years it wrote mainly for handhelds. *Nintendo Power* reported in November 1996 that Tiertex had packed "all the essentials" of *Madden 97* into a 4-megabit cartridge for Black Pearl, and in June 1997 previewed its Game Boy *Hercules* for THQ, credited to "TierTex Design Studio". Campbell listed Game Boy versions of *Toy Story*, *A Bug's Life* and *FIFA* among its titles. In 2002 *Edge* named Tiertex Design Studios as the developer of the Game Boy Advance version of *Galidor*. By Campbell's account, Tiertex "withdrew silently from the games development world" in about 2003, when the handheld market stalled.
