Enix published Japan's favourite role-playing game without employing the people who made it. In 1998 a manager told *Gamers' Republic* that the company had no plans for an in-house development team: "We're a publishing house, we have game producers and managers, but all the programmers and graphic designers are either freelance or working in small companies." That model went back to the early 1980s, when Enix ran a game-programming contest and signed up its winners. Two of them, the writer Yuji Horii and the programmer Koichi Nakamura, made *[Dragon Quest](/vault/games/dragon-quest)* for it in 1986.

## Fast facts

- **Founded:** September 1975 as Eidansha Boshu Service Center; renamed Enix Corporation in August 1982.
- **Founder:** Yasuhiro Fukushima.
- **Best known for:** publishing [Dragon Quest](/vault/games/dragon-quest), developed by [Chunsoft](/vault/companies/chunsoft) for its first five games.
- **Other games:** *The Portopia Serial Murder Case*, *ActRaiser*, *Star Ocean*, *Bust A Move*.
- **Ended:** absorbed [Square](/vault/companies/square) on 1 April 2003 and took the name Square Enix.

## The contest

The company that became Enix was set up in September 1975 and took the name Enix Corporation in August 1982, according to Square Enix's corporate history. In the early 1980s it ran a contest for amateur game programmers. Horii, then a freelance writer who programmed games on his own computer, won a place, and, in *Edge*'s words, "got a foot in the door with the publisher with which he's remained until today". His first game for Enix was a detective adventure, *The Portopia Serial Murder Case* (1983), for the NEC PC-6001. Nakamura's first success was a puzzle game, *Door Door*, in the same competition, and he went on to set up his own studio, Chunsoft, when he was 19.

Enix's early catalogue for Japanese home computers was mixed. *Retro Gamer*'s guide to Japanese computers notes that it "openly made adult-only titles", as several companies that later became large did at the time.

## Dragon Quest

Nakamura worked with Horii on the Famicom version of *Portopia*, and it was Nakamura who proposed to Enix that he make a role-playing game. Akira Toriyama, the artist of the manga *Dragon Ball*, drew the characters, and the composer Koichi Sugiyama, whom Enix approached after he sent back an appreciative questionnaire card for one of its shogi games, wrote the music. *Dragon Quest* came out on the Famicom in May 1986. Chunsoft developed the first five games of the series and Enix published them. *Edge* later credited the first with laying out "the fundamentals of the Japanese RPG".

By the third game a *Dragon Quest* launch was national news. *Compute!* reported in June 1988 that *Dragon Quest III* had sold more than a million copies in a single day, with "unbelievably long lines" on television. The story that Japan then passed a law against weekday releases is not supported by public sources; *Gamasutra* reported in 2005 that Enix itself had changed its policy of releasing new games on weekdays after complaints about absences from school and work. In America the first game came out as *Dragon Warrior* in 1989, published by Nintendo; Enix published the sequels there through its own company, Enix America. After *Dragon Warrior IV* in 1992, *Edge* noted, Enix did not release another *Dragon Quest* in the US until *Dragon Warrior VII* on the PlayStation in 2001.

## A publisher of other people's games

The *Dragon Quest* model, a publisher's producer working with an outside studio, ran through the rest of Enix's list. The German magazine *Video Games* explained in 1992 that Enix had teamed up with the programming team Quintet to give itself a second pillar beside *Dragon Quest*. Quintet's first game for it was *ActRaiser* (1990), a mixture of side-scrolling action and god game with music by [Yuzo Koshiro](/vault/people/yuzo-koshiro), followed by *Soul Blazer* and *Illusion of Gaia*. tri-Ace made *Star Ocean: The Second Story* for the PlayStation, the sequel to a Super Famicom original. In 1995 *Edge* described a rivalry so close that when Square announced *Chrono Trigger*, "arch-rival Enix was quick to counter with news of a new instalment of Dragon Quest", and it reported that Enix had been "combing the UK's development community in search of potential partners for a European push". One Enix producer, Mr Futami, whose games included *ActRaiser*, told the magazine that "it can take up to two years to decide upon some of the finer aspects of the game".

On the PlayStation Enix also published odder things. *Bust A Move* (1998) was a dance game; the manager who spoke to *Gamers' Republic* explained that "a dance game like Bust a Move hasn't been done before. So just by creating the first one, we'll automatically be the top one." He also said that Enix was no longer only a game company. Enix had run a publishing business since 1988, and in some years, he said, publishing earned more than software.

## Merging with Square

At the end of 2002 Square and Enix announced that they would merge. Legally, Square's assets and liabilities were absorbed by Enix, which then took the name Square Enix on 1 April 2003; Square's president, Yoichi Wada, headed the new company. *Edge* presented it as a match between "Square's success in the west" and "Enix's domestic success". Looking back in 2010, the same magazine judged that in the rivalry between the two, Enix had been "easily winning — on home turf, at least" until they merged. In 2008 Square Enix became a holding company, Square Enix Holdings, and *Dragon Quest* continued under the new name, with Horii, Toriyama and Sugiyama still working on it.
