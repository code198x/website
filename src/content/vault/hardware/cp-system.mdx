The **CP System** was the standard arcade board [Capcom](/vault/companies/capcom) introduced in 1988 and built games for until 1995. Until then Capcom, like most arcade makers, had designed fresh hardware for each game. The CP System was designed to reverse that: one machine would stay in the cabinet, and each new game would come on a board that was mostly memory chips holding the program, graphics and sound. It carried *[Final Fight](/vault/games/final-fight)* and *[Street Fighter II](/vault/games/street-fighter-ii)*, and the protection Capcom built into it still decides whether some of those boards work today.

It later became known as CPS-1. *The Ultimate Retro Companion*, a compilation from *gamesTM*'s retro section, expanded the letters as "Capcom Play System", but Fabien Sanglard, who has written a book on the board, found that nothing about it "ever officially transpired except for a code name, CP-System". The wider history of arcade boards, and the JAMMA wiring standard that let one cabinet take any game, is told in [arcade hardware](/vault/hardware/arcade-hardware).

## Fast facts

- **Introduced:** 1988, with *Forgotten Worlds*.
- **Boards:** three stacked circuit boards, A, B and C.
- **Processors:** one [Motorola 68000](/vault/hardware/68000) at 10 MHz (12 MHz on later games); a [Z80](/vault/hardware/z80) at 3.58 MHz for sound.
- **Sound chips:** a Yamaha YM2151 for [FM music](/vault/technologies/fm-synthesis) and an OKI MSM6295 for sampled sound.
- **Graphics:** two custom chips, CPS-A and CPS-B; a 384 × 224 picture at about 59.6 frames a second.
- **Games:** about thirty, from *Forgotten Worlds* (1988) to *Mega Man: The Power Battle* (1995).
- **Later versions:** the CP System Dash (1992), then the CPS II board (1993).

## Why Capcom built it

Capcom's publicity made the board sound like a huge bet. In June 1989 *The Games Machine* reported a "super chip" built over two and a half years, which squeezed "the power of ten normal arcade circuit boards into two microchips" and cost £5.5 million to develop. "Capcom have practically staked their company on the new super chips," the magazine wrote. Sanglard gives a different figure, two years and five million dollars, which he took from a *Forgotten Worlds* flyer and himself treats as questionable. *ACE* said only that Capcom "threw millions of dollars" at it. In a passage Sanglard quotes from a 1989 interview in the Japanese arcade magazine *Gamest*, Capcom's producer [Yoshiki Okamoto](/vault/people/yoshiki-okamoto) put it plainly: "we have gambled everything on it."

The aims, as Sanglard lists them, were practical. Building one standard machine would cut production costs. New game boards "containing mostly ROMs" could be sold to arcade operators separately and more cheaply, so they could change games more often; *The Games Machine* made the same point, that the board would let "your local arcade owner" swap games in cabinets. Programmers would get stable tools instead of rewriting them for every new board. The graphics had to stand comparison with [Sega](/vault/companies/sega) and [Namco](/vault/companies/namco).

The last aim was to stop the bootleggers. Capcom's earlier boards were built from chips anyone could buy, so a pirate could read the game out of its ROMs, wire up the same parts and sell a copy for less than the original. Sanglard cites Matt Leone's oral history of *Street Fighter II* for an estimate that 200,000 bootleg Capcom boards were in circulation in Mexico.

## Three boards in a stack

The CP System was three printed circuit boards plugged on top of one another and handled as one unit. *ACE* described it in November 1989 as "made up of interlocking mother boards", so that to change a game "in theory you would just replace the necessary sections, rather than having to invest in a completely new board."

- **Board A** held the processors, the sound chips, the CPS-A graphics chip and the video memory. It was the same for every game.
- **Board B** held the game itself: its ROM chips sat in sockets, together with small programmable logic chips (PALs) that told the hardware where each kind of data lay. Because each game laid out its ROMs differently, its PALs were different too.
- **Board C** carried the second graphics chip, the CPS-B, and with it most of Capcom's protection. Capcom revised it far more often than the other two.

The custom chips were where the secrets lay. *ACE* noticed "two custom-designed, very highly integrated chips, that make it really fly", and added that Capcom was "understandably, reluctant to reveal more". Sanglard writes that Capcom had [Ricoh](/vault/companies/ricoh) make the CPS-A and CPS-B for it alone, with metal grids layered over the silicon to make decapping, opening a chip to photograph and copy its circuits, harder.

## Processors and sound

The game ran on one Motorola 68000, clocked at 10 MHz on the early boards and at 12 MHz on some later ones. A Z80 at 3.58 MHz looked after the sound. It drove a Yamaha YM2151, the FM synthesis chip that played the music, and an OKI MSM6295, which played back recorded samples such as voices and effects.

The period press got the processor count wrong. *ACE* told its readers that "the CP has got 2 68000's chugging away inside", a claim its licensed Italian edition, *K*, repeated a month later. A *Forgotten Worlds* flyer, according to Sanglard, went further and claimed three. There was only ever one.

## Picture and layers

The CP System drew a picture 384 pixels wide and 224 high, refreshed about 59.6 times a second. Its dot clock ran at 8 MHz, and each frame was 512 dots by 262 lines in total, the rest being the blank margins a television needs between lines and between frames. Sanglard sets the usable width beside the competition: 320 pixels on the [Mega Drive](/vault/systems/sega-mega-drive) and the [Neo Geo](/vault/systems/neo-geo), 256 on the [Super NES](/vault/systems/super-nintendo).

The picture was built from layers stacked on top of each other:

- **Three scrolling tile layers**, made of 8 × 8, 16 × 16 and 32 × 32 pixel tiles. Each could be scrolled independently, so backgrounds could move at different speeds for a sense of depth (parallax).
- **A sprite layer** for characters and other moving objects. The hardware could draw at most 256 sprite tiles in a frame.
- **Two starfield layers**, which drew points of light from a short program in ROM instead of from tiles. Only *Forgotten Worlds* and *[Strider](/vault/games/strider)* used them for stars; *[Ghouls 'n Ghosts](/vault/games/ghouls-n-ghosts)* put its dark sky on one.

Each colour was a 16-bit value: four bits each of red, green and blue, and four bits of brightness, which Sanglard counts as 65,536 possible colours. Colours were grouped into palettes of 16, and each layer had 32 palettes to choose from.

Graphics memory was rationed. Board B had room for 12 MiB of graphics ROM, but ROM chips were expensive, and Sanglard found that no game was given the maximum. Most had 2 to 4 MiB. *Final Fight* had 2 MiB, the *Street Fighter II* games 6 MiB, and *Dynasty Wars* 8 MiB.

## The games

About thirty games appeared on the CP System between 1988 and 1995. Sanglard counts more than thirty and *The Ultimate Retro Companion* 29, from *Forgotten Worlds* to *Mega Man: The Power Battle*. *The Games Machine*'s reviewer called *Forgotten Worlds* "the first of Capcom's games to use the awesome new generation CP graphics chip".

The order after that is less settled. Sanglard's table of games lists *Ghouls 'n Ghosts* second, and the game's own ROMs carry the date 26 October 1988. *The Games Machine*, reporting from Britain in 1989, called *Strider* Capcom's second "super chip" game.

The rest of the list includes *U.N. Squadron* and *Final Fight* (1989); *Street Fighter II*, *[Captain Commando](/vault/games/captain-commando)* and *Knights of the Round* (1991); and the *Street Fighter II* updates *Champion Edition* and *Hyper Fighting* (1992).

Contemporary reaction was mixed. *ACE* found the games "technologically outstanding in their own ways", but thought that "other than Strider" they had "shown little originality of gameplay". At a show in 1989, *Computer and Video Games* found *U.N. Squadron*, "along with virtually every other CP System game", to be "a bit of a lack of excitement".

## Protection and the suicide battery

Capcom's protection lived in the CPS-B chip on board C, and its central idea was that the chip behaved differently for each game. Sanglard counts about 25 versions. The chip's registers, the addresses a game wrote to when it scrolled its layers or loaded palettes, sat in different places from one version to the next. If the CPS-B saw values written to the wrong registers, Sanglard explains, it turned every palette black: the game kept running and the sound kept playing, but the screen stayed dark. Sanglard also quotes Capcom's chief executive, Kenzo Tsujimoto, calling the CP System "the only PCB hardware today that cannot be copied", though he footnotes the passage to the same *Gamest* interview with Okamoto.

From *Three Wonders* in 1991, every board used the same chip, the CPS-B-21. Instead of having its behaviour fixed in the silicon, it kept each game's configuration in a few bytes of RAM, and it could multiply two numbers so that the game could check the answer. RAM forgets its contents without power, so a battery on the board kept the chip alive while the cabinet was switched off. When the battery went flat, the configuration was lost and the board stopped working, which is why collectors call it the "suicide battery". Hobbyists now bring dead boards back in two ways: by patching the game so that it no longer needs the lost settings, which they call "phoenixing", or by writing the configuration back into the chip, "de-suiciding" it.

## Bootlegs

The protection did not stop the pirates. Bootleg and modified *Street Fighter II* boards spread through arcades, among them the "Rainbow" editions, which *Wireframe* remembered turning up at local leisure centres. *Edge* later described *Street Fighter II' Turbo: Hyper Fighting* (1992) as the series' "one final outing on the aging CPS1 board in an attempt to counter the ever-increasing number of pirate copies". Even the custom chips were eventually copied: Sanglard reports that replica CPS-A and CPS-B chips were made under the name COMCO.

## Making the games

According to Sanglard, Capcom's programmers wrote CP System games on Sharp X68000 home computers, a Japanese machine built round the same 68000. Capcom artist Akira Yasuda, known as Akiman, said in 2018 that the company's development kit, called CAT-1, was started during the making of *Street Fighter II* and ran on the X68000. Sanglard's own reading is that the X68000 was a station for writing and testing code, not a machine that could run the arcade games as they were. Capcom did, however, convert several of its CP System games for the X68000 itself.

## CP System Dash

In 1992 Capcom brought out the CP System Dash, sometimes called CPS-1.5. It was the CP System boxed in grey plastic, with a fourth board for QSound, a positional stereo system. The sound processor was a special Z80, called Kabuki, that decrypted its program as it ran, using a key kept alive by a second battery. Five games used it, among them *[Cadillacs and Dinosaurs](/vault/games/cadillacs-and-dinosaurs)* and *Warriors of Fate*. *Wireframe* called the Dash "stopgap" hardware.

## The CPS Changer

Capcom also sold the board for the home. *Wireframe* dates the CPS Changer, "a home console version of the CPS hardware", to 1994. In March 1995 the Brazilian magazine *SuperGamePower* reported a Capcom adapter, which it called the "CPS II Changer", that ran arcade boards on a television; the unit and a *Super Street Fighter II Turbo* board sold together for US$330.

## Emulation

In February 1998 the Italian edition of *[The Games Machine](/vault/magazines/the-games-machine-italy)* reported that the Callus emulator could run "almost all the games of Capcom's CPS-1 board", listing *Final Fight*, three versions of *Street Fighter II*, *Strider*, *U.N. Squadron*, *Captain Commando*, *Knights of the Round* and *Rockman: The Power Battle* among others. A year later Brazil's *SuperGamePower* told its readers that "Callus emulates Capcom's CPS1 board". [MAME](/vault/emulators/mame) runs the games too; its driver for the board, credited to Paul Leaman, records the many versions of the A, B and C boards and the bootlegs.

## After the CP System

In 1993 Capcom followed the board with CPS II, which made its debut with *Super Street Fighter II*. *Edge* described it as "an encased base board" that game boards plugged into "without a single ROM chip in sight". CPS II encrypted the game program itself, and *Wireframe* reports that its encryption was not fully reverse-engineered until 2007.
