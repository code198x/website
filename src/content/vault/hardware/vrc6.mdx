The VRC6 is a chip that [Konami](/vault/companies/konami) put inside some of its Famicom cartridges. Like other mapper chips, it lets the console reach more program and graphics data than it can address at once. Its distinction is sound. The Famicom's own sound hardware, the [APU](/vault/hardware/apu) inside the 2A03 processor, has five voices: two pulse waves, a triangle, noise and samples. A VRC6 cartridge adds three more: two pulse waves of its own and a sawtooth wave, a buzzy, bright tone that none of the console's own five voices generates.

Only three commercial games used it: *Akumajō Densetsu*, Konami's Japanese *[Castlevania](/vault/games/castlevania) III*, which came out on 22 December 1989, *Mōryō Senki MADARA* and *Esper Dream 2*. The Famicom's cartridge slot carries the console's sound out to the cartridge and back again, so a cartridge can mix in sound of its own. The NES's slot has no such path. Kevin Horton, who documented a real chip in 1999 with a multimeter, oscilloscope and logic probe, called it "a fami-only chip".

## Fast facts

- **Maker:** [Konami](/vault/companies/konami). The chips are marked VRC VI.
- **Package:** 48-pin DIP. Known parts are marked 053328, 053329 and 053330.
- **First game:** *Akumajō Densetsu*, 22 December 1989.
- **Games:** *Akumajō Densetsu*, *Mōryō Senki MADARA* and *Esper Dream 2*.
- **Sound:** two pulse channels and one sawtooth, mixed with the 2A03's five voices on a Famicom.
- **Program ROM:** a 16 KB window at `$8000` and an 8 KB window at `$C000`, with the top 8 KB fixed.
- **Graphics ROM:** eight 1 KB banks.
- **Interrupt:** a counter that counts CPU cycles or scanlines.
- **iNES mappers:** 24 (VRC6a) and 26 (VRC6b).

## The pulse channels

A pulse wave switches between two levels, on and off. Its duty cycle is the fraction of each cycle it spends on, and changing it changes the tone: a narrow pulse sounds thin and nasal, a 50% square wave hollow. The 2A03's pulse channels have four duty settings. Each VRC6 pulse channel has eight, in sixteenths: 1/16 (6.25%), 2/16, 3/16 and so on up to 8/16 (50%). That gives a composer finer steps between thin and hollow, and more room for the tone to move during a note.

Each channel also has a 4-bit volume, so sixteen levels, and a mode bit that ignores the duty setting altogether. With the bit set, the channel stops switching on and off and outputs its volume as a steady level. Horton's notes call this the "digitized" mode.

The VRC6 pulse is upside down compared with the 2A03's: at the same duty setting it is inverted in polarity, according to NESdev. As with the 2A03's 75% setting, the flip changes the wave's phase, not how it sounds on its own.

## The sawtooth

A sawtooth wave rises steadily and then drops straight back to the bottom, and it is rich in harmonics. The VRC6 makes one with an accumulator, a counter that keeps a running total. On each step the chip adds a rate value to the total and sends the top 5 bits of it to the output. After six additions it resets the total to zero and begins again. The result is a ramp of seven steps, a staircase that the ear hears as a sawtooth.

The rate value sets how tall the ramp grows, so it works as the channel's volume. It has a ceiling. The accumulator holds 8 bits, and NESdev gives 42 as the largest rate that six additions can carry without overflowing. Larger values wrap round and distort the sound.

## Pitch and output

Each channel's pitch comes from a 12-bit period register, one bit longer than the 2A03's 11-bit pulse periods, clocked by the CPU's 1.79 MHz. For a period value `t`:

- pulse frequency = CPU clock / (16 × (t + 1))
- sawtooth frequency = CPU clock / (14 × (t + 1))

In NESdev's account, the sawtooth's 14 comes from its seven steps, each taking two ticks of the period timer.

A further register at `$9003` can halt all three oscillators or multiply all their frequencies by 16 or 256. NESdev reports that all three games write `$00` to it at start-up.

The chip does not produce sound directly. It adds the two 4-bit pulse levels and the 5-bit sawtooth into one 6-bit number on six output pins. On the cartridge board those pins drive a resistor ladder, a row of resistors that turns a binary number into a voltage. That is the digital-to-analogue converter, or DAC. NESdev notes that it appears to be linear, where the 2A03's mixing is not. The voltage is then mixed with the 2A03's sound.

## How the sound reaches the television

On the Famicom's 60-pin cartridge connector, pin 45 carries the sound from the 2A03, already amplified, and pin 46 takes sound to the RF output that feeds the television. An ordinary cartridge joins the two with a wire. A VRC6 cartridge passes the 2A03's sound through its own mixing circuit, adds the VRC6's output, and sends the combined sound back on pin 46.

The NES's 72-pin connector has no such pair of pins, so a VRC6 would have nowhere to send its output. The chip was never fitted to an NES cartridge. Firebug's mapper document, version 0.80, put it as "As far as I know, it was not used on U.S. games." To hear cartridge audio on an NES, the console has to be modified. The homebrew community's convention, as NESdev describes it, is to carry expansion audio on the connector's EXP6 pin.

When Konami released *Akumajō Densetsu* in the West as *Castlevania III: Dracula's Curse*, the game was rebuilt on Nintendo's [MMC5](/vault/hardware/mmc5) mapper and its music plays through the 2A03 alone. The [MMC5](/vault/hardware/mmc5) and [APU](/vault/hardware/apu) entries describe that version.

## Who designed it

Hidenori Maezawa, a Konami composer and sound programmer, told *1UP.com*'s Jeremy Parish in 2009 that he had been involved in designing the chip. In Maezawa's words: "I was actually the one who developed the chip. Of course, there were other technical people who put the parts together, but I was involved in its design. A chip is small, but the prototype is huge!" He added: "I think the chip was first used in Akumajou Densetsu." He did not work on the American port, so he did not have to cut the music down for the NES himself.

Masahiro Ueno, a Konami programmer, recalled in *Retro Gamer* in 2013 that Konami "had the best sound team". Of his team's move to the Super Famicom, he said that "we felt we'd done everything we could do on the Famicom without additional chips in the cartridge".

## The first game

NESdev's list of mappers by release date, Maezawa's recollection and a 2023 *GAME Watch* review of Konami's Famicom soundtrack collection all put *Akumajō Densetsu* first. *GAME Watch* calls it the first title to use "KONAMIが独自に開発した拡張音源「VRC6」", the expansion sound chip Konami developed itself, with two square-wave voices and one sawtooth. A 2015 *Famitsu.com* report on a reissue of the *MADARA* soundtracks says otherwise: it calls *Mōryō Senki MADARA* "KONAMI初のファミコン拡張音源（VRCVI）使用作品", Konami's first game to use a Famicom expansion sound chip.

## The mapper side

The VRC6 is also a mapper, a chip that practises [bank switching](/vault/techniques/bank-switching): it swaps sections of a large ROM in and out of the small windows the console can see. On the program side, a 16 KB bank switches in at `$8000` and an 8 KB bank at `$C000`. The top 8 KB is fixed, so the interrupt vectors at the top of memory never move. For graphics, eight registers each choose 1 KB of the [PPU](/vault/hardware/ppu)'s pattern tables, the same fine division as the [VRC2](/vault/hardware/vrc2).

It has an interrupt counter, the same design as Konami's VRC4 and VRC7. A game sets it going and the chip interrupts the CPU when the count is up, counting either CPU cycles or scanlines, which it measures in CPU cycles. An interrupt like this lets a game change the picture partway down the screen, for a status bar or a split scroll, without timing the change by hand.

The chip can also supply nametables, the maps of which tile goes where on screen, from cartridge ROM, and it supports a board with more graphics ROM. Konami never built that board, and NESdev reports that none of the three games uses ROM nametables.

### VRC6a and VRC6b

The chip tells its registers apart by two of the CPU's address lines, A0 and A1, and Konami's two boards wire them the opposite way round. On the VRC6a, used by *Akumajō Densetsu*, the registers sit in one order. On the VRC6b, used by *MADARA* and *Esper Dream 2*, the two lines are swapped, so the registers at `$x001` and `$x002` trade places. Emulators give the two wirings different iNES mapper numbers, 24 for the VRC6a and 26 for the VRC6b. As the [VRC2](/vault/hardware/vrc2) entry explains, those numbers are an emulator convention that Konami never used.

NESdev lists three revisions of the part, marked 053328, 053329 and 053330 "VRC VI", and identifies the 053330's die as an S-MOS SLA7340 gate array, a general-purpose chip customised by its final wiring layer. That detail comes from NESdev alone.

## Afterlives

The VRC6's three channels outlived its three games. The NSF format, which stores NES music as a small program that plays on the hardware or in an emulator, has a header flag that marks a tune as using VRC6 audio.

[FamiTracker](/vault/software/famitracker), the free Windows tracker for NES music, added VRC6 support in version 0.2.9 in January 2009. Composers writing new NES-style music could then use the sawtooth and the finer pulses, and export the result as NSF.

*[Shovel Knight](/vault/games/shovel-knight)* (2014) used that path. Yacht Club Games broke several of the NES's rules for its 8-bit look, and one was the sound: its music has three more channels than the NES's five, from Konami's VRC6, an expansion chip some Famicom cartridges carried. Jake Kaufman wrote the main score in FamiTracker.
