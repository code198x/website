**Paddle controllers** were among the first video game inputs - rotary devices that translated knob rotation into on-screen movement. From Pong (1972) to Breakout (1976) to Arkanoid (1986), paddles defined an era of gaming with their precise, analogue single-axis control.

Unlike joysticks, paddles had no centering - your on-screen position stayed exactly where you left it, enabling the precise positioning that bat-and-ball games required.

## Fast facts

- **Technology:** Rotary potentiometer
- **First use:** 1972 ([Pong](/vault/games/pong))
- **Key characteristic:** Continuous rotation, no centering
- **Atari model:** CX-30 (sold in pairs)
- **Arcade variant:** Spinner (360° rotation)

## How they work

Mechanically, a paddle is a knob, a potentiometer and a button:

- A knob rotates a potentiometer
- Potentiometer outputs variable resistance
- Console converts that resistance to a number
- On-screen element moves accordingly

No springs, no centering - position is absolute.

### On the C64, the sound chip does it

The paddle inputs on a Commodore 64 are not wired to the joystick logic or to a
dedicated interface. They go to the **SID**, whose datasheet lists among its
features that "two A/D converters are provided for interfacing SID with
potentiometers", and names the use: "these can be used for *paddles* in a game
environment or as front panel controls in a music synthesizer".

Reading one is a single load. `POTX` at register `$19` returns "values ranging
from 0 at minimum resistance, to 255 ($FF) at maximum resistance", and the value
"is always valid and is updated every 512 φ2 clock cycles" — about two thousand
readings a second, with no polling loop to write.

The conversion is a timing trick rather than a voltage measurement: a capacitor
from the pot pin to ground is charged through the potentiometer, and the chip
measures how long it takes. Commodore specifies `RC = 4.7E-4`, recommending
470 kΩ and 1000 pF, and warns that "a separate pot and cap are required for each
POT pin". A bigger capacitor buys steadier readings.

## Atari Paddle Controllers

Atari's CX-30 paddles:

- Sold in pairs (two paddles, one port)
- Each included a fire button
- Cables notoriously short
- Required for certain games (Kaboom!)

Many 2600 owners only had joysticks - some games went unplayed.

## Spinners

Spinners were continuous-rotation paddles:

- 360° rotation without stops
- Used in Tempest, Tron, Arkanoid
- Created "spinning" momentum effect
- More complex mechanism

## Decline

Paddles faded because:

- Joysticks worked for more game types
- Manufacturing complexity
- Single-axis limitation
- Games moved to multi-directional control
