A **D-sub connector** belongs to a family used for many of a computer's
connections to the outside world: game controls, communications, printed output,
and connections to displays and drives. Across different machines, the familiar
D-shaped shell kept appearing with a different job behind it.

That breadth is why it belongs in computing history. D-sub was reusable physical
connection hardware, rather than a connector tied to one manufacturer's machine
or one kind of signal. The nine-pin connector drawn here is one member of that
larger family.

## Why it mattered

A few examples show how far the family reached:

- **Game input on the [Commodore 64](/vault/systems/commodore-64).** Its two nine-pin game ports accept joysticks or paddles; one also accepts a light pen.
- **Several jobs on the [Amiga](/vault/systems/commodore-amiga).** Nine-pin controller ports accept mice, trackballs, joysticks and other supported input devices. Separate 25-pin connections serve serial communications and parallel printers. Two different 23-pin connections serve video and external floppy drives.
- **Communications and printing on PCs.** IBM's PC Server 520 provides nine-pin serial ports for serial equipment and a 25-pin parallel port for a printer or another parallel device.

These are documented in the [*Commodore 64 Programmer's Reference Guide*,
p. 343](https://www.devili.iki.fi/Computers/Commodore/C64/Programmers_Reference/Chapter_6/page_343.html),
the [*Amiga Hardware Reference Manual*, appendix E and chapter 8](https://amigadev.elowar.com/read/ADCD_2.1/Hardware_Manual_guide/node0000.html),
and [IBM's PC Server 520 connector guide](https://www.ibm.com/support/pages/inputoutput-connectors-diagram-pc-server-520-8641-ez0-ezv-ezl-ezs-eze).

The useful commonality was the physical connection: a shaped shell to orient the
halves, multiple contacts in a compact space, and provision for securing the
connection. Designers could use members of the same family for different
circuits. For the user, those connections turned the computer into something
they could play, point at, print from, or exchange data with.

The trade-off was that a familiar connector did not guarantee a familiar
interface. A nine-pin connector alone does not tell us whether we are looking at a
game port or a PC serial port. Even the Amiga's
serial and parallel connections use the same contact count for different jobs.
**The connector identifies the physical connection; the pinout and interface
specification explain what it carries.**

## What holds it together?

Our exploded drawing separates the pieces so we can follow their relationships.
The contacts pass through holes in the **insulating insert**. That insert keeps
them positioned and apart. The metal shell surrounds the mating area; the flange
and mounting hardware support the assembly.

## What does DE-9 mean?

In the standard-density range, **E** identifies the shell size used for nine
contacts. **B** identifies the larger shell used for 25. That gives us **DE-9**
and **DB-25**. The shell letter and contact count both matter; the letter is not
another way of saying “connector”. Amphenol's
[D-sub overview](https://www.amphenol-cs.com/connectors/d-sub.html) distinguishes
shell sizes from contact counts, including the different counts used by
high-density versions.

The illustrated male connector has projecting pins. Its mating partner uses
socket contacts. Amphenol lists separate nine-way pin and socket versions in
its [economy-range catalogue, p. 5](https://cdn.amphenol-cs.com/media/wysiwyg/files/documentation/datasheet/inputoutput/io_dsub_economy.pdf).

Source: Commodore, [*Commodore 64 Programmer's Reference Guide*, appendix I,

The manual names the analogue inputs POT AY/AX on port 1 and POT BY/BX on port 2.
The direction and fire inputs are active low: a normal digital joystick closes
a switch to ground to indicate a press.

The ports have the same basic pin assignments but reach different CIA register
bits. **Control port 1** uses CIA #1 port B at `$DC01` (56321);
**control port 2** uses port A at `$DC00` (56320). Pin 6 is bit 4 in each case.
These lines also participate in keyboard scanning, so their direction and the
keyboard circuitry matter when interpreting a read. See the manual's register
map, pp. 328–329, and [“The Game Ports”, pp. 343–344](https://www.devili.iki.fi/Computers/Commodore/C64/Programmers_Reference/Chapter_6/page_343.html).

## From the fire button to a bit

Follow **control port 2, pin 6**. With that CIA line configured as an input,
the pull-up holds an unpressed fire input high. Pressing fire closes the
joystick switch between pin 6 and ground at pin 8. The input goes low, and
bit 4 of `$DC00` reads as zero. Releasing the button opens the switch and lets
the input return high.

That ground connection is intentional. It pulls down a signal with a limited
pull-up current; it does not directly join the +5 V supply to ground. Commodore
describes the active-low button in [“The Game Ports”, p. 344](https://www.devili.iki.fi/Computers/Commodore/C64/Programmers_Reference/Chapter_6/page_344.html)
and the CIA's pull-ups in appendix M, pp. 427 and 429.

## Why the wrong device can damage a port

The shell checks physical orientation, not electrical compatibility. A wrong
device might do nothing or produce incorrect inputs. Damage becomes possible
when its wiring or electronics create a connection the computer was not
designed to tolerate. There are several distinct ways this can happen.

**A short from power to ground.** Suppose an incompatible device or miswired
cable connects C64 pin 7 directly to pin 8. It joins +5 V to ground through a
low-resistance path: a short circuit. Current is then limited by the wiring,
power supply and any protection, rather than the intended peripheral load.
The result can be a voltage drop, a reset, heating or damage. The manual's
50 mA load rating does **not** promise a circuit that safely limits every fault
to 50 mA. This is an example derived from the pinout, not a claim that every
incompatible controller makes this connection.

**Two outputs fighting.** A peripheral with electronics may actively drive a
line high while the computer drives it low. This is called *output contention*:
current flows through the competing output stages. It differs from a passive
joystick switch, and can stress the chips. The CIA's port lines can be outputs
as well as inputs; their keyboard-scanning role is one reason not to assume
that they only listen. See the [6526 specification in appendix M,
p. 429](https://www.commodore.ca/manuals/c64_programmers_reference/c64-programmers_reference_guide-07-appendices.pdf).

**The wrong voltage.** RS-232 serial signals can swing below ground as well as
above it. They are not the logic-level inputs expected by the C64's joystick
circuit. Feeding such a signal into a CIA pin can exceed the chip's limits;
an adapter that only rearranges wires does not translate voltage levels.
Texas Instruments distinguishes the cable and logic sides in its
[*RS232 Design Guide*, sections 2 and 3.3](https://www.ti.com/lit/pdf/SDAA219).
The CIA's maximum ratings and damage warning appear in appendix M, p. 422.

Before connecting a device, check its intended machine and interface, the
pin assignments, supply requirements and signal levels. Switch the equipment
off before connecting or disconnecting these legacy control-port devices;
powering off does not make incompatible wiring safe when power returns.
A matching plug is the beginning of that check, not its conclusion.


## When compatibility depends on a switch

A controller labelled **multi-system** may need the correct setting before it
is connected. If a selector changes which connector pin a button reaches,
its position changes the electrical circuit as well as the button's function.

An MSX joystick's second trigger joins **pin 7 to pin 8**, as shown in the
[*MSX2 Technical Handbook*, figure 5.23](https://konamiman.github.io/MSX2-Technical-Handbook/md/Chapter5a.html#52-joystick-use).
On a C64, those same pins carry **+5 V and ground**. A joystick wired that way
can therefore appear to work until the second button is pressed: the directions
and first fire button share the C64's switch connections, but the second button
shorts its supply.

The drawing below shows one way a selector could change that connection.
In the C64 position, the extra button duplicates the normal fire button on
pin 6. In the MSX position, it becomes a separate trigger on pin 7. Both use
pin 8 as the switch common. This is an illustrative circuit, not the internal
wiring of a particular commercial controller; other switches may change
additional connections.

A switch's purpose matters too. A button-swap switch merely exchanges the
buttons' jobs; an autofire switch enables repeated firing. Neither label
promises that the controller changes its wiring for another machine. Check
the controller's instructions for the exact model and select the intended
system before connecting it with the equipment powered off. Protection against
a wrong setting depends on the circuit, not on the presence of a selector.
