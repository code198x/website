The **light pen** was a pointing device that detected the electron beam of a CRT display, allowing users to draw or select items directly on the screen. Popular in the 1980s home computer era for art programs, light pens offered an intuitive drawing experience years before graphics tablets became affordable.

Like light guns, light pens only worked with CRT displays and faded as technology moved on.

## Fast facts

- **First use:** 1952 (MIT Whirlwind computer)
- **Home computer era:** 1980s
- **Technology:** Photodiode detects CRT beam timing
- **Primary use:** Drawing, menu selection
- **Limitation:** CRT-only, arm fatigue

## How they worked

Light pens detected screen position by sensing the electron beam:

1. **Phototransistor** in pen tip detects the brief flash of phosphor as the CRT raster scans past
2. The timing of that flash is captured — usually by latching a hardware counter when the comparator output goes high
3. Computer calculates screen coordinates from when the hit occurred relative to the start-of-frame interrupt — given a fixed scanline timing, the cycle count between vsync and pen-fire localises the pen on the screen
4. Pen position tracked once per frame, and once only — a second hit in the same frame is discarded

Unlike light guns, light pens needed to touch or nearly touch the screen.

### Why CRT-only

The technique relies on a *scanning* electron beam. LCDs (and any progressive-scan flat panel) refresh the whole frame at once — there's no scanning beam to detect. Even on real CRT hardware fed through a modern scan converter, light pens are unreliable: the converter usually buffers and re-clocks a frame, breaking the timing relationship the pen depends on.

### Hardware integration examples

- **C64**: The VIC-II latches the beam position into `$D013` (LPENX) and `$D014` (LPENY) when the light pen pin fires, and can raise an interrupt (ILP) on the same event. Software just reads those registers — but not at equal precision in both axes. X comes from a 9-bit, 512-state counter of which only the top 8 bits are latched, so horizontal resolution is two dots; Y latches to a single raster line. Commodore also warns that one reading is not enough: "you must take several samples before turning the light pen to the screen (3 or more samples, average), depending upon the characteristics of your light pen".
- **ZX Spectrum**: No dedicated hardware register — software polls a status bit during the frame and counts T-states from the start-of-frame IRQ to localise the hit (see DK'Tronics Light Pen, below).

The Koala Pad is sometimes confused with a light pen but is a **graphics tablet** with a stylus — different operating principle (resistive sensor on the pad, not CRT timing).

## Why They Faded

Light pens had significant problems:

- **Arm fatigue** - Holding at screen level
- **Precision** - Difficult to draw finely
- **CRT-only** - Technology limitation
- **Competition** - Mice and tablets were better
- **Cost** - Decent pens weren't cheap

By the late 1980s, mice and joysticks dominated.

## The Graphics Tablet Succession

What light pens couldn't do, tablets could:

- Draw comfortably on desk
- Work with any display
- Pressure sensitivity (eventually)
- Better precision

Wacom tablets became the artist's choice.
