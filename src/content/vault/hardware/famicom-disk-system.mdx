For a few years in the late 1980s, a Japanese Famicom owner who had finished a game could take the disk back to a toy shop, feed it into a kiosk and walk away with a different game on it for 500 yen. That was the promise of the Famicom Disk System, the add-on [Nintendo](/vault/companies/nintendo) launched on 21 February 1986. Its disks held more than the cartridges of the day, cost less to make, and could be written to, so a game could save a player's progress without a battery.

Nintendo said that from then on the best Famicom games would be exclusive to the Disk System, and at first they were. *[The Legend of Zelda](/vault/games/legend-of-zelda)* went on sale the same day, and *[Metroid](/vault/games/metroid)*, *[Kid Icarus](/vault/games/kid-icarus)* and the first *[Castlevania](/vault/games/castlevania)* all began life on disk. *Retro Gamer* reports that half a million units sold in the first three months and about 4.5 million in all. But the format never left Japan, and by the end of the decade publishers were going back to cartridges.

## Fast facts

- **Maker:** [Nintendo](/vault/companies/nintendo), for the Japanese Family Computer (Famicom), the console sold in the West as the [Nintendo Entertainment System](/vault/systems/nintendo-entertainment-system).
- **Launched:** 21 February 1986 in Japan, at ¥15,000.
- **Parts:** a disk drive (HVC-022) and a RAM adapter (HVC-023) that plugs into the Famicom's cartridge slot.
- **Memory:** 32 KB of program RAM, 8 KB of graphics RAM and an 8 KB BIOS ROM, all in the RAM adapter.
- **Media:** the Disk Card, a modified Mitsumi Quick Disk, holding about 64 KB per side.
- **Sound:** one extra wavetable channel with frequency modulation, mixed with the Famicom's own sound.
- **Disk Writer service:** ¥500 to rewrite a disk with another game, until 30 September 2003.
- **Sales:** about 4.5 million, all in Japan, according to *Retro Gamer*.

## Why a disk

Cartridges were expensive. A game on a cartridge carried its program in mask ROM, made in a factory months ahead and in quantity. *Edge* summed up the Disk System in 1998 as "an attempt to counter rising ROM costs", noting that disk games cost roughly half the price of cartridges. *Retro Gamer* gives the prices: Disk System games sold for about ¥2,500 to ¥3,000 when new cartridge games cost ¥5,000 to ¥7,000.

The disks also held more. Nintendo's own description says a Disk Card had about three times the capacity of a ROM cartridge. When the Disk System was being prepared, [Shigeru Miyamoto](/vault/people/shigeru-miyamoto)'s team treated *[Super Mario Bros.](/vault/games/super-mario-bros)*, a 40 KB cartridge, as a last big cartridge game before the disks arrived. And a disk could be written to. Few cartridges of 1986 had battery-backed memory for saving, so an adventure that took several sittings needed a password or a disk. *Retro Gamer* quotes the collector Sean Corse: *Metroid* and *Zelda* "were long adventures that required multiple gaming sessions to complete, facilitated by the Disk System's save feature".

## Two boxes and a cable

The Disk System came in two parts. The drive sat beside the console, powered by six AA batteries or its own mains adapter, and connected by a cable to the RAM adapter, a cartridge-shaped unit that plugged into the Famicom.

The RAM adapter did the real work. It held the memory the games loaded into and a custom chip, the RP2C33, which drove the disk interface, generated the extra sound, and provided a timer interrupt the Famicom itself lacks. The NESdev Wiki gives the memory map:

| Address | Contents |
|---|---|
| CPU `$6000-$DFFF` | 32 KB of RAM, loaded from disk |
| CPU `$E000-$FFFF` | 8 KB BIOS ROM |
| PPU `$0000-$1FFF` | 8 KB of RAM for tile graphics |

Everything in that map is fixed; nothing is switched as it is on cartridge mappers such as the [MMC1](/vault/hardware/mmc1) or [MMC3](/vault/hardware/mmc3). A game loaded what it needed from disk into RAM, and loaded more when it needed something else. The BIOS ran at power-on, played the start-up screen, and offered routines for reading and writing files. The timer counts down once per CPU cycle and can raise an interrupt when it reaches zero, repeatedly if a game asks it to.

Sharp also sold the Twin Famicom, a console with the Famicom and the Disk System built into one case.

## The Disk Card

Nintendo based its disk on the Quick Disk, a cheap format made by Mitsumi. A Quick Disk drive has only one motor. It stores data on a single spiral track, and the head moves in towards the centre as the disk turns, so there is no random access: a program waits for the head to reach the file it wants, much as it would on tape, only far faster. NESdev puts a full pass over one side at about seven seconds. The drive clicks as the head springs back to the start.

The commonly used disk image format holds 65,500 bytes per side, and the physical disk holds a little more. A double-sided card therefore carried about a megabit, which is how the team making *Super Mario Bros.* thought of it.

Nintendo built its own protections into the card. The case has recesses spelling "NINTENDO" that fit moulds inside the drive, so an ordinary Quick Disk will not go in. Each disk side begins with the text `*NINTENDO-HVC*`, and the BIOS refuses to read a disk without it. By convention, the first file on a disk was the licence screen shown at start-up, a file named `KYODAKU-` (Japanese for "permission"), and the BIOS checked for it before running the game.

The cards had tabs that could be broken off to protect a side from being written, like a floppy disk. What they lacked was a shutter over the magnetic surface. *Retro Gamer*'s contributor Laurent Kermel recalls that most disks relied on a wax-paper sleeve instead, so dust, fingerprints and scratches reached the film, and a disk left near a television's speaker could be damaged by its magnets.

## The extra voice

The RP2C33 adds one sound channel to the Famicom's five. It plays a 64-step waveform that the program writes into the chip, so a game can shape the sound of the note, and a second unit bends the channel's pitch in a pattern of its own. NESdev describes it as similar to the Game Boy's wave channel "but with more sophisticated modulation". *Retro Gamer* calls it an "additional audio channel for FM synthesis", and the modulation does give it a family resemblance to FM. The musical shooting game *Otocky* used it to imitate different instruments.

*Retro Gamer*'s collector Corbie Dillard describes it as generally used for percussion-type effects in the background, and sometimes for extra sound effects. The Famicom's cartridge slot has audio pins, which is how the RAM adapter's sound reached the television; the NES's slot has no such input (see the [APU](/vault/hardware/apu) entry). When *Zelda* and *Metroid* were converted to NES cartridges, their music was altered to play on the console's own channels; Dillard counts hearing the disk soundtracks among the reasons to collect the format.

## The Disk Writer

The most unusual part of the system stood in the shops. Nintendo placed Disk Writer kiosks in retailers across Japan. *Retro Gamer* describes the arrangement: a blank Disk Card cost ¥2,000, and the kiosk would write a game onto it for another ¥500. A player could bring the same disk back and have a new game written over the old one. Nintendo's own page gives the same ¥500 fee, with some exceptions. *Edge* in 1998 put it in sterling: games for "as little as ¥500 (£2.50)".

A rewritten disk came without a box or a full manual. Miyamoto has explained that this is why *Zelda* ends its opening with "Please look up the manual for details": Nintendo made the booklet worth having, with hints and story, so that players who rewrote a disk still had something to show for their purchase (the *Zelda* entry has the full account).

From 1987, *Retro Gamer* reports, Nintendo also ran score contests through Disk Fax machines in shops. Specially made blue disks of games such as *Golf Japan Course* recorded a player's score, which the machine sent to Nintendo, and the best players won prizes.

Nintendo ended the disk rewriting service on 30 September 2003.

## The games

The Disk System's first year gave Nintendo several of its longest-running series.

- **[The Legend of Zelda](/vault/games/legend-of-zelda)**, 21 February 1986, the same day as the hardware. Players saved their progress to the disk.
- **[Metroid](/vault/games/metroid)**, 6 August 1986, from [Gunpei Yokoi](/vault/people/gunpei-yokoi)'s [R&D1](/vault/companies/nintendo-rd1). It also saved to disk.
- **Akumajō Dracula**, Konami's first *[Castlevania](/vault/games/castlevania)*, September 1986.
- **[Kid Icarus](/vault/games/kid-icarus)**, December 1986, also from R&D1. Its team kept working until three days before release, because the finished master could be copied onto disks so quickly.
- **Zelda II: The Adventure of Link**, January 1987.
- **Famicom Detective Club** (1988) and its sequel (1989), the text adventures that [Yoshio Sakamoto](/vault/people/yoshio-sakamoto) wrote for the format.

When these games came to the West, they came on cartridge. *Zelda* went into a gold cartridge with a battery to keep its save; *Metroid* and *Kid Icarus* gave the player passwords instead (see [password systems](/vault/techniques/password-systems) and [battery backup](/vault/techniques/battery-backup)).

## Why it faded

The Disk System's advantages lasted only a few years. *Retro Gamer* quotes Kermel: "Soon after the release of the Disk System, cartridges reached the 128k mark and started to include inbuilt batteries." Sean Corse makes the same point: the cost of cartridge ROM and of battery-backed saves fell until neither was the problem it had been in the early 1980s. With [bank switching](/vault/techniques/bank-switching), a 128 KB cartridge already held more than both sides of a disk. The cartridge version of *Zelda* in 1987 showed that a disk was no longer needed for either.

Piracy hurt it too. A disk that a shop could rewrite could also be copied at home. *Retro Gamer* describes bootleggers linking two drives, programs that copied disks on a single machine, and Nintendo revising the drive and adding copy protection, which was cracked within months.

Publishers had their own reasons to leave. *Retro Gamer* reports that Nintendo asked for half the copyright in each disk game published by an outside company, and that the ¥500 rewrites left little room for profit. *Edge* listed "consumer confusion regarding a dual-format console, piracy and contractual problems with licensees" among the reasons disks were phased out.

The hardware aged badly as well. The drive turns the disk with a rubber belt, and the belts perish; *Retro Gamer* notes that many surviving drives need a new belt before they will read a disk, and Corse describes a cryptic disk error screen that shows only a number.

*Retro Gamer* reports that by 1989 many publishers had abandoned the format, and that shops complained that the unused kiosks were taking up space. Nintendo kept the rewriting service running until 2003. *Edge*, writing in 1998 as Nintendo prepared another disk add-on, the Nintendo 64DD, described the Disk System as having "fell far short of being the success it was intended to be".
