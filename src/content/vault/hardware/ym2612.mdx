When Sega described the Mega Drive's sound chip to developers, it began with the chip's family: "The Yamaha 2612 Frequency Modulation (FM) sound synthesis IC resembles the Yamaha 2151 (used in SEGA's coin-operated machines) and the chips used in Yamaha's synthesizers." The YM2612 put six channels of four-operator FM synthesis in a home console, the same kind of sound as Sega's arcade boards, alongside the Texas Instruments PSG (programmable sound generator) the Master System already had.

The same manual is frank about the limits. Sampled sound takes over one of the six FM channels, and has to be timed by the processor byte by byte, "meaning that unless the software is very cleverly constructed, it is impossible to use any of the FM channels at the same time as the DAC". The two timers "do not cause interrupts". Stereo comes out of the headphone socket only. And one of the manual's own claims about the chip is wrong.

## Fast facts

- **Maker:** Yamaha. The chip belongs to Yamaha's OPN family and is also known as OPN2.
- **FM channels:** six, in two sets of three (channels 1-3 and 4-6).
- **Operators:** four per channel, connected in one of eight arrangements ("algorithms"); operator 1 can feed back into itself.
- **Channel 3:** can give each of its operators its own frequency.
- **DAC:** 8-bit samples, replacing FM channel 6 while enabled, timed entirely by software.
- **LFO:** one low-frequency oscillator for vibrato and tremolo, with a depth set per channel.
- **Timers:** two, which set flags but do not interrupt the processor on the Mega Drive.
- **Stereo:** each channel can go left, right or both; on the original Mega Drive only the headphone socket carries it.
- **Clock:** the 68000's clock, 7.67 MHz on an NTSC console and 7.60 MHz on PAL.
- **Access:** the Z80 reaches it at `4000h-4003h`, the 68000 at `$A04000-$A04003`.

## Four operators to a voice

Sega's manual explains FM through its operators. A channel has one overall frequency, and four operators turn it into the final waveform, "each possessing a frequency (a variant of the overall frequency), an envelope, and the capability to modulate its input using the frequency and envelope". An operator's frequency is the channel's multiplied by a whole number from 1 to 15, or by ½, and nudged by a small detune.

The algorithm decides which operators modulate which, and which ones are heard. Each operator has its own envelope, with an attack, two decay stages and a release, and a total level that sets its loudness in steps of about 0.75 dB. Sega's advice follows from that: to make a note softer, change the level of the operators that are heard, because "changing the other operators will affect the flavor of the note". The envelope of a modulating operator changes the tone over the length of a note, which is how one channel can sound like a plucked bass that starts bright and dulls as it fades. The [FM synthesis](/vault/techniques/fm-synthesis) entry explains the method.

## Talking to the chip

The YM2612 has two banks of registers. Part I holds the global registers and channels 1-3, Part II holds channels 4-6. A program writes a register number to the first address of a pair and the value to the second: `4000h` and `4001h` for Part I, `4002h` and `4003h` for Part II, as the Z80 sees them.

The chip needs time to take each value, and bit 7 of its status byte is set while it is busy. Sega's manual tells programmers to wait until it clears. In September 1991 Sega of America sent developers Technical Bulletin #11 about games whose sound stopped: they had read the status at `4001h`, where the YM2612 does not reliably report it, so it seemed never busy and writes were lost. The fix was to read status only from `4000h`.

This Z80 routine follows those rules for a Part I register:

```asm
; A = register number, C = value (global registers and channels 1-3)
fm_write1:
        push af
wait:   ld   a,(4000h)     ; status: bit 7 set while busy
        rla                ; bit 7 into carry
        jr   c,wait
        pop  af
        ld   (4000h),a     ; register number
        ld   a,c
        ld   (4001h),a     ; value
        ret
```

A few registers matter in every sound driver. Register `28h` keys notes on and off: the low three bits pick the channel (0-2 for channels 1-3, 4-6 for channels 4-6) and the top four switch its operators on, so writing `F0h` starts channel 1 and `00h` releases it. A channel's frequency is 14 bits long, a 3-bit octave ("block") and an 11-bit frequency number, split across two registers, and Sega's manual warns that multi-byte values must be written high byte first.

Sega was not always sure of the chip either. Its manual lists a per-operator register now known as SSG-EG as "a proprietary register whose usage is unknown. It should be set to zero." It controls an envelope mode left over from the SSG, the square-wave sound generator built into Yamaha's earlier OPN chips, which the YM2612 otherwise leaves out.

## Channel 3, not channel 6

The manual's overview says that "Channels 3 and 6, the last in each set, have the capability to [set a] fully separate frequency for each operator", and gives percussion as the reason, with a hedge: it "works well (we believe) for percussion instruments, which have harmonics at odd multiples such as 1.4 or 1.7 of the fundamental".

The manual's own register pages disagree. The mode bits in register `27h` offer "Channel 3 is the same as the others" or "Channel 3 has four separate frequencies", and the extra frequency registers, `A8h`-`AEh`, exist only in Part I. Only channel 3 has the special mode. Freed from whole-number ratios, its operators can play inharmonic sounds such as bells and metallic percussion, or four separate pitches at once, which Sega's author suggested in the key-on section: "Using a special channel 3, I believe it possible to have each operator be a separate note."

## The DAC and its cost

The DAC is not a seventh voice. Setting bit 7 of register `2Bh` turns FM channel 6 off and sends whatever byte was last written to register `2Ah` out in its place; of channel 6's own settings, only its left and right switches still apply. The chip does no timing of its own. To play a sample at a steady rate, the program must write each byte at exactly the right moment, and that is the work the manual calls "unfortunately" impossible to combine with FM "unless the software is very cleverly constructed".

Sega's technical overview gives the Z80 the job of controlling the sound chips, which makes it the natural place for sample playback: a driver there can feed the DAC in a tight loop and fit FM register writes between samples, leaving the 68000 to run the game. Every sampled drum over FM music was paid for twice, once with channel 6 and once with the Z80's time.

## Timers that only set a flag

The two timers were meant for software timing. Timer A is 10 bits long and Timer B 8 bits, and Sega gives their periods as 18 × (1024 − A) microseconds and 288 × (256 − B) microseconds, up to about 18.4 ms and 73.7 ms. Those figures assume an 8 MHz clock; on an NTSC Mega Drive the chip runs at 7.67 MHz, so the real periods are about 4 per cent longer. Register `27h` loads, enables and resets them.

When a timer runs out it sets a bit in the status byte, and that is all. "Unfortunately," says the manual, "these two timers do not cause interrupts - they must be read by the software to determine if they have finished counting." The manual offers the timers as an alternative to the Z80's vertical-blank interrupt, so a driver that takes its tempo from them has to keep checking the status byte between its other work.

## The ladder effect and the YM3438

The YM2612 converts its own output to an analogue signal, which many of Yamaha's FM chips left to a separate converter. It does so with a 9-bit converter shared between the channels in turn. Its quiet output is distorted: low-level signals are quantised much more coarsely than they should be. The effect is known as the "ladder effect", and it can be heard in fades and quiet notes.

Yamaha also made the chip in CMOS as the YM3438 (OPN2C), with a cleaner converter. Sega used it in arcade boards such as System C, and in later Mega Drive models the YM3438 design moved inside a single custom chip that combined it with the video processor. Those consoles play the same music with less of the distortion, so the same game can sound different on an early and a late Mega Drive.
