A trackball is a ball sitting in a socket, resting on rollers, which the player spins with the palm of the hand. It reports movement in two directions at once and at any speed: nudge it and the cursor creeps, spin it and the cursor flies. When Atari put one on the arcade game *Football* in October 1978, *Video Games* later wrote, players "experienced the most physically involving video game ever created". Games such as *[Missile Command](/vault/games/missile-command)*, *[Centipede](/vault/games/centipede)* and *[Marble Madness](/vault/games/marble-madness)* were then designed around it, and home trackballs followed so that the conversions could be played the same way.

## Fast facts

- **Mechanism:** a ball on two rollers, one for each axis, each turning a slotted disc read by light sensors.
- **Output:** movement in two directions, not position.
- **In the arcade:** Atari's *Football* (1978) and *Soccer*, then *Missile Command* (1980), *Centipede* (1981), *Crystal Castles* (1983) and *Marble Madness* (1984).
- **At home:** Atari's Trak-Ball (CX22 for the VCS, CX80 for the computers, CX53 for the 5200), WICO's Command Control trackball, Coleco's Roller Controller and Commodore's CD 1200 for the CDTV.
- **Spelling:** *Electronic Games* noted in 1983 that "no two manufacturers seem to spell the word 'tracball' the same way". Atari wrote Trak-Ball.

## How it works

Atari's service manual for its 5200 trackball takes the mechanism apart. The case holds "the roller shaft assemblies, an idler shaft assembly and a cue ball". "When the cue ball is spun, the two roller shafts rotate. Each shaft has a slotted encoding wheel at one end which interrupts the two light sources in the optocoupler for that channel." Each wheel produces two square waves "approximately 90° out of phase", and the circuit tells the direction from "which clock leads the other". The pulse rate gives the speed. It is the same principle as a [spinner](/vault/hardware/spinner), used twice, one encoder for each axis.

What happens next depends on the machine it is plugged into. An arcade board can count the pulses directly. A home console has to accept them through whatever port it already has, and that took some ingenuity:

- The 5200's joystick ports expect potentiometers, so the CX53 is, in Atari's words, "a 2-channel D to A converter which translates optically coupled clocks into analog currents", fed into "the two paddle line inputs used for X-Y control". Spinning the ball speeds up or slows down the charging ramp the console measures.
- The VCS and home-computer joystick ports only understand switches. WICO's trackball carried "its own custom microprocessor built in. So it can talk to your system in a language all its own", and its advert promised that spinning the ball "2 revolutions per second" gave "that exact same speed on the screen".

## Arcade trackball games

*Missile Command*'s designers never considered anything else. "My recollection is that it was going to be a trackball from the start and it never deviated from that," programmer Rich Adam told *The Atari Book*. "From the moment we implemented it, it was pretty obvious that the trackball was optimum." Dave Theurer added that Atari was already using trackballs "in a lot of our other games at the time such as Soccer and Football".

*Centipede* used a smaller one. In March 1982 *Electronic Games*, describing how an arcade game was made, mentioned "the mini-trackball recently introduced on Centipede". *Crystal Castles*, *Video Games* explained in 1983, "features a trackball with a jump button on either side" to move Bentley Bear around its maze.

*Marble Madness* made the match literal: the player rolls a ball to roll a marble. That did not win everyone over at first. Looking back in 1995, *Edge* said the game "initially had a mixed reaction, primarily because of its unusual controls — like other Atari titles, Marble Madness used a trackball".

## Home trackballs

Home trackballs appeared in 1983, sold on the promise of arcade feel. A mail-order listing in *Video Games* in February 1983 priced the WICO Trackball Controller at $69.95. WICO's advertising that spring called it "The Authentic Arcade Trackball" and named the arcade games it was meant for: *Missile Command*, *Centipede* and *Space Invaders*. It worked "with Atari, Commodore VIC-20 and 8 other popular video game and home computer systems".

Atari's own came in three versions. The 5200 trackball, announced for early 1983, was in *Electronic Games*' words "a nifty looking piece of work that actually fits over the face of the system", with buttons on either side of the ball. The CX22 Trak-Ball was the VCS version, and *ANALOG Computing* reported the CX80 in July 1983 as "a TRACKBALL controller replicating those in the arcades" for the Atari computers. Coleco's Roller Controller for the [ColecoVision](/vault/systems/colecovision) was to be packaged with a version of the arcade game *Slither*, and TG Products sold the TB-600 for the Apple II. By June 1983 *Electronic Games* reckoned that trackballs had "become so ubiquitous, in fact, that even the Apple's getting at least one."

The idea outlived the joystick port. Commodore's CD 1200 of 1991 was a trackball for the CDTV that doubled as the remote control: "Use the ball in the center of the controller as you would normally use the arrow keys on the CDTV remote controller." It ran on two C batteries, or on power from the player when plugged in with its cable.
