A game cartridge holds ROM chips wired straight to the console's address and data lines. The processor reads its program from them exactly as it would from memory inside the machine, so nothing has to load: switch on, and the game is already there.

The less obvious consequence mattered more. Because the cartridge sits on the processor's bus, it can hold more than memory. A publisher could put almost anything the console's designers had left out into the slot: logic to switch banks of ROM, RAM with a battery to keep saved games, extra sound channels, even a second processor faster than the console's own. A cartridge console could be upgraded one game at a time.

## Fast facts

- **First:** the Fairchild Channel F, shown in June 1976, which *ANALOG Computing* called "the first programmable video game system".
- **What is inside:** program ROM; on many consoles graphics ROM or RAM; and sometimes a mapper, save RAM with a battery, or a co-processor.
- **Growing past the address space:** [bank switching](/vault/techniques/bank-switching), from the [Atari 2600](/vault/systems/atari-2600)'s 8K games to the NES's [MMC1](/vault/hardware/mmc1) and [MMC3](/vault/hardware/mmc3).
- **Co-processors:** the [Super FX](/vault/techniques/super-fx-chip) and SA-1 on the Super NES, and Sega's SVP for *Virtua Racing* on the Mega Drive.
- **Drawbacks:** mask ROM is expensive and cannot be changed after manufacture.

## The Channel F's Videocarts

Fairchild's console, shown at the Consumer Electronics Show in Chicago on 15 June 1976, took its games on cartridges it called Videocarts. They were deliberately shaped like eight-track music tapes, and the manual told owners to insert them in "much the same way an eight-track audio cartridge is into a cartridge tape player". *ANALOG Computing* summed up the change in 1988: Fairchild "introduced the first programmable video game system, the Channel F, in 1976", and "transferred the game programs from the console to interchangeable game cartridges". Until then a home video game console played the games built into it and nothing else.

The idea came out of the processor. Jerry Lawson's team built the console round Fairchild's own F8, which came as a pair of chips: a 3850 processor and a 3851 program storage unit holding 1K of ROM. Retro Gamer's *Videogames Hardware Handbook* describes a Videocart as containing "almost no circuitry – just two of the companion ROM chips". Richard Olney, who worked on the F8's design, wrote that its two-chip design, with the program fixed into the 3851 when the chip was made, "made the F8 a good choice for a cartridge based game system". The two 3851s inside the console held the built-in games and "also provide subroutines that are used by the cartridge based programs". According to the Handbook, removable game memory "hadn't been done before" because of the risk of sparks and static, so the US Federal Communications Commission made Fairchild submit every new cartridge for testing, and the console, the cartridge bay and each cartridge had to be shielded in metal.

## More game than the address space

Every cartridge machine hit the same limit sooner or later: games outgrew the part of the address space the processor could give them. The Atari 2600's 6507 processor can see only 4K of cartridge ROM at a time. *[Electronic Games](/vault/magazines/electronic-games)* explained to a reader in November 1982 that from *Asteroids* and *Missile Command* on, the console "is capable of up to 8K, using a special bank switch that flip-flops two 4K programs". The cartridge holds more ROM than the processor can see, and logic on the cartridge decides which part is visible.

The NES made that logic a product line. The console gives a cartridge the processor's upper 32K, `$8000-$FFFF`, for program ROM, and its picture chip, the [PPU](/vault/hardware/ppu), reads its tile patterns straight from the cartridge too, so the graphics never pass through the processor. A game that fits in 32K of program and 8K of graphics, as *[Super Mario Bros.](/vault/games/super-mario-bros)* does, needs nothing else. Bigger games carry a mapper chip. Nintendo's MMC1 of 1987 switched up to 256 KB of program in 16 KB banks and let a game choose its own screen mirroring; the MMC3 of 1988 switched 8 KB banks, reached 512 KB, and added an interrupt part-way down the screen, which *Nintendo Power* credited for *[Super Mario Bros. 3](/vault/games/super-mario-bros-3)*'s fixed status bar. [Konami](/vault/companies/konami) designed its own mappers, among them the [VRC2](/vault/hardware/vrc2), which switched graphics in eight 1 KB pieces.

The Japanese Famicom's cartridge slot also has an audio input, which the Western NES dropped, so Famicom cartridges could add sound hardware. Konami's VRC6 gave *Akumajō Densetsu* two extra pulse channels and a sawtooth wave; its Western release, *Castlevania III*, had to make do with the console's own sound.

## A second processor in the box

The Super NES took the idea furthest. Nintendo's development manuals document three chips that could ride in the cartridge: the DSP-1 maths processor, the SA-1 and the Super FX. The Super FX, designed with [Argonaut](/vault/companies/argonaut), was a graphics processor that ran, in Nintendo's words, "six times as fast as the Super NES CPU", and it drew the polygons of *[Star Fox](/vault/games/star-fox)* (*Starwing* in Europe) in 1993. Argonaut told *Edge* in 1994 that the faster Super FX 2 was rated at 21.477 MHz. The SA-1 was a 65C816 like the console's own processor, running at 10.74 MHz, "four times faster than the normal operating speed of the Super NES CPU", alongside it; *Edge* reported that *Super Mario RPG* used one to unpack the compressed graphics that filled its 32-megabit cartridge.

Sega answered with the SVP chip in the Mega Drive version of *Virtua Racing*. [Yu Suzuki](/vault/people/yu-suzuki), whose team made the arcade original, told *Edge* in 1994 that the conversion was attempted "mainly because the SVP chip meant it wasn't an over-complicated conversion. Without it, I don't think I would have even attempted it." The approach had a cost. Argonaut pointed out that the Super FX ran its program from the game's own ROM, so one chip design served every game, while the SVP appeared to carry its own program in masked ROM, so that "every new game needs a new SVP chip".

## Saving the game

A cartridge could also remember. RAM on the cartridge, kept alive by a lithium cell, held saved games. Nintendo's MMC1 specification of 1987 named a CR2032 for the job, and the cartridge release of *[The Legend of Zelda](/vault/games/legend-of-zelda)* kept three saved games that way. *Nintendo Power* explained the point to its readers in January 1991: before battery back-up, a player who wanted to finish a game "had to do it during one play session". It reckoned a battery would keep the data "about five years". Owners of games on Nintendo's newest mapper, the MMC5, were promised that they "won't have to push RESET on your Control Deck while pushing POWER when you want to quit".

## What cartridges cost

A cartridge's program is fixed when its mask ROM is made, in a factory and in quantity, and a bigger ROM costs more on every copy sold. *Edge* described Nintendo's [Famicom Disk System](/vault/hardware/famicom-disk-system) of 1986 as "an attempt to counter rising ROM costs", with disk games at roughly half the price of cartridges. Within a few years cheaper ROM and battery saves had brought publishers back to cartridges.

The price never went away. When the [Game Boy Advance](/vault/systems/game-boy-advance) arrived in 2001, *Edge* found some publishers refusing to develop for it "owing to the low margins offered by Nintendo's cartridge pricing scheme". Martin Merren of Crawfish Interactive told the magazine what that meant for the game itself: "we might get squeezed on the amount of cartridge space that we can use, and the possibility of battery back-up." The [Nintendo DS](/vault/systems/nintendo-ds) of 2004 kept a Game Boy Advance slot, but *Edge* reported that its own software would come on neither cartridges nor discs, "but a new 'thin memory card' format".

## The slot as a gate

Because a cartridge is hardware, the company that controls its manufacture controls the platform. The NES carried a lockout chip, the 10NES: a matching chip in every licensed cartridge had to answer it, or the console would not run the game. *Compute!'s Gazette* put the consequence plainly in 1990: "Since Nintendo is the only producer of this chip, it's able to control the quality of the games produced for the NES." The Famicom had no such chip. Nintendo also built the cartridges, and its licensing rules are described in the [Nintendo Seal of Quality](/vault/phenomena/nintendo-seal) entry.

The paperwork reached inside the ROM. Nintendo's *Game Cassette Registration Specification* of 18 February 1987, an unpublished document for licensees, required every cartridge to carry a registration area near the top of memory, from `$FFF0`, holding a maker code, a registration number, the cassette type and memory size, checksums over the program and the graphics data, and the game's registered title. The Super NES's SA-1 carried the control into the co-processor: Nintendo's manual notes that SA-1 cartridges use a different ROM from standard game paks, which "guards against unlicensed products".

## Home computers

Home computers used the same idea for different reasons. On the [Commodore 64](/vault/systems/commodore-64), a cartridge at `$8000` starts itself if it carries the five bytes `$C3 $C2 $CD $38 $30` (the letters CBM, inverted, and the digits 80) at `$8004`: at power-on the machine checks for them and jumps to the address the cartridge gives. The same expansion port took hardware that was not a game at all, such as the [Action Replay](/vault/hardware/action-replay) freezer cartridge, which worked because the port is wired to the processor's bus.
