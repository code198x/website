The Game Genie let a Nintendo owner type in a six-letter word and start a game with infinite lives. It did it without touching the game's memory or stopping the processor. It sat between the console and the cartridge and, for up to three addresses, gave the console a different byte from the one the cartridge held. Code Masters, the British budget-software house, designed it. The American toy company Lewis Galoob sold it, and Nintendo went to court to stop Galoob selling it. Nintendo lost, and in 1992 it was ordered to pay Galoob $15 million.

## Fast facts

- **Designed by:** [Code Masters](/vault/companies/codemasters): Ted Carron with [Richard and David Darling](/vault/people/darling-brothers)
- **Sold by:** Camerica in Canada, [Galoob](/vault/companies/galoob) in the United States, Hornby Hobbies in Britain
- **First sold:** 1990 in Canada, where 100,000 were sold that year
- **Versions:** [NES](/vault/systems/nintendo-entertainment-system), [Mega Drive](/vault/systems/sega-mega-drive), [Game Boy](/vault/systems/game-boy) and [Super NES](/vault/systems/super-nintendo)
- **Court case:** *Lewis Galoob Toys v. Nintendo of America*, decided for Galoob in July 1991 and upheld on appeal in 1992

## From Power Pak to Genie

Code Masters had been thinking about cheats for its own cartridges. "We were thinking of adding switches to the actual cartridges so you could select the amount of lives available and things like that," Richard Darling told *Edge* in 1996. "We thought it would be novel if you could do it for a game but better if we could come up with some sort of attachment that worked on any title." The engineer Ted Carron built it. *Edge*'s 2004 history of *[Micro Machines](/vault/games/micro-machines)* calls the prototype Carron's "Power Pak". David Darling renamed it the Game Genie because players could enter three codes, or "wishes", at a time. The American patent for the device, filed in May 1990, names Richard Darling, Edward A. Carron and David Darling as its inventors.

Code Masters showed the device at the Consumer Electronics Show in Chicago, where a talent scout from Galoob "loved it", Richard Darling said. Galoob licensed it for the United States. In Canada, Camerica made and sold it. *Game Informer* called Camerica the Genie's "Canadian owner and manufacturer", and a Camerica feature in 1992 described it as "the company that brought you Game Genie". The design was Code Masters' own: its British advertising said "INVENTED BY CODEMASTERS". Camerica was a separate, Canadian company that also published Code Masters' unlicensed NES games in North America.

## How it worked

Plug the Genie into the NES, plug a game into the Genie and switch on. Instead of the game, the Genie's own code-entry screen appears. You type in up to three codes from the codebook and press Start, and the game begins with the changes in place. *Mean Machines*, reviewing it in 1992, found codes for infinite lives, time and energy, codes to start on later levels, and oddities such as "super bounce on the cars in Micro Machines" or stopping the vitamin capsules falling in *Dr. Mario*.

Behind that screen the Genie is doing something simple. The NES reads its program from the cartridge ROM at CPU addresses $8000 to $FFFF. The Genie passes those reads through, except at the addresses it has been told to watch. The NESdev Wiki, the community reference for NES hardware, puts it this way: "It can intercept any three addresses in CPU $8000…$FFFF and respond with a single replacement for each." If a game loads its starting lives with an instruction whose operand is 3 and the Genie answers 9 whenever that byte is read, the game starts with nine lives. The program has not been changed. The console has simply been given a different byte.

The start-up is a two-stage trick. At power-on the Genie presents its own 4 KB ROM to the console, which runs the code-entry screen. Pressing Start writes the codes into the Genie's registers and then switches it into game mode, where it passes the real cartridge through. It stays in that mode until the console is switched off.

### The codes

A Game Genie code is written in a 16-letter alphabet (A P Z L G I T Y E O X U K S V N), and each letter stands for four bits. Six letters give 24 bits. That is enough for a 15-bit address inside the $8000 to $FFFF window and the 8-bit replacement value.

Eight-letter codes add an 8-bit compare value, and the Genie replaces the byte only if the cartridge's own byte matches it. The reason is bank switching. Many NES cartridges swap different banks of ROM into the same addresses, so an unconditional code could fire in the wrong bank and corrupt something unrelated. The compare value makes that much less likely. The patent describes the same safeguard: the data is changed "only if the data from the external data storage medium matches data set by user-operable input means".

Owners could not search for codes themselves on the NES version. As *Mean Machines* put it, "Since you can't actually hack into the games yourselves, you're forced to rely on further code sheets." New codes came from the publishers. In Britain, Hornby and Code Masters ran a telephone helpline for them, and magazines printed codes sent in by readers.

## Nintendo v. Galoob

Nintendo's case was that the Genie infringed its copyrights. Brian Moore of Serif, Nintendo's UK distributor, told *ACE* in 1990 that "the designers of games spend a long time getting everything right", and said the Genie infringed their copyright. *Game Informer* reported Nintendo's argument that changing a game's rules would make it boring, so that fewer games would be sold. Galoob and Camerica replied that the Genie would "breathe new life into old games".

Nintendo sued in the United States and in Canada, where it tried to get an injunction against Camerica. In the United States it won a preliminary injunction that stopped Galoob selling the Genie until the trial in the spring of 1991. In Canada it did not, and Camerica went on selling. In July 1991 US District Judge Fern Smith ruled for Galoob. The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals upheld her decision in May 1992. It held that the Genie's changes were not fixed in any permanent form, so they did not create a derivative work. It also held that players using the Genie at home were making fair use of games they had bought.

Nintendo had posted a bond when it obtained the injunction. In 1992 Judge Smith ordered Nintendo to pay Galoob $15 million for the sales it had lost while the Genie was kept off the market for a year, as *Game Informer* reported that September. *Edge* said in 1996 that the money was paid from the bond, and that about $3.5 million of it went to Code Masters as the Genie's creator. "I am overjoyed. We were not intimidated by Nintendo's aggressive legal behaviour," David Darling said at the time. The Game Genie, he added, "proves that innovation cannot be stifled".

## Sales and later versions

The injunction did not stop the Genie selling well once it was lifted. In January 1992 *Mean Machines* said it had sold more than a million units, and *Edge* later put its American sales at some 1.4 million. Richard Darling remembered it as "America's fifth-best-selling toy that year".

Sega took the opposite view to Nintendo. It gave the Mega Drive Genie its Seal of Quality, *Mean Machines* reported in February 1992, and the NES and Mega Drive versions reached British shops in July 1992, at £39.99 and £44.99. A Game Boy Genie, which Code Masters showed as a prototype at the Consumer Electronics Show in 1992, clipped onto the handheld and had a button to switch the codes on and off during play. An American Super NES version was on sale by the start of 1993. For Britain, Galoob and Hornby restyled its case to suit the European console, and set a price of £44.99 for a June 1993 release.
