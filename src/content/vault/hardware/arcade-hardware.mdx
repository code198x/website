Arcade machines ran on specialised hardware designed for specific games or game types. Early games used discrete logic circuits; later systems evolved into programmable boards. Manufacturers like Sega, Namco, and Capcom developed their own architectures. Arcade hardware consistently outperformed home systems, justifying the coin-op business model.

## Fast facts

- **Evolution:** discrete logic → CPU-based → standardised platforms.
- **Advantage:** purpose-built, no cost constraint of home pricing.
- **Manufacturers:** [Sega](/vault/companies/sega), [Namco](/vault/companies/namco), [Capcom](/vault/companies/capcom), [Konami](/vault/companies/konami), [Taito](/vault/companies/taito), others.
- **Lifespan:** reusable boards came later. Through the mid-1980s the hardware was commonly redesigned per game.
- **Modern:** arcade-specific hardware largely replaced by PC-based systems.

## Notable platforms

Key arcade hardware:
- **Namco System 1/2:** *Pac-Land*, *Final Lap*.
- **Sega System 16:** *OutRun*, *Golden Axe*.
- **Capcom CPS:** *Street Fighter II*.
- **Sega Model 2:** *Virtua Fighter 2*, *Daytona USA*.
- **NAOMI:** Sega's Dreamcast-based board.

## "Arcade hardware" was never one thing

The generational table below is a useful shape, but it hides how far machines of
the same year could differ. Fabien Sanglard sets two 1987 titles side by side.

Sega's X Board, running *Afterburner*: "dual 12.5 MHz Motorola 68000 CPUs", a
Super Scaler graphics chipset "clocked at 50MHz... capable of both scaling and
rotating up to 256 sprites, over two background layers, and one 'road' layer",
and a SegaPCM 16-channel stereo PCM chip for sound.

Capcom's board for *1943*, the same year: "a 6Mhz z80 CPU with a graphics system
capable of animating 32 sprites on top of one text layer and two background
layers", with FM synthesis from a second Z80.

**256 sprites against 32.** Both are "1987 arcade hardware".

## The boards were rebuilt for nearly every game

This entry lists long board lifespans among arcade hardware's characteristics.
That is the standardised era talking. Before it, the pattern was the opposite —
Capcom's PCBs from 1984 to 1988 show "a high variation of components", and "even
titles produced the same year wouldn't necessarily feature the same chips". The
Z80 appears as game CPU, as sound CPU, and twice over in the *1942* board.

> Even though games did mostly the same thing (move sprites over backgrounds),
> the hardware had to be re-invented over and over again.

Reusable platforms like CP-System and System 16 are the answer to that cost, not
a description of what preceded them.

## Hardware generations

Arcade technology progression:
- **1970s:** discrete logic, then the first CPUs.
- **1980s:** custom sprite hardware, multiple CPUs.
- **1990s:** 3D polygon hardware.
- **2000s+:** PC-based with standard components.
