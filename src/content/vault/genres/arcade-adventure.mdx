An arcade adventure is an adventure played with a joystick. The player explores a connected map, collects and applies objects, and solves environmental puzzles — but movement, jumping and combat are handled in real time with arcade controls, not typed commands. It sits between the text adventure and the pure action game, and for most of the 1980s British computer press it was one of the most useful words available.

It is also the clearest case of a genre modern taxonomies have lost. There is no current label that means this. "Action-adventure" is the usual substitute and it is too broad: it covers games with nothing of the room-by-room, object-puzzle structure that defined the form. Flattening arcade adventure into action-adventure loses the category instead of translating it.

The archive shows how routine the term was: searching this library for *arcade adventure* and *arcade adventures* returns **5,187 pages across 2,416 issues**, of which 929 pages are in the Spectrum press alone.

## Fast facts

- **Core loop:** explore, collect, apply object, unlock more map.
- **Control:** real-time and arcade-style, never a parser.
- **Structure:** discrete interconnected rooms or screens.
- **Defining titles:** *Atic Atac*, *Sabre Wulf*, *Knight Lore*, *Head Over Heels*.
- **Heartland:** British 8-bit machines, above all the ZX Spectrum.

## Genre conventions

- **Room-per-screen:** the map is a graph of single screens, not a scrolling world.
- **Inventory limits:** carrying capacity as a puzzle constraint, not realism.
- **Keyed progression:** an object found here opens something there.
- **Environmental hazards:** patrolling enemies as moving obstacles, not opponents to defeat.
- **No save:** a single sitting, with the map held in the player's head or on paper.

## Presentation, not genre

Many of the best-known examples use an isometric view — *Knight Lore*, *Head Over Heels*, *Alien 8* — to the point that "isometric" is sometimes treated as the genre itself. It is not. Isometric projection is a rendering technique that also appears in shoot 'em ups, strategy games and puzzlers; the arcade adventure is defined by its structure, not its viewpoint. Ultimate Play the Game's Filmation engine made the pairing famous.

## Two schools

The form had at least two distinct traditions, and collapsing them into one
misses what the category could hold.

[Ultimate](/vault/companies/ultimate) built the fast, wordless version:
*Atic Atac*, *Sabre Wulf*, then *Knight Lore* and the Filmation engine. The
puzzles are spatial, the presentation is the argument, and the games are
famously unforthcoming — *CRASH* reviewed *Sabre Wulf* and returned a verdict
of **"Unrateable"** rather than a percentage.

[Gargoyle Games](/vault/companies/gargoyle-games) built the slow, literate
version out of Birmingham: *Tir Na Nog*, [*Dun Darach*](/vault/games/dun-darach),
*Marsport*, [*Heavy on the Magick*](/vault/games/heavy-on-the-magick). Same
room-and-object structure, but with rotoscoped animation, mythological settings
and demons who bargain rather than attack.

The reviews did not separate them. *Knight Lore* took 94% from *CRASH* and 99%
from *Sinclair Programs*; [*Head Over Heels*](/vault/games/head-over-heels) took
97%; *Heavy on the Magick* took 9/10 from *CRASH* and 5/5 from both *Sinclair
User* and *Your Computer*.

The charts did separate them, sharply. Gargoyle's games lived in the Adventure
chart — *Heavy on the Magick* held **#1** there three times while sitting at
**#16** in the general one. Ultimate's sold to everybody. Two schools of the
same genre, reviewed alike and bought differently, which is a reminder that a
chart position measures reach rather than quality.

## Evolution

- **1983:** *Atic Atac* and *Sabre Wulf* establish the flip-screen exploration form.
- **1984:** *Knight Lore* adds isometric projection and object physics.
- **1986-87:** the form peaks in ambition with *Head Over Heels*.
- **1990s:** absorbed into console action-adventure, and the British term falls out of use.
