In most shooting games the player steers. In a rail shooter the game does the steering. The camera travels along a route the designers laid down in advance, like a fairground ride on its track, and the player's whole job is to aim and fire at whatever the route brings into view. Giving up that freedom bought something in return. Because the designers knew exactly where the player would be at every moment, they could stage each attack like a scene in a film: a gunman leaping from behind a door, a battle cruiser sliding overhead, a dragon banking away from the camera.

The label came later than the games. Magazines of the 1990s described games whose ship "flies on rails", and by the 2000s "on-rail shooter" had become a genre name. It covers two families that grew up side by side: flying games that race into the screen, and light-gun games that walk the player through a building or a battlefield.

## Fast facts

- **Defining trait:** the game moves the player along a fixed route; the player aims and fires.
- **Early example:** Sega's *Buck Rogers: Planet of Zoom* (1982).
- **Into the screen:** *[Space Harrier](/vault/games/space-harrier)* (1985), *[After Burner](/vault/games/after-burner)* (1987), *Starblade* (1991), *[Star Fox](/vault/games/star-fox)* (1993), *[Panzer Dragoon](/vault/games/panzer-dragoon)* (1995), *[Rez](/vault/games/rez)* (2001).
- **With a gun:** *Operation Wolf* (1987), *[Virtua Cop](/vault/games/virtua-cop)* (1994), *[Time Crisis](/vault/games/time-crisis)* (1995), *[The House of the Dead](/vault/games/house-of-the-dead)* (1997).
- **Later homes:** the Wii's pointer in the late 2000s; virtual reality with *Rez Infinite* (2016).

## Flying into the screen

*Retro Gamer*'s history of the shoot 'em up traces the "on-rail shooter" back to Sega's *Buck Rogers: Planet of Zoom*, and describes the type as pseudo-3D or 3D games in which a lone fighter flies "into the screen" and mows down "wave after wave of enemies". *Planet of Zoom* reached American arcades late in 1982 and scaled its sprites to make objects rush towards the player. *Electronic Games* called it "the most visually gripping new coin-op this season" in April 1983, and singled out its "trench" scene, in which the player threads a ship through a narrow valley dodging obstacles.

Sega took the idea much further with [Yu Suzuki](/vault/people/yu-suzuki)'s *[Space Harrier](/vault/games/space-harrier)* in 1985, which sent a jet-propelled hero racing over a chequered plain at monsters flying out of the screen, drawn by [sprite scaling](/vault/techniques/sprite-scaling) hardware. Suzuki's *[After Burner](/vault/games/after-burner)* (1987) put the player behind an F-14 flying into the screen, in deluxe cabinets that rolled with the plane.

Polygons came next. Namco's *Starblade*, which *Edge* called the coin-op "taking pride of place in every arcade" in late 1991, drew its enemies in real time over backgrounds stored on a LaserDisc. "Your ship flies on rails throughout the game," *Edge* explained; the player controls only the gunsight. *Edge* also reported that Nintendo's team on *[Star Fox](/vault/games/star-fox)* had apparently taken inspiration from it. *Star Fox* limited the player's movement "to more or less straight ahead", as Argonaut's Jez San put it, to ease newcomers into 3D.

When *Edge* previewed Sega's *[Panzer Dragoon](/vault/games/panzer-dragoon)* for the Saturn in 1995, it spelt out the bargain these games made. It looked like free flight, but "instead of having complete control of the dragon, you fly through the level environments 'on rails'. In effect, you play the dragon's 'gunner'." The magazine compared it with *Space Harrier*: both were "simple 3D into-the-screen shoot 'em ups". Six years later *[Rez](/vault/games/rez)* used the same structure for a game in which every lock-on added a sound to the music.

## Light guns on rails

The other family began with the [light gun](/vault/hardware/light-gun). Early gun games such as Nintendo's *[Duck Hunt](/vault/games/duck-hunt)* were shooting galleries: the targets moved and the player stood still. Taito's *Operation Wolf* (1987) sent the player on a journey instead. It reached British arcades at the end of 1987, and *Sinclair User*'s coin-op column in January 1988 described the machine gun "perched on the outside of the cabinet", with a built-in launcher for grenades, and six scenes that took the player through jungle, villages and a concentration camp to an airport, with hostages and civilians who must not be shot. The reviewer thought it "extremely fast and addictive", and also "very violent and tasteless".

Polygons changed this family too. Sega AM2's *[Virtua Cop](/vault/games/virtua-cop)* (1994) moved the camera through 3D warehouses and offices, with gangsters who reacted to where they were hit. Namco answered with *[Time Crisis](/vault/games/time-crisis)* (1995), whose foot pedal let the player duck behind cover while a clock pushed them back out. Sega's *[The House of the Dead](/vault/games/house-of-the-dead)*, which *Computer and Video Games* called "a mix of Resident Evil meets Virtua Cop" when it appeared at Japan's AOU show in early 1997, swapped gangsters for zombies and scientists to rescue. Its routes branched, and unlike *Virtua Cop 2*'s fixed junctions they changed with the player's progress, so experts could reach routes that beginners never saw.

## After the CRT

Light guns needed a cathode-ray tube television, and flat screens stopped them working (the [light gun](/vault/hardware/light-gun) entry explains why). The Wii gave the genre a second life. Its remote needs no picture to time: *Edge* explained in 2006 that the "misleadingly-named sensor bar" on the television is a row of infrared emitters, seen by a sensor in the end of the remote when it points at them. Nintendo's Wii Zapper was, as *Retro Gamer* put it in 2008, "not actually a lightgun" but a plastic cradle for the Wii Remote and Nunchuk, and it had "led to a renaissance of lightgun shooters on the Wii", including the first home version of Sega's *Ghost Squad* and a two-game *House of the Dead 2 & 3* collection. Sega told the magazine that it intended all its future light-gun releases to be on the Wii. Capcom's *Resident Evil* spin-offs and Visceral Games' *Dead Space Extraction* followed; *Edge* called the latter "the Citizen Kane of on-rail gun games" in 2009.

Virtual reality gave the flying branch a new home. *Rez* returned in 2016 as *Rez Infinite*, played inside a VR headset.
