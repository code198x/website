In a role-playing game of the early 1980s, a fight was a menu. The player chose Attack, the program rolled the dice, and the monster took its turn. The action RPG kept the character sheet, the experience points and the ever-better sword, but put the fight under the player's thumb: move, strike, back away, and live or die by timing. It grew up in two places at once, on Japanese home computers and in American dungeon games. Its descendants stretch from *Zelda*-style adventures to *Diablo*'s loot and the punishing *Souls* games.

## Fast facts

- **The idea:** role-playing progression (experience, levels, equipment) with combat in real time.
- **Beginnings:** Japanese computer games of 1984, among them Falcom's *Dragon Slayer* and T&E Soft's *Hydlide*; American dungeon games of the same years, such as Epyx's *Gateway to Apshai*, are also named as forerunners.
- **Landmarks:** *Ys* (1987), *Zelda II: The Adventure of Link* (1987), *Secret of Mana* (1993), *[Diablo](/vault/games/diablo)* (turn of 1997), *[Dark Souls](/vault/games/dark-souls)* (2011).
- **Branches:** *Zelda*-style adventure, *Diablo*-style loot games, the *Souls*-like, the looter-shooter.

## Where it began

No single game can fairly be called the first. Writers on the period name several from 1984. Falcom released *Dragon Slayer* for NEC's PC-8801 that year, billing it as a "New Type Real-time Role-Playing Game"; Falcom itself now calls it a representative work of the action RPG's early period. T&E Soft's *Hydlide*, designed by Tokihiro Naito, came out at the end of the same year. Naito has said that "at the time, I was in love with The Black Onyx and Namco's The Tower of Druaga": one a Japanese role-playing game, the other an arcade maze game. According to John Szczepaniak, a smaller game, Cosmos Computer's *Courageous Perseus*, came out before *Hydlide* and possibly before *Dragon Slayer*.

*Hydlide* was converted to other Japanese computers, among them the [MSX](/vault/systems/msx). Reviewing the MSX2 version in December 1986, the French *MSX Magazine* noted that, according to its manual, the game "combine un jeu d'arcade, un jeu de rôle et un jeu d'aventure": an arcade game, a role-playing game and an adventure in one.

American players had met the mixture already. [Epyx](/vault/companies/epyx)'s *Gateway to Apshai*, a cartridge sequel to its *Temple of Apshai* that was new in the shops at the start of 1984, put a lone adventurer with "only a dagger, a suit of leather armor and a prayer" into 128 dungeons, controlled with a joystick and three buttons. *ANALOG Computing*'s reviewer opened in February 1984 with the effect it had on him: "I'm mad! Very, very mad! I just lost my last man to a Mamba Snake on level six of Gateway To Apshai". "Game play is fast and furious," he wrote, and he recommended it to anyone "into D&D games".

## Ys, Zelda and the consoles

Falcom followed with *Ys* (1987), whose red-haired hero, Adol Christin, searches for the six books of Ys. Falcom sold it on the promise that role-playing games were entering "an age of kindness": it was meant to be a game anyone could enjoy. Nintendo's *[The Legend of Zelda](/vault/games/legend-of-zelda)* (1986) was an action adventure without experience points, but its sequel, *Zelda II: The Adventure of Link*, released on the [Famicom Disk System](/vault/hardware/famicom-disk-system) in January 1987, turned side-on and let Link, in *Retro Gamer*'s words, "earn experience points to beef up his attacks, raise his stamina and also obtain magic points to cast spells".

By the mid-1990s these games had become yardsticks. *[Edge](/vault/magazines/edge)* captioned pictures of *Ys I and II* on the PC Engine and *Zelda: A Link To The Past* on the Super Famicom "classic action RPGs" in 1995, and wrote that "Nintendo's Zelda is the benchmark for action RPGs". Square's *[Secret of Mana](/vault/games/secret-of-mana)* showed why the style appealed. *Super Play* explained in 1994 that its "action-based combat system ('motion battle', as those Square folk call it) is similar to that of Zelda": hold a button to charge a weapon, then release it. The hit points and experience were still there, underneath, but a player could ignore them, which made the game "far more accessible than the turn-based, menu-driven combat" of *Final Fantasy* and its like.

## Diablo and the loot game

On the PC the form's great success was Blizzard's *[Diablo](/vault/games/diablo)*, which began as a turn-based game in the [roguelike](/vault/genres/roguelike) tradition and switched to real time during development. *[Computer Gaming World](/vault/magazines/computer-gaming-world)* hailed it in March 1997 as "the new king of action-RPGs"; *Edge* was cooler, calling it "essentially a *Gauntlet* clone". Its loop of clicking on monsters and collecting what they dropped became a reference point, and years later reviewers used it to explain other games. *Edge* said of *Borderlands 2* in 2012 that it was "like Diablo in terms of progressing your character through skill trees and gathering sackfuls of shiny loot": a first-person shooter built on the same loop, and what *Wireframe* would call "a classic looter-shooter".

## The Souls games

The newest branch came from FromSoftware. *[Demon's Souls](/vault/games/demons-souls)* (2009) and *[Dark Souls](/vault/games/dark-souls)* (2011) made combat slow, deliberate and deadly; *Dark Souls* had no difficulty settings at all, and its director, Hidetaka Miyazaki, told *Edge* he wanted "the most difficult game that it is possible to make, which at the same time can be conquered by those who persevere". Other studios followed so closely that the press named the style after them: *Wireframe* reviewed games as "Soulslike" from 2019, and one of its columnists described FromSoftware's *Elden Ring* in 2022 as its "latest entry into the genre they created, and still rule over". The [FromSoftware](/vault/companies/fromsoft) entry tells that story.
