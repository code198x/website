The racing game puts the player behind a vehicle and asks for speed under control. In the home-computer era it spanned a wide range: arcade conversions built on sprite scaling, top-down sprint racers that suited limited hardware, and — increasingly through the late 1980s — genuine attempts at handling models and circuit simulation.

The British press treated *driving game* and *racing game* as near-synonyms, using whichever fitted the sentence. Together they account for around 6,200 pages across nearly 2,700 issues in the archive. Where a distinction was drawn, "driving" tended to imply the arcade lineage and "racing" the competitive or simulation end.

## Fast facts

- **Core loop:** complete the route faster than the clock or the field.
- **Perspectives:** behind-the-car, top-down, and later in-cockpit.
- **Failure model:** time expiry more often than damage.
- **Defining titles:** *[Out Run](/vault/games/out-run)*, *Chase H.Q.*, *Stunt Car Racer*, *Sega Rally*.
- **Technical showcase:** the genre that most visibly demanded raw drawing speed.

## Genre conventions

- **The extending timer:** checkpoints granting seconds, an arcade inheritance that outlived the arcade.
- **Rubber-banding:** opponents that keep pace regardless, preserving tension over fairness.
- **Road as illusion:** on 8-bit machines the road was drawn, scaled and shifted, not modelled.
- **Course as set-piece:** each stage identified by scenery, not layout.
- **Handling model:** from pure lateral position to weight transfer and grip.

## Why it strained the hardware

Racing games demanded a full-screen redraw every frame at a time when most genres could get away with moving a few sprites. Home versions used scaled sprite stacks, road-line tables and aggressive colour reduction to keep the frame rate up, and the results varied enormously between machines. A racing conversion was often the sharpest available demonstration of what a given computer could and could not do.

## Evolution

- **1976-82:** top-down and fixed-perspective origins.
- **1986-88:** sprite-scaled arcade racers define the look, and conversions chase it.
- **1989-92:** polygon handling models arrive; *Stunt Car Racer* and *Hard Drivin'* trade smoothness for physics.
- **1993 onward:** texture-mapped 3D splits the genre into arcade and simulation branches.
