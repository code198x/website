Driving is the one kind of game everybody already knows how to play. Arcade owners understood that before there were video games at all. In 1983 *[Electronic Games](/vault/magazines/electronic-games)* remembered the "definitive arcade racing game of the pre-video era", a sit-down cabinet in which the player gripped "an authentic-seeming steering wheel" and steered a plastic car on a stem over a road painted on a scrolling belt. "What do you mean: what was the object?! The object was to stay on the road." The magazine thought that for all the technology since, the aim of most racing games "has stayed pretty constant".

What changed was the view, and then the car. The first video racers looked down on a track from above. By the early 1980s the screen had become a windscreen, with a road running to the horizon. By the end of the decade a second kind of racing game was trying to model how a real car grips, slides and breaks, and in the 1990s texture-mapped 3D carried both kinds into homes.

## Fast facts

- **Before video:** electro-mechanical driving games, such as Kasco's *Indy 500* (1968).
- **Wheel meets video:** Atari's *Gran Trak 10*, March 1974, a top-down track against the clock; Taito's scrolling *Speed Race* the same year.
- **Facing down the road:** Atari's *Night Driver* (1976); Sega's *Turbo* (1981); Namco's *[Pole Position](/vault/games/pole-position)* (1982); Sega's *[Out Run](/vault/games/out-run)* (1986).
- **Simulation branch:** Geoff Crammond's *Revs* for the BBC Micro (1985); Papyrus's *Indianapolis 500: The Simulation* (1989, by *Computer Gaming World*'s and *Motor Sport*'s later accounts; on sale by early 1990); Atari Games' *[Hard Drivin'](/vault/games/hard-drivin)* (1989).
- **Texture-mapped 3D:** Namco's *Ridge Racer* (1993), Sega's *[Daytona USA](/vault/games/daytona-usa)* (1994) and *[Sega Rally Championship](/vault/games/sega-rally)* (1995), Sony's *[Gran Turismo](/vault/games/gran-turismo)* (1997).
- **Usual enemy:** the clock. Many arcade racers had no rival cars that mattered; the player raced to the next checkpoint before time ran out.

## Top-down tracks

Atari's first video driving game, *Gran Trak 10*, appeared in March 1974: one player, one [steering wheel](/vault/hardware/steering-wheel), a track seen from overhead and a fixed time limit. It was not a happy start. *The Atari Book* calls it a "financial disaster", because the machines were so badly built that they came back to Atari "in droves". Taito's *Speed Race*, designed by Tomohiro Nishikado and released the same year, scrolled the road down the screen, and *Wireframe*'s columnist Mr Biffo credits it as the first vertically scrolling game.

Neither was really the beginning. In the same column Mr Biffo traced driving games back through Atari's *Space Race* (1973) to the electro-mechanical games that had inspired Nolan Bushnell, above all Chicago Coin's *Speedway* of 1969, a licensed version of Kasco's Japanese *Indy 500* (1968). *Indy 500* sold 2,000 machines in Japan, he wrote, and Chicago Coin's version "sold five times that in the US".

The top-down game kept going. Atari's *Sprint 2* (1976), programmed by Dennis Koble, let two players race at once and, *Retro Gamer* wrote in 2012, began "a long-running series for Atari". *Wireframe* sees the line continuing in games such as *Super Off Road* and Code Masters' *[Micro Machines](/vault/games/micro-machines)* (1991), which raced toy cars across desks, pool tables and bathtubs.

## Through the windscreen

The view swung round in 1976. Atari's *Night Driver* put the player behind the wheel at night, with white markers along the edges of the road. *Retro Gamer* explained that "the premise that you were driving at night was used to disguise the lack of detail on the road". *Edge* later called it "the very first '3D' racing game in history", but Atari had not invented the idea: *Retro Gamer* describes it as "a 3D take on the genre licensed from a German company", identified elsewhere as the 1976 German coin-op *Nürburgring 1*. Sega's *Road Race* of the same year also put the player in first person, and in *Wireframe*'s account shook its handlebars whenever the player hit another vehicle.

In 1983 *Electronic Games* thought *Night Driver* had "failed to dent the arcade on-ramp". What changed things, it said, was Sega's *Turbo*: "Only when Sega dared the laughter of the cynics by marketing Turbo did racing games get a serious second look." *Turbo* drove through countryside, cities, tunnels and night, over hills where oncoming cars rose and fell from view. The magazine sorted the genre into four kinds that year: driving games, where the point was to stay on the road; steering games, where it was to dodge things in the road; racing games, where it was to finish first or fastest; and car-battle games, the "really wild ones".

Namco's *Pole Position*, which reached Japanese arcades in September 1982 and led the American earnings charts in 1983, set the pattern for the next few years. It had a real circuit, Fuji Speedway, and made the player set a qualifying time of "73 game seconds or less" before starting the race. In Britain its name became shorthand: *[CRASH](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine)* reviewers described Spectrum games as "a 'Pole Position' type game" long before the official conversion arrived.

## Racing the clock

Arcade racers were usually races against time, not against other drivers. The operator needed each game to end, so the player had a few seconds to reach a checkpoint, and reaching it bought a few more. In Sega's *Out Run* the player had 80 seconds to reach the first checkpoint, then 65 for the next, with whatever was left carried forward, and there were no rival racers at all. *Out Run* sent a red Ferrari down a road that forked at the end of each stage, so the player chose a route through five stages to one of five goals. Taito's *[Chase H.Q.](/vault/games/chase-hq)* (1988) kept the clock but gave the chase a target: a criminal's car to be rammed off the road before time ran out.

The road these games drew was a trick. Each line of the screen below the horizon stood for one distance ahead, shifted sideways for bends and up or down for hills, with cars and scenery drawn as pictures scaled to their distance. Sega built custom chips to scale those pictures, from *[Hang-On](/vault/games/hang-on)* in 1985 onwards. The [pseudo-3D road](/vault/techniques/pseudo-3d-road) entry explains how the trick works, and [sprite scaling](/vault/techniques/sprite-scaling) covers the hardware behind it.

## Home conversions

Home computers had no scaling hardware, so their programmers did everything in software, and the road came first. "We always do the road first," Pete Hickinson of Teque Software told *[The One](/vault/magazines/the-one)* in 1989, while converting *Chase H.Q.* to the Atari ST and Amiga: "that's the central routine that the entire game is based on." Ocean's own Spectrum version began with John O'Brien rewriting the road routine from *WEC Le Mans* to run more than twice as fast, and scored 95% in *CRASH*.

Results varied between machines. US Gold's *Out Run* conversions ran from 93% for the Spectrum version in *CRASH* to 37% for the Amstrad version in *[Amstrad Action](/vault/magazines/amstrad-action)*. When Domark converted *Hard Drivin'*, whose world was built from solid polygons, the 16-bit versions ran at eight to ten frames a second and the Spectrum version at five. The [arcade conversion](/vault/culture/arcade-conversion) entry follows that trade more widely.

## Simulations

A second kind of racing game took the car more seriously than the arcade did. In spring 1985 Acornsoft released *Revs* for the BBC Micro, written by [Geoff Crammond](/vault/people/geoff-crammond) with the racing driver David Hunt. It modelled a Formula 3 Ralt RT3 at Silverstone, with the circuit's "curves, dips and hills accurately reproduced", as *[Acorn User](/vault/magazines/acorn-user)* reported, and adjustable wings "to change the 'ground effect'". Acornsoft's advertisement told buyers to study David Hunt's circuit diagram before trying for the lap record.

In America, David Kaemmer founded the Papyrus Design Group in the summer of 1987 and began work on *Indianapolis 500: The Simulation*, published by Electronic Arts. "Nothing like that had really been done over here before," he told *The One*. "At the time the market was being flooded with flight simulators." *[Computer Gaming World](/vault/magazines/computer-gaming-world)* gave the review to a former autocross champion, who listed the adjustable shock absorbers, wheel stagger, camber, anti-roll bars, tyre pressures and wings, and warned: "Gamers looking for immediate gratification should stick to Out Run."

In the arcade, Atari Games' *Hard Drivin'* (1989) combined a polygon world with a handling model and a steering wheel that fought back, and its Stunt Track ran round a loop-the-loop. Crammond's own *Stunt Car Racer* (MicroStyle, 1989) put the handling model on "eight roller coaster-style racing tracks", according to *[The Games Machine](/vault/magazines/the-games-machine)*, with a car winched up by a crane and dropped onto the track for the start.

## Texture-mapped 3D

Sega's *Virtua Racing* (1992), made by [Yu Suzuki](/vault/people/yu-suzuki)'s team on the new Model 1 board, was Sega's first polygon 3D game. A year later Namco's *Ridge Racer* covered its polygons with texture maps. *[Edge](/vault/magazines/edge)* saw it at the Amusement Machine show at Makuhari Messe, near Tokyo, in 1993 and called it the most exciting example yet "of the new era of texture-mapped 3D graphics". *Ridge Racer* became a launch game for the PlayStation, and Sega answered with *Daytona USA* on its [Model 2](/vault/techniques/model-2) board.

The two branches of the genre now ran on the same technology. *Sega Rally Championship* (1995) put rally cars on gravel and mud where they slid "realistically", in the words of Codemasters producer Guy Wilday, whose *Colin McRae Rally* was built around that handling. The team behind its Saturn conversion said that Namco's cars seemed to drift "on invisible rails", while theirs were "based on physical calculations". Psygnosis's *[WipEout](/vault/games/wipeout)* (1995) left the road altogether for anti-gravity craft.

*Gran Turismo* (1997) went furthest towards the car. Players bought real production cars, passed driving licences and spent their prize money on tuning. *Edge* gave it ten out of ten for a game that "so brilliantly straddles the divide between a comprehensive simulation and an accessible, incredibly playable arcade game". By Polyphony Digital's figures it sold 10.85 million copies, and the series had passed 90 million by 2022.
