In the autumn of 1996 *Edge* walked round a Japanese games show and picked out "possibly the strangest title on show": a children's game, drawn by the American cartoonist Rodney Greenblat, in which the player copies the ever more complicated moves of a kung-fu teacher called Master Onion. Within a year its creator was telling *Computer and Video Games* that *[PaRappa the Rapper](/vault/games/parappa-the-rapper)* had "sent Japan into a PlayStation-buying frenzy last Christmas". That is where the music game, or "rhythm action" as the British magazines called it, took off. The rules are simple. Music plays, prompts scroll towards a line, and the player presses, steps, scratches or strums as each one arrives. Within ten years the genre had filled Japanese arcades with turntables and dance floors, and a decade after that it had sold millions of plastic guitars in the West.

## Fast facts

- **The idea:** keep time with a piece of music by matching on-screen prompts, with the score, and often the music itself, reacting to how well you do.
- **The breakthrough:** *PaRappa the Rapper* (Sony, PlayStation, Japan, 1996), by the musician Masaya Matsuura.
- **The arcade genre:** Konami's *[Beatmania](/vault/games/beatmania)* (1997) and its successors, which Konami grouped as [Bemani](/vault/companies/bemani), including *[Dance Dance Revolution](/vault/games/dance-dance-revolution)* (1998).
- **The Western boom:** [Harmonix](/vault/companies/harmonix)'s *[Guitar Hero](/vault/games/guitar-hero)* (2005) and *[Rock Band](/vault/games/rock-band)* (2007).
- **The slump:** price cuts in 2009; in February 2011 [Activision](/vault/companies/activision) closed its *Guitar Hero* business.
- **Since then:** *Rock Band 4* (2015), Harmonix's *Fortnite Festival* (2023), and arcade drum and dance machines still running in Japan.

## Before PaRappa

Games had asked players to copy sequences of lights and sounds long before they asked them to keep a beat. [Ralph Baer](/vault/people/ralph-baer) remembered playing Atari's coin-op *Touch-Me* at a Chicago trade show in 1976, a cabinet with four big buttons that lit up in random order, and finding it "nice game-play, terrible execution". With the toy designers Marvin Glass & Associates he turned the idea into a handheld game, choosing four notes a bugle can play, G, C, E and G, so that any sequence would sound pleasant. Milton Bradley sold it as *Simon*. When *Edge* first saw *Dance Dance Revolution* in 1998, it described it as "much like an over-sized Simon game".

A few games in the 1980s and early 1990s put music and movement together. *Edge*, looking back in 1998, credited [Epyx](/vault/companies/epyx) with leading the way "back in the '80s with *Breakdance*", a Commodore 64 game inspired by the dance craze of the day. On the NES, the [Power Pad](/vault/hardware/power-pad) mat's *Dance Aerobics* asked players to copy the computer's steps. *Retro Gamer* counts the rhythm mini-games in *ToeJam & Earl II* among the genre's earliest ancestors, and in 1997 *Computer and Video Games* compared *PaRappa* with an earlier PC game built around the band Aerosmith, *Quest for Fame*. But as *Edge* put it, "in terms of true games, though, there would be little else" until *PaRappa*.

## PaRappa the Rapper

Masaya Matsuura was a pop musician with ten albums behind him and a contract with Sony Music. He told *Computer and Video Games* that he had started composing on computers in 1983 and grown tired of computer music that "always sounded the same". He built his own sampler software for the PC, found rap "very funny to play on the computer", and prototyped a soundtrack that changed with the player's performance on Philips' CD-i before taking the idea to the PlayStation.

The game he made with Greenblat was a series of lessons. A teacher raps a line, symbols for the PlayStation's buttons pass along a bar at the top of the screen, and PaRappa answers by pressing them in time. The characters are flat cut-outs, "wafer-thin" in the magazine's words, moving through scenes staged like a pop video. The music does not come from the console's sound chip. Matsuura explained that the CD carries four versions of each track, for "cool", "good", "bad" and "awful", and the game switches between them as the player's rating rises and falls. Do well enough and the teacher leaves the stage and lets PaRappa freestyle; do badly and, as *Computer and Video Games* put it, "the rap sounds embarrassingly demented".

The magazine's March 1997 import review called it "the world's first rap game" and loved it, but reported that Sony's British office "don't reckon there's any market for it". Sony did release it in the West, and it did not sell as hoped. *Edge* wrote at the end of 1998 that the game "received plaudits aplenty in the press" but "failed to sell in the west in quantities anywhere near the levels Sony expected". Matsuura's team followed it with *Um Jammer Lammy* (1999), which kept the controls and swapped the rap for rock guitar.

## Konami's arcade genre

Konami showed *Beatmania* at the JAMMA arcade show in 1997, where *Edge* described it as "the first game based on - get this - DJ mixing". Its controls were five keys and a turntable. Players matched the prompts on screen with the keys and now and then scratched the record, and early attempts, *Edge* warned, "can get quite messy". Hiroshi Takyasu, manager of Konami's sound development, told the magazine he had not expected it to catch on: "I didn't think Beat-Mania would become as popular as it is now." The reason, he thought, was that "players can make a good-looking performance in front of other people looking on. Players can show off."

Showing off became the point. *Dance Dance Revolution*, made by a separate Konami team in Kobe, was "a massive hit with JAMMA attendees" in 1998, *Edge* reported, and crowded out the new games from Sega and Namco. Players stood on the machine and stepped on four arrow panels in time with the music; the [dance mat](/vault/hardware/dance-mat) entry follows it into homes and, as *Dancing Stage*, into British arcades and toy shops. At the AOU show in Tokyo in February 1999, rhythm games were the leading genre, and Konami showed two more: *Drummania*, and "the rock-inspired and self-explanatory" *Guitar Freaks*, with a plastic guitar for a controller. An "elaborate version" linked the two cabinets "for a semi-band experience", eight years before *Rock Band*. By then Konami had a name for the whole family, Bemani, from *Beatmania*.

Other companies followed with controllers of their own. *Retro Gamer*'s 2004 family tree of the genre runs from Enix's *Bust A Move* (sold in Europe as *Bust-A-Groove*) and Sega's *[Space Channel 5](/vault/games/space-channel-5)* through *Vib-Ribbon*, which made its levels from any music CD the player put in, to Sega's maracas game *Samba de Amigo* and Namco's *Taiko no Tatsujin* drums. Namco's game is still in Japanese arcades. *Wireframe* called it in 2021 "such a national institution in Japan" that its drum peripheral turns up cheaply in second-hand shops.

## Harmonix, Guitar Hero and Rock Band

In the West the genre's story runs through one studio in Cambridge, Massachusetts. Harmonix's *Frequency* (2001, PlayStation 2) played, *Edge* thought, like "one of the early levels from *Tempest*". Each channel stood for an instrument, vocals, bass, drums or guitar; the player completed a bar on one, then moved across to another, building up the whole track part by part. *Amplitude* (2003) added online play, and Harmonix and Konami made *Karaoke Revolution* the same year.

Then came *Guitar Hero*. Harmonix built it with the independent peripherals maker RedOctane, around a plastic controller modelled on a Gibson guitar. It came out in North America in November 2005. *Edge* later called it "a surprise hit". In 2006 Activision bought RedOctane for $100 million, and because Harmonix did not own the *Guitar Hero* name, it "found itself cut loose".

Harmonix's answer, *Rock Band*, came out in North America in November 2007 and in Europe the following year, published with MTV and Electronic Arts. It added drums and a microphone to the guitar, so four people could play one song together. It was also the first game to ask record companies for the original multitrack studio recordings of its 58 songs, so that each instrument could be played separately. "Nobody had ever asked for multitrack masters before," the project lead, Greg LoPiccolo, told *Edge*, and some of them could not be found. Harmonix grew from 90 to 300 staff in two years, with developers "sitting in the hallways with their laptops resting on skateboards" because there were not enough desks. The company had never made hardware before; the chief executive, Alex Rigopulos, remembered going "from never having developed hardware to a year later shipping millions of units" of several instruments at once.

## The slump

The boom was short. Within a few years there were, as one analyst counted, "four or five versions of *Rock Band* and *Guitar Hero*", each followed by add-on songs. In January 2010 *Edge*'s review of British retail in 2009 reported "the waning of the music game market", with rhythm-action games "undergoing dramatic price reductions ahead of Christmas". The same analyst thought players were "unlikely to spend money on peripherals on a regular basis". The surprise hit of that Christmas was a dance game with no instrument to buy, Ubisoft's *Just Dance*, which the company's British managing director said "significantly outperformed our expectations".

On 9 February 2011 Activision announced that "due to continued declines in the music genre" it would disband its *Guitar Hero* business unit and cancel the year's game. Looking back in 2012, Rigopulos did not accept the simplest explanation. "You often hear people saying Activision killed the category by over-saturating it with too many releases," he told *Edge*. "Maybe that's part of the story, but I certainly don't think that's the entire story." He pointed to the recession, to gameplay that had barely changed, and to casual players who were "easily lured away to the next bubble". Both companies, he said, went into the fight believing there was room for only one series, and *Edge* added that they overspent on marketing and song licences competing for the same shelves, while stockpiled instruments were later sold off at slashed prices.

## Since the boom

The genre shrank but did not end. Harmonix moved on to the dance series *Dance Central*, and brought back its band game as *Rock Band 4* in October 2015; it stopped adding new songs to it in January 2024. Epic Games bought Harmonix in 2021, and in December 2023 the studio launched *Fortnite Festival* inside *Fortnite*, a band game with lanes for lead, bass, drums and vocals that later added support for plastic guitars. In virtual reality, *Beat Saber*, in which players slice through blocks in time with the music, became the rhythm game *Wireframe*'s writers reached for as an example. And in Japan the arcade machines that started it are still there, drums, dance floors and all.
