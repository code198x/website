## Overview

A wargame simulates military conflict at the operational level: the player commands formations across terrain, manages supply and morale, and wins through position, not reflex. It arrived on home computers as a direct descendant of the tabletop hex-and-counter hobby, and for years it kept that hobby's conventions — dense manuals, numbered scenarios, and an assumption that the player already knew what a zone of control was.

It was a substantial category in the British press. Searching this library for *wargame*, *wargames* and *wargaming* returns **7,956 pages across 2,972 issues**; widening to *war game* and *war games* raises it to 11,089 pages, though that catches other senses of the phrase. Either way it is a large body of coverage, and usually in its own reviewing ghetto: many magazines gave wargames a separate column, on the reasonable grounds that their readership overlapped little with the arcade audience.

Distinguish it from later real-time strategy. The wargame is turn-based, historically specific, and interested in accuracy; the RTS that displaced it in the 1990s is real-time, usually fictional, and interested in tempo.

## Fast facts

- **Core loop:** assess, order, resolve, repeat by turn.
- **Map:** hexes or squares, with terrain modifying movement and combat.
- **Subject:** usually a specific historical campaign.
- **Defining titles:** the SSI catalogue, *Laser Squad*, *Chaos*, later *UFO: Enemy Unknown*.
- **Audience:** older, overlapping with tabletop wargaming.

## Genre conventions

- **Turn structure:** orders issued, then resolved simultaneously or in sequence.
- **Combat resolution:** odds ratios and a random factor, inherited from tabletop tables.
- **Supply and morale:** units degraded by attrition, not destroyed outright.
- **Fog of war:** limited intelligence about enemy positions.
- **Scenario sets:** one engine, many historical situations.


## The £1.99 crossover

The wargame's specialist audience was a commercial ceiling, and the clearest
record of someone going through it is *Edge*'s September 1996 retreview of
[Julian Gollop](/vault/people/julian-gollop)'s *Rebelstar*.

*Rebelstar Raiders* appeared in 1984 as a two-player, single-screen Spectrum
skirmish wargame, published at full price by Red Shift. *Edge*'s verdict on how
it did is one line long: it "sold less than 'some'".

Gollop rebuilt it — several screens, a one-player option, the word *Raiders*
dropped — and took it to [Firebird](/vault/companies/firebird), who released it
in 1986 at **£1.99**, "whereupon it promptly sold thousands".

The design did not become less of a wargame. Only the price changed, and the
audience that had been the genre's boundary turned out to be a function of what
it cost to find out whether you liked it. *Edge* credits *Rebelstar* with
introducing **action points** and **opportunity fire**, and with inventing what
it calls the sugar-coated wargame — the strategy game that does not require you
to already be a wargamer.

That line runs directly to [*Laser Squad*](/vault/games/laser-squad) and then to
[*UFO: Enemy Unknown*](/vault/games/x-com-ufo-defense), which is how a hex-map
hobby became one of the best-known systems in strategy gaming.

## Evolution

- **1980-84:** direct translations of tabletop systems, often by their designers.
- **1985-89:** the computer starts doing what paper could not — hidden movement, automatic resolution, an opponent.
- **1990s:** the squad-level branch (*Laser Squad*, *UFO: Enemy Unknown*) reaches a wider audience than the operational one.
- **Mid-1990s:** real-time strategy takes the mainstream, and the wargame returns to its specialist base.
