"Everyone knows that wargamers thrive on 'complexity'," wrote Angus Ryall in October 1984, introducing *[CRASH](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine)*'s new column for them, "and this is what has kept the hobby so elitist for so long." Home computers, he thought, should have changed that, "by getting rid of the number-crunching and all those fiddly little card counters that always end up down the back of the settee". Most of the Spectrum wargames he had just played had not managed it.

A wargame puts the player in the commander's chair. Instead of steering one tank or one soldier, the player moves divisions, fleets or squads across a map, manages supply and reinforcements, and wins by position and timing more than by reflexes. The computer version grew straight out of the board-wargame hobby of hex maps and cardboard counters, and for years it kept that hobby's rulebooks, its historical subjects and much of its audience. The history of the genre on home computers is largely the story of that inheritance, and of designers trying to sell it to people who had never pushed a counter.

## Fast facts

- **What it models:** a battle, campaign or war, from a command perspective; usually a historical one.
- **Typical structure:** turns, with orders given and then resolved; maps of hexes or squares; terrain, supply and morale affecting movement and combat.
- **Early publishers:** Strategic Simulations Inc. (California, from 1980), Strategic Studies Group (Sydney, from 1983), and in Britain M.C. Lothlorien, Cases Computer Simulations (CCS) and PSS.
- **Landmarks:** *Computer Bismarck* (SSI, 1980), *Eastern Front (1941)* (Chris Crawford, Atari, 1981), *Carriers at War* (SSG, 1984), *Theatre Europe* (PSS, 1985), *Arnhem* (CCS, 1985), *Rebelstar* (Firebird, 1986), *Panzer General* (SSI, 1994).
- **In the press:** its own columns, including *CRASH*'s "Frontline" from October 1984; *Computer Gaming World* was full of wargames from its first issue.

## From the board to the Apple II

Board wargames were a hobby with its own publishers long before home computers. Joel Billings had played through "the whole series of Avalon Hill wargames", *Antic* reported in 1985, and his lasting problem had been finding opponents. Computers could take the paperwork away and, more important, could be the opponent: "90% of board war gamers played solitaire, moving the troops on both sides of the board."

In 1979 Billings looked for programmers who were also wargamers, and found John Lyon, who was already writing a game about the hunt for the *Bismarck* on an Apple II. Their company, Strategic Simulations Inc. (SSI), released *Computer Bismarck* early in 1980 at $59.95. Its advertisements pitched it at exactly the players Billings knew: "the computer program eliminates the drudgery of paper & pencil wargames", and it was "More like Chess than Pong". *Computer Gaming World* later called SSI "the very first company to publish a serious wargame for a micro-computer", while admitting that someone would write in about an earlier game for the Altair. What set SSI apart was presentation: *Bismarck* came in a box, with a typeset rulebook, reference cards, plastic-coated maps and grease pencils, when most computer games came in a plastic bag.

SSI built a catalogue on the formula. Billings told *Creative Computing* in 1981 that its games were designed for two players first and a computer opponent second, because a game designed only for solitaire play tends to "simplify a lot of things". In its first three years five designers or teams, including Lyon, Dan Bunten and the Australian Roger Keating, wrote 18 of SSI's 28 games. By 1988 SSI had published almost 90 titles and had sales of more than $5 million in its best year.

Keating went on to co-found [Strategic Studies Group](#strategic-studies-group) in Sydney. In the same years Chris Crawford's *Eastern Front (1941)* for the Atari 400 and 800 showed what a wargame could look like on a machine with good graphics: a scrolling map of about ten screens, orders given with the joystick. *Computer Gaming World*'s first issue, in November 1981, called it "the most impressive computer wargame on the market".

## Strategic Studies Group

Roger Keating and Ian Trout started Strategic Studies Group in Sydney. Its first game, *Reach for the Stars* (1983), was a space strategy game for up to four players, three of which could be the computer, and *Computer Gaming World*'s reviewer credited its "great artificial intelligence" to Keating's earlier designs for SSI. He also noted that the rules had been written "in a foreign language (Australian)", in which "C/R" meant Control-R, not carriage return. SSG then turned to history. *Carriers at War*, fleet carrier operations in the Pacific from 1941 to 1945, was advertised in 1984 as "Roger Keating and Ian Trout's greatest gaming achievement", and in 1986 *Europe Ablaze* moved the same system to the strategic bombing of Europe.

SSG was still making operational wargames in the 21st century. In 2010 its own website listed *Kharkov: Disaster on the Donets* and a new *Carriers at War*, sold through the publisher Matrix Games.

## British wargames

In Britain the first wargames came in on import. *Computer Gamer*'s Martin Croft wrote in 1986 that "the first real computer wargame to reach this country" was *Eastern Front*, and that American titles came first; later "people began writing for the Spectrum and the BBC". U.S. Gold went on to publish SSI and MicroProse wargames in Britain "at far more competitive prices".

**M.C. Lothlorien**, of Cheadle Hulme in Cheshire, started with ancient-world strategy games for the ZX81, such as *Tyrant of Athens* and *Roman Empire*, and by April 1983 advertised itself as "Britain's leading wargames software house". Its Warmaster series included *Johnny Reb* and *Paras*. *CRASH* explained the appeal in 1984: "Lothlorien specialise in battle strategy war games which require hours to play properly." Croft was less kind. The games were "very complex, but unfortunately rather dull", and mostly for two players, which meant looking away while an opponent typed in orders for dozens of units: few things were "more likely to lead to terminal boredom". A marketing deal with Argus Press Software brought a change. Its game of the Battle of the Bulge, sold as *Bulge*, which *CRASH*'s Sean Masterson called "the first title to take advantage of Lothlorien's skills as a design and programming house in combination with Argus Press Software's marketing power", was glossily produced, had single- and two-player options and, Croft noted, ran in real time.

**Cases Computer Simulations (CCS)** began in 1982 in south-east London with business simulations such as *Airline* and *Autochef*, then had a wargaming hit with R.T. Smith's *Arnhem*. *CRASH* reviewed it in June 1985. It came in a big box with a sixteen-page booklet and fold-out map, and replaced typed coordinates with a cursor. Its five scenarios ran from seven turns, about an hour, to 26 turns and "eight to ten hours". Smith followed it with *Desert Rats*. In 1986 *CRASH*'s wargame columnist named CCS and Lothlorien as "responsible for many of the pioneering titles" on the Spectrum.

**PSS** of Coventry ran a Wargamers series, and its best-known game was also its most controversial. In *Theatre Europe* (1985) NATO and the Warsaw Pact fight a war in Germany, and the player can call for chemical and nuclear strikes; to take full control of the nuclear option the player had to phone a real number for an authorisation code. The designer, Alan Steele, told *ZZAP!64* that PSS had researched both sides' forces, including contacts with the Ministry of Defence and the Soviet embassy. When they let the computer play itself, the Warsaw Pact won every time, leaving NATO the choice of nuclear weapons or losing West Germany, so PSS "had to fiddle the figures" to make a playable game. PSS added optional arcade action screens to its wargames; *ZZAP!64*'s advice was that "serious wargamers" should play without them.

## Sugar-coating

The squad-level wargame took a different road to a wider audience. [Julian Gollop](/vault/people/julian-gollop)'s *Rebelstar Raiders* (Red Shift, 1984) was a two-player skirmish on one Spectrum screen. *Edge*, looking back in 1996, said it "sold less than 'some'"; Gollop later remembered being told it sold 40,000 to 50,000. He rebuilt it with a bigger map and a computer opponent, and [Firebird](/vault/companies/firebird) released it as *Rebelstar* in 1986. In *Edge*'s account Firebird "saw a simple 2D wargame which understandably meant niche market" and priced it at £1.99, "whereupon it promptly sold thousands". *Edge* credited it with action points and opportunity fire, and with introducing "the concept of sugar-coated wargames, where a strategy game with sufficient dressing up would appeal to the middle-of-the-road consumer". *[Laser Squad](/vault/games/laser-squad)* (1988) and *[UFO: Enemy Unknown](/vault/games/x-com-ufo-defense)* (1994) followed from it. How that turn system works is described under [turn-based combat](/vault/techniques/turn-based-combat).

SSI made the same move with the operational wargame. *Computer Gaming World* reviewed *Panzer General* in January 1995 as "in many ways an atypical SSI wargame", with no "detailed orders of battle, complicated supply rules, or arcane combat routines". It was easy enough to learn without the manual, and its campaigns were "such fun that even non-wargamers are likely to find themselves hooked". Its design team included Joel Billings.

## The 1990s and after

The turn was the wargame's most recognisable convention, and in the 1990s it began to look old. *Edge*'s strategy feature of November 1995 thought it "ironic that the most hectic simulations of real life — wargames — feature the most regimented turn-based structure of all". The [real-time strategy](/vault/genres/rts-genre) games that followed *[Dune II](/vault/games/dune-ii)* took much of the mainstream strategy audience.

SSI itself changed hands. In 1994 [Mindscape](/vault/companies/mindscape) bought it, and *Computer Gaming World*'s report of the sale summed up the company as one "founded in 1979" that had "built its reputation on high quality war and strategy games for the Apple II". Its later owners included the toy company Mattel, and by 2001 the business was part of Ubi Soft, which stopped using the SSI name a few years later.
