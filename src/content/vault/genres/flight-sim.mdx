The flight sim asks the player to fly something plausibly: to manage thrust, attitude, fuel and instruments, not steer alone. It covers combat aircraft, civil aviation and — importantly for the British scene — space trading, where *Elite* turned a flight model into an economy.

It was the era's most demanding genre in the literal sense. Flight sims shipped with the thickest manuals, expected keyboard overlays, and assumed a player willing to read before playing. In a market otherwise moving toward immediacy, they made study the point.

The archive puts it among the largest categories: roughly 16,000 pages across nearly 5,000 issues, under *flight sim*, *flight simulator* and *flight simulation* — four spellings the press used interchangeably.

## Fast facts

- **Core loop:** take off, navigate, engage or trade, land.
- **Input:** the whole keyboard, plus a joystick.
- **Documentation:** manuals as thick as the game was long.
- **Defining titles:** *[Elite](/vault/games/elite)*, *[Starglider](/vault/games/starglider)*, *[Wing Commander](/vault/games/wing-commander)*, [Microsoft](/vault/companies/microsoft)'s *Flight Simulator*.
- **Skill model:** knowledge and planning over reaction speed.

## Genre conventions

- **Instrument panel:** a substantial fraction of the screen given to gauges.
- **Multiple view modes:** cockpit, chase, external, map.
- **Flight model:** momentum and inertia, not direct positional control.
- **Failure by attrition:** fuel, damage and navigation errors, not enemy fire alone.
- **Persistent progress:** ranks, ratings, cargo, or a save that mattered.

## The British variant

*Elite* (1984) is the genre's local landmark and does not fit its usual description. It combined a wireframe 3D flight model with procedurally generated galaxies and an open-ended trading economy, on a BBC Micro. Its influence ran through the British scene for a decade, and its structure — fly, trade, upgrade, choose your own goals — arguably founded a genre of its own that the period had no name for.

## Evolution

- **1980-83:** wireframe beginnings, constrained by 8-bit maths.
- **1984:** *Elite* fuses flight, trade and open structure.
- **1986-90:** 16-bit machines allow filled polygons and credible terrain.
- **1990s:** the genre splits into hardcore study sims and accessible space combat.
