A tycoon game promises a fortune to anyone who can build one. The player starts with a small company and not much money, and grows it into an empire of railways, bus routes, rollercoasters or elephant enclosures, while the program's customers decide whether the empire pays. The word came from one MicroProse game, *Railroad Tycoon*, in 1990. Nine years later [Chris Sawyer](/vault/people/chris-sawyer)'s *[RollerCoaster Tycoon](/vault/games/roller-coaster-tycoon)* sold so well that publishers put "Tycoon" on everything from golf courses to fast food. By Christmas 2001 *PC Gamer* was calling it "the most over-used word in the history of computer games".

Tycoon games are one branch of the wider [management game](/vault/genres/management-game), which covers football clubs, cities and airlines as well. The tycoon line is the part that grew from railways into parks, and it is mostly a PC story.

## Fast facts

- **Named after:** [Sid Meier](/vault/people/sid-meier) and Bruce Shelley's *Railroad Tycoon* ([MicroProse](/vault/companies/microprose), 1990).
- **The core:** build a network or a park, set prices, keep customers happy, and grow a company from its profits.
- **Main line:** *Railroad Tycoon* (1990), *[Transport Tycoon](/vault/games/transport-tycoon)* (1994), *[RollerCoaster Tycoon](/vault/games/roller-coaster-tycoon)* (1999), *Zoo Tycoon* (2001).
- **The boom:** after *RollerCoaster Tycoon*, many games took the name, including *Monopoly Tycoon*, *Fast Food Tycoon*, *Golf Resort Tycoon*, *Ski Resort Tycoon* and *Mall Tycoon*.

## Railroad Tycoon

*Railroad Tycoon* gave the player a million dollars and an empty map. There were four to choose from: the eastern United States in 1830, the western United States in 1866, England in 1828 and Europe in 1900. The player surveyed routes, bridged rivers, bought period locomotives and matched cargo to demand, carrying coal to a city that turned it into steel and steel to another that turned it into manufactured goods. Three computer-controlled railways, headed by real railway barons of the period, competed for the same towns. A player could start a rate war by pushing track into a rival's station, or buy enough of its stock to take it over, and lose the job in the same way. The landscape changed with each game, so that Chicago might be "a broad-shouldered metropolis in one game and just a burg near a lake in another".

*Compute!*'s Gregg Keizer called it "a capitalist's dream" and praised an interface that productivity software "could learn a thing or two from", though he wanted an undo command for the track he laid by mistake. In Britain *The One* gave the PC version 93% and compared it with *[SimCity](/vault/games/sim-city)*: both challenged the player "to create a better, more efficient system every time you play". The game sold for $59.95 in America and £34.99 in Britain, and came to the Amiga the next year.

MicroProse later let other studios revisit it. *Railroad Tycoon II* (1998) was made by PopTop Software, whose Phil Steinmeyer, *Computer Gaming World* reported, "jumped at the chance to remake one of his favorite games" when MicroProse started shopping its old titles around.

## Transport Tycoon

The next tycoon was named to match. Chris Sawyer's transport business game started life as *Chris Sawyer's Transport Game*, and MicroProse, which published it in November 1994, renamed it *Transport Tycoon* "to tie in with their own *Railroad Tycoon*". It added roads, docks and airports to the railway, gave the player a century from 1930 to 2030, and let towns grow when they were served well. *[Edge](/vault/magazines/edge)* called it "utterly riveting" and decided that "*Railroad Tycoon* was a mere rehearsal". Sawyer wrote it by himself in x86 assembly language, using Microsoft's Macro Assembler, and it came out for PCs, the Macintosh and the PlayStation, but never for the Amiga.

Other publishers were already borrowing the word. In the same month *PC Gamer* previewed *Pizza Tycoon*, a German game from Software 2000 that MicroProse was translating for Britain. Players designed pizzas from over 70 ingredients and could pay the Mafia to bomb rival restaurants.

## RollerCoaster Tycoon

*RollerCoaster Tycoon* (1999) turned the name into a trend. Sawyer's theme park game let players build their own coasters piece by piece and watch individual guests enjoy them or be sick. In his own words it is "99% written in x86 assembler/machine code", and he programmed it alone, with music by Allister Brimble and graphics by Simon Foster. It was the best-selling PC game of 1999 in America, with 719,635 copies according to the retail tracker PC Data, and it sold another 1,251,770 copies there in 2000.

Imitators followed quickly. Reviewing *Zoo Tycoon* in 2002, *Computer Gaming World*'s Jeff Green observed that "everybody and their grandma has decided to make a Tycoon game this year", and called *RollerCoaster Tycoon* "the game that started this whole dumb trend". In 2003 the magazine said it had "started a barrage of clones both good and bad", from *SimGolf* and *Monopoly Tycoon* to *Mall Tycoon*. *PC Gamer*'s Christmas 2001 issue reviewed *Fast Food Tycoon*, *Golf Resort Tycoon*, *Ski Resort Tycoon* and *Zoo Tycoon* together, and had already given *Monopoly Tycoon* 88% the month before. Its *Golf Resort Tycoon* review found the game "an unnaturally close sibling to *Theme Park*, *Rollercoaster Tycoon* and their ilk".

## Zoo Tycoon

*Zoo Tycoon*, developed by Blue Fang Games and published by Microsoft in October 2001, swapped the park's rides for animals. Players built enclosures, matched each animal's terrain, plants and companions to its needs, and kept visitors, keepers and animals happy, either in 13 scenarios or in a freeform mode. Green found that it followed the *RollerCoaster Tycoon* formula "to the letter, just substituting a zoo environment for an amusement park". He thought it too tame, and some habitats tedious to get right: he and his seven-year-old spent 20 minutes hunting for "the one tile of sand that was dragging down our lion's happiness rating". Still, he called it "the rare family game that doesn't bore parents to death". *PC Gamer*'s review, by Richard Cobbett, was harder on it. It complained that the game had brought back the "hideously blocky graphics" of 1999, and ended: "buy Rollercoaster Tycoon and get a pet instead."

It kept selling all the same. Expansions added dinosaurs (*Dinosaur Digs*, 2002) and sea creatures (*Marine Mania*), the original was still in *Computer Gaming World*'s top 20 chart in January 2003, and *Zoo Tycoon 2* followed in 2004. In 2013 [Frontier Developments](/vault/companies/frontier-developments), which had already taken over *RollerCoaster Tycoon*, made a new *Zoo Tycoon* for Microsoft, released on the Xbox 360 and Xbox One in November 2013.

## What came after

The two Sawyer games have outlived their publishers. The rights to *Transport Tycoon* returned to Sawyer, who sold them to Atari in 2024, and fan re-creations, OpenTTD and OpenRCT2, keep *Transport Tycoon Deluxe* and *RollerCoaster Tycoon 2* running on modern computers. Frontier's *Planet Coaster* (2016), which *Wireframe* called "a fantastic spiritual sequel to the likes of *Theme Park* and *RollerCoaster Tycoon*", carried the idea on without the name.
