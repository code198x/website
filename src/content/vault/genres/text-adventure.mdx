Text adventures described worlds in prose and responded to typed commands. "GO NORTH" moved you. "GET LAMP" picked it up. "EXAMINE MAILBOX" revealed secrets. Infocom elevated the form to literature; countless bedroom coders created their own with tools like The Quill. The genre evolved into interactive fiction when graphics arrived but the principles—exploration, puzzle-solving, narrative—endure.

## Fast facts

- **Interface:** text descriptions, typed commands.
- **Parser:** interprets player input.
- **Pioneer:** *Adventure* (Colossal Cave, 1976).
- **Commercial peak:** Infocom (1980s).
- **Modern term:** interactive fiction.
- **In this library:** 3,448 pages across 2,037 issues mention *text adventure*.

## The parser

How text adventures understood you:
- **Verb-noun:** "GET KEY", "OPEN DOOR".
- **Complex sentences:** "PUT THE BLUE KEY IN THE WOODEN BOX".
- **Disambiguation:** "Which key do you mean?".
- **Vocabulary limits:** only certain words recognised.

## Notable creators

Text adventure innovators:
- **Infocom:** *Zork*, *Hitchhiker's Guide*, commercial leader.
- **Scott Adams:** early commercial adventures.
- **Level 9:** British adventure company.
- **Magnetic Scrolls:** *The Pawn*, illustrated text.

## The genre with its own staff

Text adventures are the only category in this period that British magazines
routinely gave a dedicated columnist, which says something about how separate
its audience was.

*CRASH* ran Derek Brewster's **Adventure Trail**; *Sinclair User* ran
[Mike Gerrard](/vault/people/mike-gerrard). Between them the two names appear on
**390 pages** of the Spectrum press held here, and *Adventure Trail* alone on
187. The magazines also kept a separate Adventure chart, on the reasonable
grounds that the games competing for an adventure player's money were not the
games competing for everyone else's.

That separation is visible in the sales data. A game could hold **#1** in
*CRASH*'s Adventure chart and **#16** in the general one in consecutive months —
[*Heavy on the Magick*](/vault/games/heavy-on-the-magick) did exactly that. The
adventure audience was devoted, vocal, and smaller than it looked from inside
the column.

## The Quill and the amateur boom

The other thing that separates this genre is that its players could make one.

[*The Quill*](/vault/tools/the-quill) reached the Spectrum in 1984, and *CRASH*
introduced it as a machine-code utility that let someone who could not program
produce "high-speed machine code adventures of superior quality to many
available at the moment". It appears on **270 pages** of the Spectrum press held
here.

The result was a flood of amateur adventures sold by mail order, reviewed in the
adventure columns beside commercial releases, and priced accordingly. No other
genre of the period had a production tool good enough to erase the line between
reading the column and appearing in it — and it is the closest 1980s parallel to
the modern indie tool.

## The British commercial houses
