## Overview

The management game gives the player an institution, not an avatar. Play consists of allocating limited resources, setting policy and watching consequences arrive over simulated time. The player rarely acts directly: they set conditions, advance the clock, and read the results.

It suited 8-bit hardware unusually well. A game made of numbers, menus and tables needed almost no graphical capability, which meant a management title could be deeper than an action game on identical hardware. That economy is why the genre flourished on the Spectrum, where an ambitious simulation could ship in 48K.

## Fast facts

- **Core loop:** allocate, set policy, advance time, respond.
- **Interface:** menus, tables and figures over direct control.
- **Time model:** discrete turns — a week, a match, a month.
- **Defining titles:** *Football Manager*, *SimCity*, *Theme Park*, *Transport Tycoon*.
- **Hardware fit:** demanding on simulation, undemanding on graphics.

## Genre conventions

- **Indirect agency:** the player influences outcomes but does not execute them.
- **Budget as the central constraint:** money limits everything and recurs every turn.
- **Emergent failure:** collapse arrives through compounding small decisions, not a single mistake.
- **No win condition:** many are open-ended, ending only when the player stops.
- **Readable numbers:** the interface is the game, so table design is design.

## The British football manager

*Football Manager* (Kevin Toms, 1982) began a strand that became a genuine national phenomenon. Its descendants sold in enormous numbers, sustained a specialist press, and outlasted nearly every other 8-bit British genre — the modern *Football Manager* series is a continuous line back to a text-and-tables game written for the ZX81 and Spectrum. Few home-computer genres can claim that continuity.

## Evolution

- **1982-85:** text-and-table simulations, football foremost among them.
- **1989:** *SimCity* establishes the open-ended "software toy" and a wider audience.
- **1990s:** Bullfrog and Chris Sawyer add graphical presentation without losing the systems.
- **Onward:** the genre splits into hardcore simulation and accessible tycoon games.
