A beat 'em up is a fight you walk through. One or two players push along a street, a subway or a factory, and gangs come at them from both sides. In the 1980s arcade this was a business as well as a game: two players at once, Double Dragon's designer Yoshihisa Kishimoto recalled, was a marketing idea, because "it also allowed game centres to potentially double their incomes". The name has a history of its own too. To British magazines of the mid-1980s, a "beat-'em-up" was usually two karate fighters facing each other, and it took several years for the scrolling brawler to claim the word.

## Fast facts

- **Core idea:** fight through a scrolling stage full of enemies, with a boss at the end.
- **Usually named as the first:** Irem's *[Kung-Fu Master](/vault/games/kung-fu-master)* (1984).
- **Landmarks:** *[Renegade](/vault/games/renegade)* (1986), *[Double Dragon](/vault/games/double-dragon)* (1987), *[Final Fight](/vault/games/final-fight)* (1989), *[Streets of Rage](/vault/games/streets-of-rage)* (1991).
- **Players:** often two at once, sometimes four.
- **Close relative:** the one-on-one fighting game, from *Karate Champ* (1984) to *[Street Fighter II](/vault/games/street-fighter-ii)* (1991), which shared the name in Britain for years.

## Kung-Fu Master

*Kung-Fu Master* came from [Irem](/vault/companies/irem) in 1984. *Computer and Video Games* described it in April 1985: the hero, Thomas, climbs a temple floor by floor to rescue his girlfriend, held on the fifth, fighting off henchmen, with each floor guarded by a master of its own. It filled arcades. *Retro Gamer* remembered in 2004 that an arcade without a *Kung-Fu Master* cabinet "probably wasn't worth visiting". Later writers name it as the start of the scrolling beat 'em up: *Wireframe* wrote in 2021 that "Irem established the genre with 1984's *Kung-Fu Master*". *Computer and Video Games* was more careful in 1996, calling it "THE FIRST scrolling beat 'em up (correct us if we're wrong)".

Its hero walked left or right along a single line. Everything the genre added later, moving up and down the screen, being surrounded, picking up a weapon, came from the games that followed.

## Beat-'em-ups with one opponent

At the same time the name was being given to a different kind of game. Data East's *Karate Champ*, reviewed by *Electronic Games* in February 1985, put a fighter in white against one in red, controlled with two joysticks and "some 24 possible maneuvers", and scored blows "much the same as in actual karate competition" (see the [Data East](/vault/companies/data-east) entry). In Britain Melbourne House's *[The Way of the Exploding Fist](/vault/games/way-of-the-exploding-fist)* brought the idea to home computers in 1985, followed by *[International Karate](/vault/games/international-karate)*.

By 1987 these were what *[CRASH](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine)* meant by the word. Its October 1987 round-up of beat-'em-ups put *Exploding Fist*, "the original beat-'em-up", alongside *Yie Ar Kung-Fu*, *Uchi Mata* and *Renegade*. Robin Candy praised *Renegade*'s programmer, Mike Lamb, for escaping "the one-opponent-at-a-time format typical of this genre".

The two branches separated after Capcom's *Street Fighter II* (1991), with its eight fighters, each with different moves and strengths. *Wireframe* credited it with changing one-on-one fighting "forever"; games such as *[Mortal Kombat](/vault/games/mortal-kombat)* followed it. The fighting game became a genre of its own, and "beat 'em up" came to mean, more and more, the scrolling kind.

## Renegade: the gang

Technos Japan's *Renegade* (1986), *Nekketsu Kōha Kunio-kun* in Japan, let the gang surround the player. One thug grabbed the hero from behind while another punched him, and the hero could knock an enemy down and kneel on him. Fighters could also move up and down the screen as well as across it, so lining up a blow mattered. *Wireframe* wrote that Technos was "experimenting with more complex fighting mechanics", and the [Renegade](/vault/games/renegade) entry tells how Lamb fitted it into a 48K Spectrum.

## Double Dragon: two players and a weapon

Technos's next game set the template. *Double Dragon* (1987) put two players on the screen together, as the brothers Billy and Jimmy Lee, and ran its missions into one long scrolling journey through a city, with a boss at the end of each section. Its designer, Yoshihisa Kishimoto, had noticed during *Kunio-kun*'s development that a knocked-down enemy's weapon lay on the floor, and decided the player should be able to pick it up. Whips, bats, knives, oil drums and dynamite all became weapons, and *Wireframe* argued that almost every beat-'em-up since has had weapons to pick up. It called *Double Dragon* "the genre's first blockbuster". *CRASH*'s arcade reviewer called it "depraved, sick, bloodthirsty, violent — and I love it!"

The home computer versions struggled with it: *ZZAP!64* gave the Commodore 64 conversion 15%. The [Double Dragon](/vault/games/double-dragon) entry has the story.

## Final Fight and the 16-bit years

Capcom's *Final Fight* (Japan, December 1989) took the *Double Dragon* formula and made the sprites far larger. Players chose between three heroes who fought differently, smashed bins and crates for food and weapons, and walked in and out of the screen through a city of six areas. *The Games Machine* found it in British arcades in 1990 and called it "very impressive". Its conversion to the Super Famicom in December 1990 was, *Edge* recalled in 1997, one of the first releases for Nintendo's 16-bit console and "caused quite a stir", even without its third hero, its two-player mode and one of its levels.

"Sega responded with *Streets of Rage*," *Edge* went on. Sega's 1991 Mega Drive game kept two players, added a police car that could be called in to clear the screen, and set it all to Yuzo Koshiro's house-inspired music. *Mean Machines* gave it 90% and called it "the greatest and most enjoyable beat 'em up yet seen on the Megadrive". In the arcades Konami's *Teenage Mutant Hero Turtles*, as it was called in Britain, let four players fight side by side; in December 1990 *Computer and Video Games* and Image Works gave one of the four-player machines away as a competition prize.

## Decline

By the late 1990s the form looked worn out. *Wireframe* wrote that the side-scrolling brawler "was beginning to look decidedly staid by the end of the eighties", once *Double Dragon* and *Final Fight* had shown what it could do. In December 1997 *[Edge](/vault/magazines/edge)* reviewed Core Design's 3D *Fighting Force* and wrote that "the genre more or less died with the gradual disappearance of the 16bit market". It gave *Fighting Force* six out of ten, complaining that its players "simply fight group after group of enemies until they move on to the next stage".

## Revival

The form came back in the 2010s, largely through revivals of the old names. WayForward's *Double Dragon Neon* came out in 2012, and Arc System Works, which bought the Technos games in 2015, followed with *Double Dragon IV* (2017) and *River City Girls* (2019). *Streets of Rage 4* (2020), made by Dotemu, Lizardcube and Guard Crush Games with hand-drawn characters, returned to the series after 26 years; *Wireframe* gave it 86% and called it "a masterful, modern revival of a console classic". Its designers told the magazine they wanted to fix old frustrations, such as enemies walking off the edge of the screen, without making the game feel different from the originals.

Others followed. Tribute Games' *Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles: Shredder's Revenge* (2022) played, *Wireframe* thought, like "an alternate version of the 1991 coin-op" *Turtles in Time*. Bitmap Bureau's *Final Vendetta* (2022) moved the brawl to Britain, among red phone boxes and tube stations, and was also announced for the Neo Geo. *Wireframe*'s reviewer of *Shredder's Revenge* noted how the genre had changed at home: "With no last-coin anxiety in the comfort of your own home, the contemporary brawler's emphasis has naturally shifted from survival to performance."
