When *Edge* reviewed *Castlevania: Symphony of the Night* in November 1997, it went looking for a comparison and did not find it in Konami's earlier *Castlevania* games. "A more accurate reference point," the reviewer decided, "would be Nintendo's 16bit classic *Super Metroid* to which this latest saga pays almost religious homage. Witness the gradual opening out of the map, the uncompromising puzzles and, perhaps most significantly, the burning of copious amounts of midnight oil in playing it." Fans later joined the two names into one word. A Metroidvania is an action game set on a single connected map, where the player keeps meeting places they cannot yet reach, and each new ability opens some of them up.

## Fast facts

- **The pattern:** one large map instead of separate levels; obstacles that need a particular item or power; going back through explored areas once you have it.
- **The template:** *[Metroid](/vault/games/metroid)* (Nintendo R&D1, Famicom Disk System, 1986), refined in *[Super Metroid](/vault/games/super-metroid)* (1994).
- **The second name:** *[Castlevania: Symphony of the Night](/vault/games/symphony-of-the-night)* (Konami, PlayStation, 1997), which added experience points, equipment and spells.
- **The word:** found in a fan's Usenet post in 2001, used by 1UP.com's Scott Sharkey in 2004 and in American magazines by 2005; widely associated with, but not coined by, the journalist Jeremy Parish.
- **Other names:** "Castleroid", and "Igavania" after [Koji Igarashi](/vault/people/koji-igarashi), who worked on *Symphony* and later became the series' producer.
- **Today:** a staple of independent games, from *[Cave Story](/vault/games/cave-story)* (2004) to *[Hollow Knight](/vault/games/hollow-knight)* (2017), alongside *Metroid Dread* (2021) and Igarashi's *[Bloodstained](/vault/games/bloodstained)* (2019).

## How it works

*Metroid* is still the clearest example. Samus Aran lands on the planet Zebes with a short-range gun and no map. A gap is too low to walk into until she can curl into the morph ball; a floor will not break until she has bombs; a ledge is too high until she has the high-jump boots. Because the planet is one connected space, every new item sends the player back through places already explored, looking for the obstacles it now clears. *Nintendo Power*'s advice for one tall pipe was that some "Metroid maniacs like to use consecutive bombs to cannon ball up and over the pipe",, or players could bomb a single loose brick and roll underneath.

*Super Metroid* made the approach deliberate. Its director, [Yoshio Sakamoto](/vault/people/yoshio-sakamoto), told *Retro Gamer* that his team "really didn't want to explain things to the player using too many words. We just wanted to let them play and be able to work things out for themselves." The map was built so that the player could not move on without exploring, and would sometimes find themselves back where they had started, made to stop and think about the area.

## Before the name

*Metroid* was not the first game to open up a world as the player gained abilities. *Below the Root*, a 1984 adventure for the Apple II and Commodore 64 from Windham Classics, based on Zilpha Keatley Snyder's Green-Sky novels, let players "gradually unlock new areas in open-ended game world by collecting items and abilities", as the historian Phil Salvador describes it. But it is *Metroid* that later games copied and that the genre is named after.

Konami's first *[Castlevania](/vault/games/castlevania)* (1986) was a stage-by-stage action game. Its MSX2 version, *Vampire Killer*, already asked Simon Belmont to find a key before he could leave each stage and let him buy weapons from merchants, and *Castlevania II: Simon's Quest* (1987) replaced stages with towns, swamps and dungeons to explore. *Retro Gamer*'s history of the series says *Symphony of the Night*'s structure "echoed that of the oft-misunderstood *Simon's Quest*, with the player being free to explore each nook and cranny of Dracula's fortress at will, providing they possessed the necessary items and equipment."

## Symphony of the Night

*Symphony of the Night* came out in Japan in March 1997 and reached Europe that autumn, a 2D game at a time when, as *Edge* put it, the 2D platformer was becoming "an extremely rare breed" on the PlayStation. Its hero was not a Belmont but Alucard, Dracula's half-human son, and it brought in role-playing ideas: experience points, weapons, armour, spells and restorative items. *Edge* found the first hour "a curiously dowdy 16bit-style platformer" and some of the character design "simply laughable", but thought the later game revealed "surprisingly deep RPG-based play mechanics". *Retro Gamer* later wrote that the game became "a true classic of the 32-bit generation" and a platinum seller in America, while in Europe Konami struggled to sell even the small first shipment of PAL copies.

Koji Igarashi, who worked on *Symphony*, went on to become the producer of the whole *Castlevania* series. The 2D games that followed kept *Symphony*'s structure, on Nintendo's handhelds: *Circle of the Moon* (a Game Boy Advance launch game), *Harmony of Dissonance*, *Aria of Sorrow* and, on the DS in 2005, *Dawn of Sorrow*. "Much like in *Symphony of the Night*," Igarashi, its director, told *Electronic Gaming Monthly*, "I wanted to give players a little bit of freedom." Nintendo's own 2D series carried on in parallel on the Game Boy Advance, with Sakamoto directing *Metroid Fusion* (2002) and *Metroid: Zero Mission* (2004).

## Where the word came from

*Retro Gamer* says *Symphony of the Night* "spawned the portmanteau 'Metroidvania' as an umbrella term for all the Castlevania titles that adhere to Metroid-style gameplay", and that the game "has, over time, been branded 'Metroidvania'" because it borrowed so much from *Super Metroid*. Who first said it is harder to pin down. A search published by the historian Kate Willaert in December 2025 found the earliest known use in a Usenet post of 14 June 2001, in which a fan, Richard Hutnik, jokingly nicknamed *Circle of the Moon* "Metroidvania". The word turned up again among fans in 2003, alongside rivals: "Castleroid", which put *Castlevania* first, and later "Igavania", after Igarashi. The first journalist Willaert found using it was Scott Sharkey, reviewing a game for the website 1UP.com in June 2004. His colleague Jeremy Parish popularised it from 2006, on 1UP.com and his own website, and is often taken for its inventor; Parish has denied coining it and credits Sharkey with introducing him to the word.

By 2005 the word was in print, still in quotation marks. *Hardcore Gamer* magazine's guide to the *Castlevania* series that summer said *Circle of the Moon* "returns to the 'Metroidvania' model seen in *Symphony of the Night*", and that October *Electronic Gaming Monthly* wrote that *Dawn of Sorrow* "faithfully adheres to the established 'Metroid-vania' mold pioneered by *Symphony of the Night*".

## Independent developers take over

After *Zero Mission* in 2004 and *Castlevania: Order of Ecclesia* in 2008, *Wireframe* noted in 2019, both series "went largely silent", at least as far as 2D exploration games were concerned. Independent developers filled the gap. Daisuke "Pixel" Amaya spent about five years making *Cave Story* on his own, "an homage to *Metroid* – before such a thing was commonplace", and released it in 2004. WayForward's *Shantae: Risky's Revenge* followed in 2010 and DrinkBox's *Guacamelee!* in 2013; Tom Happ's *Axiom Verge* was, in Parish's words, the best indie Metroidvania ever made. Team Cherry's *Hollow Knight* (2017) was, for *Wireframe*, among the very best of the new wave.

By 2018 there were so many that they got in each other's way. James Petruzzi, whose *Chasm* came out that July after six years in development, told *Wireframe*: "they called it the Month of the Metroidvania because eight other Metroidvania games came out the same month that we came out. It's like, how are you supposed to succeed when you have that much competition?"

The genre's founders returned too. Igarashi left Konami, raised money from fans on Kickstarter and made *Bloodstained: Ritual of the Night* (2019), which *Wireframe* reviewed under the genre heading "Igavania" and called "a *Castlevania* game in all but name". Nintendo announced *Metroid Dread* at E3 in 2021, co-developed with MercurySteam, the first new 2D *Metroid* sequel in many years; it added stealth, with Samus hiding from robots called E.M.M.I.s. Team Cherry's long-awaited *Hollow Knight: Silksong* came out on 4 September 2025.
