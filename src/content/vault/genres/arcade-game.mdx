By the mid-1980s "arcade game" had stopped meaning only a game found in an arcade. In the British computer press it also named a way of playing: instant to start, reflex-driven, scored, and structured in lives, not chapters. *[Personal Computer Games](/vault/magazines/personal-computer-games)* printed a type or category at the head of each review, and in February 1984 it labelled [Ultimate](/vault/companies/ultimate)'s *Lunar Jetman* and Timescape's *Wild West Hero* "ARCADE", though neither had ever been a coin-op. The word described how a game behaved, not where it came from.

It started, of course, in the arcades, and the arcade sense never went away. The home sense sat alongside it, and the [arcade conversion](/vault/culture/arcade-conversion), a home version of a real coin-op machine, was a separate category judged in its own way.

## Fast facts

- **First coin-operated video games:** *Computer Space* and *Galaxy Game*, both 1971, both versions of the computer game *Spacewar!*.
- **First coin-op hit:** Atari's *[Pong](/vault/games/pong)* (1972).
- **Core loop:** immediate action, rising difficulty, a high score.
- **Session length:** short. In the arcades a game had to hold a player for the two minutes one coin bought.
- **Failure model:** a few lives, no saving, back to the start.
- **Opposite of:** the adventure game, and anything else that asked for reading and patience.

## Out of the computer lab

The first coin-operated video games were both attempts to sell *Spacewar!*, the space duel written at MIT in 1962 that ran on university minicomputers. In 1971 [Nolan Bushnell](/vault/people/nolan-bushnell) and Ted Dabney built *Computer Space*, and Nutting Associates made and sold it. "We built 1,500 and had to sell some by force," Bill Nutting told *Video Games* in 1982. Players put a coin in and stood baffled at its thrust, fire and rotate buttons. At Stanford University that same autumn, Bill Pitts and Hugh Tuck took a different route with *Galaxy Game*: a DEC PDP-11 minicomputer and its vector display in a cabinet, "a mini-computer with a coin slot" in *Retro Gamer*'s words. Later accounts date its installation at the university's student union to November 1971. It never went into production.

Which came first depends on what counts. *Electronic Games* put *Computer Space* "shortly before" *Galaxy Game* in 1982, though the same article wrongly called both Pitts and Bushnell MIT students. *Computer Space* was the first to be made and sold in numbers.

The lesson Bushnell took from it shaped the whole genre: his next game would be one people already knew how to play. Atari's *Pong* (1972) carried a single line of instructions on the cabinet, "Avoid missing ball for high score." More than 25 companies copied it. The boom that followed *[Space Invaders](/vault/games/space-invaders)* in 1978 is the subject of the [golden age of arcade games](/vault/phenomena/golden-age-arcade) entry.

## What made an arcade game

The conventions came from the coin slot. When Nintendo of America studied the arcades in 1980, David Sheff writes, it found that a successful game had to capture a player "within the first thirty seconds", and "there could be no letup in the intensity for two minutes, the time that one quarter lasted". From that followed the rest:

- **No preamble.** Play began at once, and the machine explained itself by playing a demonstration of itself between games, the [attract mode](/vault/techniques/attract-mode).
- **Score as the point.** The high-score table was the progression system, and players competed for records (see [Twin Galaxies](/vault/events/twin-galaxies)).
- **Lives as a budget.** Most games gave the player a fixed number of lives, and losing the last ended the game, however far the player had got.
- **Difficulty that ramps.** The same enemies came back faster, in greater numbers or with new tricks, until the player lost.

## On home computers

Home computer games inherited the conventions and the name. The magazines used "arcade" for original games written for home machines as readily as for conversions. *Personal Computer Games*'s February 1984 reviews put "TYPE: ARCADE" over Salamander's *Gridrunner* and *Traxx* and "CATEGORY: ARCADE" over *Wild West Hero* and *Lunar Jetman*, and "PUZZLE" over Virgin's *Lojix*. That November it described Beyond's *Mr Robot*, a Commodore 64 original, as "an arcade game with a design and play your own screen facility".

The broad label had a partner at the other end of the shelf. Virgin Games Centres advertised in *Computer & Video Games* in 1985 that they stocked "Arcade Games, Adventure Games, War Games, Classic Games, Family Games, Sports Games". Games that mixed exploration with action got a combined name: *[Sinclair User](/vault/magazines/sinclair-user)* called Ultimate's *[Sabre Wulf](/vault/games/sabre-wulf)* an "Arcade-Adventure" in August 1984, and *Personal Computer Games* gave *Underwurlde* the same label.

Arcade game was an umbrella. [Shoot-'em-ups](/vault/genres/shoot-em-up), [maze games](/vault/genres/maze-game), [platform games](/vault/genres/platformer) and most driving games sit inside it, and reviewers used the broad and the narrow terms as a sentence needed.

## Later

The 16-bit computers and consoles of the late 1980s and early 1990s brought home versions closer to the cabinets without ever quite matching them; the [arcade conversion](/vault/culture/arcade-conversion) entry follows that story. The coin-op business had its own second wind in 1991, when Capcom's *[Street Fighter II](/vault/games/street-fighter-ii)* started what Capcom called the "Street Fighter II boom".
