In its January 1995 issue *Edge* reviewed *[Doom](/vault/games/doom)* on Atari's Jaguar and named the problem every console shooter would have to solve. "As aficionados of the game will confirm, the best way to play Doom is with a mouse and keyboard. Obviously, this isn't possible with a joypad-based system." id Software's own conversion was, *Edge* judged, "acceptable, if not perfect": most moves were easy, but "you can't turn around and run away quite as quickly as you'd like." Over the next seven years console developers stopped apologising for the joypad and built games around it, with auto-aim, an analogue stick for each hand and split-screen matches for four players on one television.

## Fast facts

- **Early first-person shooting on consoles:** *Faceball 2000* (Game Boy, 1991); *Wolfenstein 3D* and *Doom* conversions from 1994.
- **Analogue stick:** *Turok: Dinosaur Hunter* and *[GoldenEye 007](/vault/games/goldeneye-007)* on the [Nintendo 64](/vault/systems/nintendo-64), 1997.
- **Two sticks:** the PlayStation's [DualShock](/vault/hardware/dualshock) (1997) and the PlayStation 2 and Xbox pads after it.
- **Landmark:** *[Halo](/vault/games/halo)*, the [Xbox](/vault/systems/microsoft-xbox)'s launch game, 2001.
- **Online:** *Halo 2* on [Xbox Live](/vault/culture/xbox-live) (2004) and *Call of Duty 4: Modern Warfare* (2007).

## Before Doom

Shooting in the first person reached Nintendo's machines before *Doom* did. Bullet-Proof Software's *Faceball 2000* for the [Game Boy](/vault/systems/game-boy) came from the makers of *MIDI Maze*, a networked maze game for the Atari ST, and put the player inside a maze hunting smiley faces called Smiloids. Its advertising promised "first-person perspective, with 3D graphics and 360° maneuvering" and play against friends through the Game Link cable or the Four Player Adapter. *Game Informer* named it the best handheld game of 1992. id Software's *Wolfenstein 3D* reached the [Super NES](/vault/systems/super-nintendo) and the Jaguar in 1994.

## Doom comes to the consoles

*Doom* was the game every console maker wanted. *Edge* reported in its review that Nintendo had bought the rights for a version of its own coded by Williams, that Sega had it for the 32X, and that the Saturn was getting *Doom II*. The Jaguar got there first, with a conversion by id itself, priced at £60. *Edge* thought it "virtually indistinguishable from the PC game", running "at about the same speed on the Jaguar as it does on a 486/50 PC", and saw one advantage over the original: "you can view it on the biggest telly you can get your hands on, rather than on a cramped 14" PC monitor".

## The analogue stick

The Nintendo 64's controller had a single analogue stick and a cluster of four C-buttons, and its shooters divided the work between them in different ways. Acclaim's *Turok: Dinosaur Hunter* (1997) put movement on the C-buttons and aiming on the stick. That layout became a reference point: a year later *N64 Magazine* described another game as using a "Turok-style set-up using the C-buttons for movement and the analogue stick for direction". *Turok* went into the British all-formats chart at number two in its week of release.

[Rare](/vault/companies/rare)'s *GoldenEye 007* followed in August 1997 and let players choose. *Edge* listed "numerous control configurations", among them "a Turok-like setting (handy for those familiar with Acclaim's title)" and a dual-analogue option "which requires players to hold a joypad in each hand - one controlling Bond's movements, the other in charge of aiming duties". It was not the most intuitive choice, *Edge* thought, but "it's worth persevering with as it offers certain advantages over the single-controller system". Holding two controllers was a clumsy fix, and it showed what players wanted: a stick for moving and a stick for looking.

*GoldenEye* also helped the player. Looking back in *Edge* in 2003, a reader wrote that the game "provided a generous amount of auto-aiming to make up for its slightly less forgiving aiming system". Its four-player split-screen mode, added late in development, became what the [GoldenEye entry](/vault/games/goldeneye-007) describes: the game groups of friends played round one television, setting their own rules.

## Two sticks

Sony put two analogue sticks on its PlayStation pad in 1997, and the [DualShock](/vault/hardware/dualshock) became the standard pad. The PlayStation 2 and the Xbox both shipped with two sticks, and by 2001 conversions were judged on how well they used them. *Edge* said of *[Half-Life](/vault/games/half-life)* on the PlayStation 2 that although "it's not as easy to play without a keyboard and mouse, the implementation of the dual analogue controls, enhanced by a handy lock-on function, ensures that console gamers will quickly come to terms with the controls".

## Halo

Bungie's *[Halo](/vault/games/halo)* launched with the Xbox in North America on 15 November 2001, and *Edge* gave it ten out of ten. Its rules suited a pad. The Master Chief carried only two weapons at a time. His shield recharged moments after he stopped taking damage, so finding cover while it recovered was, *Edge* said, vital. Four players could share one screen, and linking Xboxes raised the count to 16. "GoldenEye was the standard for multiplayer console combat," *Edge* wrote. "It has been surpassed."

The same reader who praised *GoldenEye*'s auto-aim put *Halo*'s success down to tuning, not assistance: "Halo's perfect analogue stick pitch and forgiving levels of accuracy negated what little auto-aiming was present in the game", helped by a reticle "a little wider than a few pixels, so as to forgive often sluggish analogue stick movement". The same letter complained that Free Radical Design, founded by members of the *GoldenEye* team, had left its crosshair out of *TimeSplitters 2* and cut auto-aim "to minimum use", leaving players "punching walls".

## Online

The next step was the internet. *Halo 2* (2004) took the series online through [Xbox Live](/vault/culture/xbox-live). Infinity Ward's *Call of Duty 4: Modern Warfare* (2007) moved its series from the Second World War to a war in "the near future", as *Edge* put it, with a single-player campaign of about six hours. *Edge* gave it nine out of ten and said that its "thrillingly unforgiving multiplayer", with "a compelling ranking system and stream of unlockables", made it "a truly great online experience".
