In January 1984 the American magazine *Video Games* reported that games, already called "educational toys for the computer age" and "time-wasting tools of the devil", had "earned a new label: Art". Washington's Corcoran Gallery had held an exhibition of games called ARTcade, and two Californian designers were selling cartridges in which "the goal in each is not to rack up points or kill aliens". "Most of the games on the market are variations on the same game," one of them, Jaron Lanier, said. "But John (O'Neill) and I are making games that live by their creativity rather than by a paranoid world view."

An art game is one its maker meant as a piece of expression first: a game about growing old, about a marriage, about growing up. Magazines were already using the word in 1983, and it came back in the late 2000s, when a handful of small, strange games made people argue about whether games could be art, and whether these were games at all.

## Fast facts

- **First wave:** *Alien Garden* (Epyx), *Moondust* (Creative Software, 1983) and *Lifespan* (Roklan), all on sale by the end of 1983.
- **Second wave:** Rod Humble's *The Marriage* (2007), Jason Rohrer's *Passage* (2007), Tale of Tales' *The Graveyard* (2008) and *The Path* (2009).
- **Recognition:** *Passage* was among the first 14 games the Museum of Modern Art in New York acquired, announced in November 2012.
- **Related:** [walking simulators](/vault/genres/walking-simulator), and the games some developers called "notgames".

## The first art games

*Video Games* named two. In *Lifespan*, by the English-born painter John O'Neill, the player travels "through all the stages of living from childhood through death", collecting aspects of character in childhood and then passing through "Opportunity Gates" and an "Experience Corridor" to a "Payoff", a light-and-sound display that changes with the life the player has led. In Lanier's *Moondust*, for the Commodore 64, a spacewalker drops seeds for passing ships to sweep over, and the patterns they make play music. Both games had an "art mode" with no points or challenges at all; O'Neill imagined people leaving his on the television during dinner.

Their makers were not programmers by trade. Lanier was a composer who had learned to program about three years earlier and had worked at Epyx on *Alien Garden*. O'Neill had painted in the 1960s art scene and grown tired of what he called its "self indulgence". "Video games are the best medium that's available to an artist today," he said. Lanier warned that games could prove an "electronic hula hoop" if they stayed clichéd, and that the business needed "the courage to invest in creativity".

*Alien Garden* was designed by Bernie DeKoven, who told *Electronic Fun* that he had wanted "an alien world, but not one that was necessarily hostile. A world that had some danger, but also some beauty." The player steers a Cosmic Critter through rows of crystal flowers that grow, mutate or explode when touched. Reviewers did not always know what to make of it. *The Book of Atari Software 1983* called it "an intellectual game that has merit, but seems to be one that few will ever learn to enjoy". *COMPUTE!* found *Moondust* "fascinating to play for the sheer beauty of it".

## The return of the art game

The term came back with independent developers a generation later. Rod Humble, a veteran of *The Sims* at Electronic Arts, made short experiments in his spare time; *Edge* described *The Marriage* as "an abstract poem centred on the nature of relationships". Jason Rohrer, whom *Edge* called "the ruler of the artgame development scene", made *Passage* (2007), a five-minute game in which, as *Wireframe* describes it, the player walks "along with a spouse, down a seemingly endless, empty corridor where minute changes are gradually introduced, your hair thinning, your pace slowing", to an ending everyone can predict. He followed it with *Gravitation* (2008) and *Sleep Is Death* (2010), a two-player game in which one player is the storyteller and the other the star, each with thirty seconds to respond. "What's the most interactive thing in our lives?" Rohrer asked. "The people around us, and the most interactive experience we have is conversation."

In Belgium, the artists Auriea Harvey and Michaël Samyn worked as Tale of Tales. *The Graveyard* (2008) gives the player an old woman to walk, slowly, to a bench in a cemetery; *Wireframe* called it "a walking simulator in the truest sense". *The Path* (2009) retold Little Red Riding Hood through six sisters. "It's a failure game," Samyn told *Edge* in 2007: "there's one goal and you must not do it!" The pair argued that games were "more closely related to architecture than film". Their last commercial game was *Sunset* (2015), after which, by public accounts, Tale of Tales stopped making games for sale.

## "Not games"

The new art games drew the old question. Writing in *Edge* in 2013, the designer Clint Hocking noted that *Passage* and *The Graveyard* "have both been labelled as 'not games' by a significant number of people", and that the same charge was later aimed at *Dear Esther* and at *Journey*, which had just won Game of the Year at the DICE Awards. Some developers took the word as a badge. In a 2013 *Edge* feature on how games had changed over twenty years, Adrian Chmielarz of The Astronauts chose: "The appearance of notgames." Games such as *Journey* and *Dear Esther*, he said, "have basically opened up a whole new world for us".

Museums took a side. In November 2012 the Museum of Modern Art in New York announced its first 14 video games, among them *Passage*, Jenova Chen's *flOw* and *[Katamari Damacy](/vault/games/katamari-damacy)*. *Edge* reported the reaction under the heading "Are games art? NYC's Museum Of Modern Art appears to think so".

## Since then

Personal and political games kept coming, though fewer of them used the label. *Edge* called Lucas Pope's *Papers, Please* (2013), in which the player checks identity documents at a border crossing, an "empathy game". Numinous Games' *That Dragon, Cancer* (2016) told the story of its makers' son Joel, and *Wireframe* discussed it in 2019 alongside Chen's *Flower* as a game built to convey feelings rather than challenge.
