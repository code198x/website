In a god game nobody takes orders from the player. The people in the world build, farm, breed and fight on their own, and the player changes the conditions they live in: the height of the land, the weather, the next disaster. The idea took its name from Bullfrog's *[Populous](/vault/games/populous)* in 1989. *[The One](/vault/magazines/the-one)* previewed it with Peter Molyneux's claim that Bullfrog had "just invented the eighth" kind of computer game, and a month later *[The Games Machine](/vault/magazines/the-games-machine)* headed its preview "God simulator from Electronic Arts". Within two years the label was being fixed to other people's games, and the magazines were arguing about who had really been first.

## Fast facts

- **The name:** "god sim" and "god game", in use from 1989 for *Populous*.
- **The defining game:** *[Populous](/vault/games/populous)* (Bullfrog, 1989), designed by [Peter Molyneux](/vault/people/peter-molyneux).
- **The rival claim:** *[SimCity](/vault/games/sim-city)* (Maxis, 1989), designed by [Will Wright](/vault/people/will-wright), which some magazines called the first god game.
- **Common ground:** indirect control, a population with its own behaviour, and powers bought with a resource the population produces.
- **Later examples:** *Powermonger*, *Mega-lo-Mania*, *[Dungeon Keeper](/vault/games/dungeon-keeper)*, *[Black & White](/vault/games/black-and-white)*, *Spore*, *From Dust*, *Godus*.

## Playing god

*Populous* set out the pattern. The player looks down on a small world with a rival god's people on it. The only direct tool is the ground: raising and lowering it makes flat land, and on flat land the player's followers build and multiply. Their numbers feed the god's power, which pays for everything, from raising one square of land to earthquakes, swamps, volcanoes and floods. People can be steered but not ordered: a religious icon, the Papal Magnet, draws them to a spot. *Computer Gaming World* called the god's power "manna".

The same ingredients recur in later games: a population that acts for itself, a god who works through it, and powers paid for by the population's worship or work.

## Which came first?

Games had put players in charge of whole populations before. In Mattel's *Utopia* for the Intellivision the player ruled an island and was, as *Video Games* put it in 1983, "entirely responsible for the health, education, and welfare of its inhabitants". Nobody at the time called that a god game.

The trouble for *Populous*'s claim was *[SimCity](/vault/games/sim-city)*, Maxis's city-building game of the same year, which gave the player no character either, just a city and its citizens. British magazines came to file it under the same heading. In 1992 an *[Amiga Format](/vault/magazines/amiga-format)* special called *SimCity* "one of the first and one of the best god games". In 1994 *The One*, reviewing a budget re-release, called it "the Grandaddy of all God games", adding that "its PC incarnation preceded Populous". *Amiga Format*'s buyer's guide that year described *SimCity 2000* as "the ultimate god game, now in 3D". Reviewing *Populous: The Beginning* in 1998, *Arcade* split the credit: *Populous*, "along with the first SimCity, invented the 'God sim'". In America *[Computer Gaming World](/vault/magazines/computer-gaming-world)* had already bracketed the two designers as "God-game gurus Will Wright (SimCity, SimEarth) and Peter Molyneux (Populous, Powermonger)".

*[Edge](/vault/magazines/edge)* put it more simply in 1996: *Populous* "created a whole new genre, the 'God' game".

## The label spreads

The name caught on fast. When the Megadrive and Master System versions of *Populous* were announced in 1990, *[Mean Machines](/vault/magazines/mean-machines)* called it "that amazing God-simulation" and *[Computer & Video Games](/vault/magazines/computer-and-video-games)* "the mega God-sim". The medieval strategy game *Betrayal* was advertised that year as "a hell of a god game".

Bullfrog's own sequels scaled the idea down. *Powermonger* (1990) "simulated a country – instead of a whole world – in a lot more detail", Molyneux told *Edge*, "Moving forward from that was Syndicate, which was a city." *Populous II* (1991) went the other way, with 1,000 worlds and a pantheon of Greek gods. Other developers joined in. *[ACE](/vault/magazines/ace-magazine)* found *Mega-lo-Mania* (1991) "certainly the most fun of all the god-games", and the *Amiga Format* special put the space-colony game *Utopia* "snugly into the niche of the God Game".

## Evil gods and creatures

Bullfrog turned the idea inside out in *[Dungeon Keeper](/vault/games/dungeon-keeper)* (1997). *Edge* explained in a 1996 preview that it took the role-playing game's premise, "hero raids dungeon, battles evil creatures and steals treasure", and reversed it: "the player is the dungeon keeper, the evil creatures are his minions and the heroic adventurers his enemies." Looking back in 2001, *Edge* thought it had managed "to stay true to the basic ethos of the god sim".

Molyneux left Bullfrog in 1997 and saw his next game as the end of the same road. In *[Black & White](/vault/games/black-and-white)* (Lionhead, 2001) the player uses "various – sometimes nefarious – means", as *Edge* put it, to make villagers worship. "You can be unspeakably cruel to these little people if you want to," Molyneux told *Edge* in 1998. *Edge* described its mixture as "realtime resource management, elements of RPG and action-adventure, beat 'em up, and Tamagotchi-style creature care", and *PC Gamer* gave it 94%. *Black & White 2* followed in 2005.

## Later god games

Will Wright came back to the scale of worlds with *Spore* (Maxis, 2008), in which the player guides a species from a single cell to a spacefaring civilisation. Eric Chahi's *From Dust* (2011) returned to the ground: the player moves sand, water and lava to protect a tribe, and *Edge* thought that "without the power From Dust grants players over its mutable landscape, its grand themes of godhood couldn't be conveyed".

Molyneux kept at it too. His studio 22cans raised money on Kickstarter in 2012 for *Godus*, described as a spiritual successor to *Populous*, and in April 2026 released *Masters of Albion* on early access, which he called an open-world god game.
