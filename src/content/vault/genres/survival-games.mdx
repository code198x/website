"I don't recall having ever seen a 'survival sim' before," wrote a previewer in *The One* in January 1994. The game was Silmarils' *Robinson's Requiem*, and its publisher, Daze, called it a "survival simulation/adventure". A scout is stranded on a prison planet with a survival kit and a computer strapped to his chest that reports on his body. The game calculated his fatigue, wounds, infections, sleep and the outside temperature in real time, and the monsters he met were there to provide "meat and hide". Twenty years later that list of needs (food, warmth, shelter, medicine) was the basis of a whole genre.

A **survival game** makes staying alive the point. The player starts with little or nothing, gathers food and materials from the world, makes tools and shelter, and keeps an eye on meters for hunger, health or warmth. There is often no other goal. The threats come from the world itself (night, cold, monsters) and, in online games, from other players who want what you have collected.

The phrase is older than the genre. In the early 1980s magazines used "survival game" for almost any game whose aim was simply to last. *Sinclair User* printed a reader's "quick and challenging survival game in which you must flee the pursuing ghost and also avoid contact with poisonous flowers" in April 1983, and *Video Games* described Synapse's *Salmon Run* in 1984 as "a survival game in a vertically scrolling obstacle course". Survival in those games meant dodging. In the later genre it means looking after yourself.

## Fast facts

- **Core loop:** gather, make, survive another day.
- **Precursor:** *Robinson's Requiem* (Silmarils, 1994), sold as a "survival simulation".
- **The model:** *[Minecraft](/vault/games/minecraft)* (started 2009; version 1.0 in 2011).
- **Online survival:** *DayZ* (2012), a modification for the military simulation *ARMA II*, then a separate game.
- **Single-player wilderness:** *Don't Starve* (Klei, 2013); *Subnautica*, on an alien ocean world.
- **Related:** [crafting systems](/vault/design/crafting-systems), [permadeath](/vault/design/permadeath), [survival horror](/vault/genres/survival-horror).

## Robinson's Requiem

[Silmarils](/vault/companies/silmarils)' game went further into the body than most games before or since. The hero had no health bar: his monitoring computer reported his condition instead, and the player had to treat wounds, fractures and malaria with what came to hand. Reviewing it in November 1994, *The One* praised the freedom it gave: "Every single item you pick up can be used in a variety of ways, just like in real life." It also warned that "for the first few days you play it, you'll keep dying very, very quickly indeed", and found some of the solutions obscure: a survival expert, the reviewer supposed, would know to make a hat by sewing leaves together with a needle and thread. Not every magazine was won over; the [Silmarils](/vault/companies/silmarils) entry gathers the other reviews.

## Minecraft

*[Minecraft](/vault/games/minecraft)* made the idea mainstream. Each game starts in a newly generated landscape, and *Edge* described the player arriving "as a block-headed Robinson Crusoe, lost, lonely", with nothing. Blocks are mined and placed, tools are crafted from what is mined, and night brings zombies, "forcing you to work during the day and then fortify yourself after dark". A creative mode turns the danger off, but *Edge*'s reviewer found surviving more rewarding.

## DayZ and the online survivors

*DayZ* began in 2012 as Dean Hall's modification for Bohemia Interactive's *ARMA II*. *Edge* that summer filed it as a "zombie survival MMOG" and set it beside *ZombiU* as games that had "realised that if you want to induce a bit of survivalist peril in the hearts of players, zombies by themselves just don't cut it any more". When the standalone version was shown in 2013, *Edge* explained what it added to *ARMA*'s simulation: "the need to eat food and drink water", the risk of being killed by zombies "and other players with designs on the beans in your backpack", infections caught from other players, individually broken bones, and the need to find heat to stay warm. Its world, *Edge* thought, "often feels more like a social experiment than a game".

## Don't Starve

Klei's *Don't Starve* (2013) took the idea into a hand-drawn Gothic wilderness. *Edge* called it "what you'd expect if you packed Minecraft's penchant for crafting and exploration off to the Bear Grylls school of wilderness taming". The player has "three meters to manage: your hunger, sanity and health", night falls on the first day, and "you won't make it to day two without a fire". Death is permanent unless you have found a way back. *Edge* gave it six out of ten, because once the player had learnt to survive there was little else to do: "merely surviving is mundane". It ended: "Klei may have modelled Hell brilliantly, but that doesn't mean we want to live there."

## Under the sea, and the last player standing

*Subnautica* moved the genre under water. *Wireframe* called it an "alien planet survival sim", and the artist behind the alien life of its sequel, *Subnautica: Below Zero*, told the magazine that "real science is a critical underpinning of my work".

Online survival games also produced a new kind of shooter. *Wireframe* traced the battle royale to a *Minecraft* mod, first called Hunger Games and renamed Survival Games, in which up to 48 players fought until one was left, and to mods of *DayZ* with the same rule. By 2018 *Fortnite*, *PUBG* and *Call of Duty* all had a battle royale mode; see [First-Person Shooter](/vault/genres/fps).
