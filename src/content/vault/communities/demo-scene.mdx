What began as signature screens on cracked software evolved into an art form. The demo scene brought together coders, graphicians and musicians to make **demos** — programs that generate their audio and visuals as they run, rather than replaying a recording.

That real-time constraint is the point, and it is what a demo is judged on: anyone can play a video, so the achievement is doing it live, on hardware that was never meant to. ⚠ It is a convention rather than a universal law — parties run separate categories for animations, wild entries and video, and demo rules commonly permit precalculated data — but within the demo compos themselves, generating the thing live is the whole game.

## When it got its name

The scene is older than the word for it, and this library can date the gap.

| Term | First appearance in the corpus |
|---|---|
| "demo crew" | **1987** |
| "cracking crew" | 1987 |
| "demo scene" | **1990** |
| "demoscene", one word | **1992** |

Nearly 12,000 magazine issues, and the community's own name for itself does not
appear in any of them until 1990 — seven years after the date usually given for the
phenomenon, and three years after the British press was already describing how the
groups worked.

That 1987 description, from *Commodore Disk User*'s first issue, is worth reading for
how settled it already sounds:

> Finally a demo crew usually has a musician, although many demos feature musical
> pieces hacked out of the latest software.

The roles — coder, graphician "who draws the pictures and all other graphics,
including the Crew's logos", musician — are reported as an established convention
rather than a novelty. So is the handle culture around the bulletin boards: "you
don't have to stick to your own normal, boring name but can call yourself anything
you like. This system works very well for the demo crews."

⚠ None of this dates the scene's beginning, and `emerged: 1983` is not verifiable
here. The corpus is overwhelmingly British and the scene was continental — Britain
noticed it late and named it later still. Absence in these pages measures the
attention of the British press, not the age of the thing.

## Finland's list, not UNESCO's

The demoscene is often said to have been recognised by **UNESCO**. It was not, and the real
story is more specific.

In 2020 the demoscene was added to **Finland's National Inventory of Living Heritage** — a
national register, kept by Finland under the UNESCO intangible-heritage convention. Finland
maintains the list; UNESCO has not inscribed the demoscene on its own international list.

The distinction matters in both directions. It is smaller than the usual claim, and it is
still the first time a digital culture entered a national heritage register anywhere — which
is the part worth reporting, and does not need the inflation.
