A **Homebrew Computer Club** meeting gave an unfinished project somewhere to go. A builder could announce what they were making, describe a problem or offer spare parts, then find the people who wanted to talk about it.

The club’s March 1976 newsletter names **Gordon French and Fred Moore** as the organisers of the first meeting on **5 March 1975**. It records 32 people at that gathering and attendance above 250 a year later. The figures belong to that contemporary account, not a claim about every meeting.

## Mapping, then random access

The newsletter describes a meeting in two parts. In the **mapping period**, moderated by **Lee Felsenstein**, people could make interests and requests known to the room. In the **random-access period**, they could find one another and continue the conversations.

The arrangement solved a practical problem for an informal club: a person with the answer to one question might be waiting for help with something entirely different. Public introductions made the later small-group exchanges easier to navigate. (*Newsletter*, volume 2 number 3, p. 1.)

## Newsletters carried work beyond the room

The May 1975 issue reports a San Francisco chapter interested in building equipment through mutual assistance. Its discussion ranges from salvaged hardware to an electronic community bulletin board and ways of indexing information.

This was not only a forum for polished products. The newsletter could pass on an experiment, a request or a proposal while the work was still unsettled. It also thanked people for demonstrations and recorded Felsenstein taking on the chairing role.

## A place for the Apple, among other machines

In his later Computer History Museum account, **Len Shustek** recalls the first garage meeting, the move to SLAC and builders demonstrating their machines. **Steve Wozniak** showed his Apple work within that wider gathering.

The club should not be reduced to a waiting room for one company. Its contemporary record includes hardware builders, software questions and ideas for shared access to information. The [Apple II](/vault/systems/apple-ii) belongs to that history, but so do the exchanges that did not turn into a famous product.

Homebrew provides a concrete example of the [user-group](/vault/culture/user-groups) method: people pooling knowledge and materials so another person can take the next step in making something.
