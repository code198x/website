In October 1985 *ZZAP!64* reviewed a platform game and wrote mostly about its music. "It's almost worth buying for the music alone!" one reviewer said of Gremlin Graphics' *[Monty on the Run](/vault/games/monty-on-the-run)*, and the magazine gave the composer, [Rob Hubbard](/vault/people/rob-hubbard), a feature of his own. The next spring its readers voted the *Monty* tune the best music of 1985, with 27% of the vote. Players were following composers by name, and listening to the music as well as playing the games.

The music they followed was chip music, written for the sound chip inside the machine. Twenty years later it had become a scene of its own. Fans had collected every Commodore 64 tune they could find into one archive, bought CDs of remixes and gathered in a London church to hear the old composers play, and in 2005 an American games magazine reported on musicians playing live shows on Game Boys, "from New York to Norway".

## The chips

Each machine gave its composers a different instrument, and each had tight limits.

- **The Commodore 64's [SID](/vault/hardware/sid-chip)** is a synthesiser on a chip: three voices, four waveforms, an envelope for each voice and a resonant filter.
- **The [AY-3-8910](/vault/hardware/ay-3-8910)** and its relatives, in the Spectrum 128, Amstrad CPC, MSX and Atari ST, have three square-wave channels, a noise source and a single envelope generator.
- **The 48K [ZX Spectrum](/vault/systems/sinclair-zx-spectrum)** has only a speaker that is either on or off. [Tim Follin](/vault/people/tim-follin)'s routine, printed in *Your Sinclair* in 1987, still made it play three parts at once.
- **The NES's [APU](/vault/hardware/apu)** has two pulse channels, a triangle, a noise generator and a channel for delta-modulated samples.
- **The [Game Boy](/vault/systems/game-boy)** has four sound circuits, which Nintendo's programming manual lists as two square-wave ("quadrangular") channels, one for any waveform the program stores, and a noise generator.
- **The Amiga's [Paula](/vault/hardware/paula)** works differently: its four channels play 8-bit recorded samples from memory, so any sound can become an instrument.

## Making three voices sound like more

Composers worked around the limits with a few tricks. An [arpeggio](/vault/techniques/arpeggio) cycles one voice through the notes of a chord so fast that the ear hears the chord. [Martin Galway](/vault/people/martin-galway) later said, "I believe I invented the fast arpeggiation technique for mimicking chords, first released in *Kong Strikes Back* in 1984", though other composers used the idea as well. Changing a note while it played gave slides and vibrato.

Samples were harder. The SID has no sample channel, but a program that writes values to its volume register thousands of times a second can play crude drum sounds, at a large cost in processor time. Galway's *[Arkanoid](/vault/games/arkanoid)* (1987) did it in a commercial game before most listeners knew it was possible, and [Chris Hülsbeck](/vault/people/chris-huelsbeck) had built his own sampled-drum player at Rainbow Arts. The Dutch pair Maniacs of Noise, [Jeroen Tel](/vault/people/jeroen-tel) and Charles Deenen, made samples part of their sound. Tel's favourite piece of his own, *Out Run Europa*, was a lesson in economy: "I only had two channels to work with, but I managed to make it sound like it was four."

The composers talked about the chips as instruments. At *ZZAP!64*'s round-table in 1986, [Ben Daglish](/vault/people/ben-daglish) called the question of whether the SID had been stretched to its limits "nonsensical": nobody asked that about the flute, and the SID was "something that you can play music on".

## Demos and trackers

The music also lived outside the games. In the Commodore 64 [demo scene](/vault/communities/demo-scene) a crew usually had a musician, and *Commodore Disk User* noted in 1987 that "many demos feature musical pieces hacked out of the latest software". The most admired demo it described, *Thrust Concert*, animated a band playing along to a Rob Hubbard tune.

On the Amiga, [SoundTracker](/vault/tools/soundtracker) and [ProTracker](/vault/tools/protracker) made music from samples, and the modules they saved spread through public domain libraries and archives such as [Aminet](/vault/communities/aminet). Some musicians made very small modules instead, built from short synthesised waveforms in place of recorded instruments. In 1993 *Amiga Computing* described synthesised sounds made on the Amiga's sound chip as "what we call 'chip music'". By the mid-1990s "chiptune" was the name for these small modules: in December 1995 *Amiga Shopper* reviewed a disk of 40K intros with "10 small chiptunes", and in 1996 the Italian *Amiga Magazine* found a CD-ROM that sorted its modules by size, with 512 normal modules, 21 large ones and 115 chiptunes.

## Collecting and remixing

By the late 1990s the Commodore 64 music had its own preservation project. The High Voltage SID Collection (HVSC) began in May 1996, when a scener known as The Shark set out to merge the SID collections on the internet, which were full of "bugged SIDs, repeats, and inaccurate credits", into one. The team began ripping the missing tunes from old software and tracked down composers to settle who wrote what, Peter Sandén of HVSC told *Retro Gamer* in 2004. The project aimed to hold "every single piece of music ever created for the SID chip". In 2002 *ZZAP!64*'s revival issue counted "well over 10,000 SID tunes" in it. Listeners played the files on their PCs with programs such as SIDPlay. A Californian DJ who played SID music on the radio told *GO64!* in 2000 that his show "wouldn't be possible without the High Voltage SID Collection".

Remixes followed. In September 1998 *Edge* reported that Chris Abbott had spent "two man years" on *Back in Time*, a CD of just over 70 minutes of remixed Commodore 64 music, from Hubbard's *Sanxion* and *Delta* to Galway's *Wizball* and Hülsbeck's *Great Giana Sisters*, sold at £12.99. Abbott then took the music on stage. At his C64 club night in Birmingham, reported by *Edge* in 2001, a crowd trapped Rob Hubbard in a corner with questions about *Monty on the Run*. "I live in the States," he said, and he had "absolutely no idea about what's going on with the whole thing". Still, he said, "It represents a unique time in history." At Back in Time Live in 2004, at St Luke's, a former church in London, Ben Daglish was master of ceremonies and played flute with the band Press Play on Tape.

## The Game Boy on stage

The next wave used the old hardware itself. In Germany Oliver Wittchow wrote *Nanoloop*, a sequencer and synthesiser on a Game Boy cartridge; *Lotek64* called it intuitive, "kinderleicht" (child's play), in 2003. *Little Sound DJ* was the other Game Boy program musicians named. In February 2005 *Electronic Gaming Monthly* described musicians "hotwiring their handhelds to create homebrewed 8-bit bop", and playing concerts that drew "hundreds of retro hipsters". Jason DeGroot, who performed as 6955 in Tokyo, had played live on a customised Game Boy since 1999. Jeremiah Johnson, known as Nullsleep, had co-founded 8bitpeoples, a New York collective that gave its releases away on its website, and *EGM* called micromusic.net "the European hub for chip tunes scene". Johnson did not expect to move on to the new Nintendo DS: it was "mostly sample-based and has a lot more audio channels to work with, so it's not as interesting of a music-creation platform."

By the end of 2005 *Edge* could mention "myriad other chiptune cover bands" in passing, while reporting that FamiComBand, a band that played Famicom tunes, was back on tour. According to public reference works, 8bitpeoples organised the Blip Festival in New York from 2006, which toured to Denmark, Tokyo and Melbourne before its last event, in Tokyo, in 2012.
