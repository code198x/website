In June 1987, *ZZAP!64* recommended a Compunet demo called **Man on the Moon**. Its caption divided the work among **Lamy** for the picture, [Rob Hubbard](/vault/people/rob-hubbard) for music and **Dirty Den** for code. Readers were being pointed towards work available through a telephone-connected service, rather than a game on a shop shelf.

**Compunet** gave [Commodore 64](/vault/systems/commodore-64) users a place to exchange messages and creative software. A modem connected the computer to the service; the people using its directories and communication facilities made it a meeting place as well as a source of files.

## A directory could become a gallery

The column’s descriptions show what users could find: pictures, music, scrolling messages, technical effects and combinations of them. The programs were not interchangeable examples of faster code. They also carried jokes, greetings and the names of their makers.

Rignall’s recommendations gave a reader a route through that material. A magazine could describe an effect or praise a tune, then send the owner back to the service to obtain the program. (*ZZAP!64* 26, p. 73.)

That connected two audiences. Existing users could recognise people and productions already circulating online, while readers outside those circles could discover names and work they had not encountered locally.

## Collaboration crossed the connection

A demo joining a picture, a tune and code made several kinds of contribution visible. The caption for *Man on the Moon* is a concrete example: its credits do not turn the musician into the programmer or erase the artist behind a group label.

The wider [demo scene](/vault/communities/demo-scene) also used posted disks, [bulletin boards](/vault/communities/bbs-scene) and parties. Compunet was one route within that history, not the only place C64 demos were made or shared.

## More than a list of downloads

Rignall’s column reads as a report from an inhabited service. Its recommendations and requests for further material show a relationship between contributors and readers. A file could be copied elsewhere, but the surrounding conversation helped people learn who was making it and what to look at next.

That social layer explains why Compunet belongs beside the technical histories of [raster effects](/vault/techniques/raster-effects) and sound. It helped turn experiments on individual computers into work that other people could see, discuss and build on.
