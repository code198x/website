A mod is a change to a game made by someone other than its publisher. It might be a new level, a new set of monsters, different rules, or so much new material that only the engine underneath is left, which is known as a [total conversion](/vault/communities/total-conversions). The practice is older than the word. *Edge* pointed out in 2003 that even typing in a few lines of BASIC to get extra lives on the Spectrum was "a mod by any reasonable definition of the term".

What makes modding surprising is how much of the games industry came out of it. Three of the best-known multiplayer games of the 2000s and 2010s, *Team Fortress*, *Counter-Strike* and *Dota*, began as free modifications of other companies' games, and all three ended up as [Valve](/vault/companies/valve) products.

## Fast facts

- **Early example:** *Castle Smurfenstein* (1983), a reworking of the Apple II game *Castle Wolfenstein* with Smurfs in place of its characters.
- **Breakthrough:** *[Doom](/vault/games/doom)* (1993), whose WAD files let players add levels, graphics and sounds without altering the original game.
- **Tools:** at first written by players, such as the Doom Editing Utility; later supplied by developers, such as Valve's Half-Life software development kit.
- **Famous mods:** *Aliens TC* (Doom), *Team Fortress* (Quake), *Counter-Strike* (Half-Life), *Defense of the Ancients* (Warcraft III), *DayZ* (ARMA II).
- **Today:** official tools such as Bethesda's Creation Kits and storefronts such as the Steam Workshop.

## Before Doom

Players had been making their own content since the early 1980s, usually with tools the publisher supplied. *[Lode Runner](/vault/games/lode-runner)* (1983) shipped with an editor for building and saving new screens. Bill Budge's *Pinball Construction Set* (1982) let users design tables and share them on disks, and Sensible Software's *Shoot-'Em-Up Construction Kit* (1987) let them draw sprites, make sound effects and plan attack waves "all without having to program a line of code", as *Wireframe* put it in 2022.

Mods in the stricter sense, altering a game its makers had not opened up, started with *Castle Smurfenstein*. *Wireframe* dates it to 1983 and calls it "one of – if not the – earliest mods around": it took *Castle Wolfenstein* and replaced its characters and text with Smurfs. *Edge* saw it as "a typical irreverent start to the phenomenon known as modding", a pattern of dropping a pop-culture favourite into a popular game that Doom modders would repeat a decade later.

## Doom and the WAD

The hobby grew enormously with *Doom* in December 1993. [id Software](/vault/companies/id-software) kept almost all of the game's data (maps, graphics, sounds and music) in a single archive, `DOOM.WAD`; the name stood for "Where's All the Data?". Fabien Sanglard's study of the engine explains what made this so easy to extend. A WAD is a header, a series of data blocks called *lumps*, and a directory at the end giving each lump's name, size and position. When the game looks up a lump by name, it searches the directory from the end backwards, "intentionally", Sanglard writes, so that a lump loaded later wins. A player's own WAD, added on the command line, could replace any map, sound or picture in the original while leaving `DOOM.WAD` untouched. Only the monsters' behaviour and the map names were fixed inside the program itself.

id asked for one courtesy. In May 1994, releasing the source of the program that built Doom's maps, John Carmack wrote: "If you are creating new DOOM maps for other people to use, we would appreciate it if the wadfiles you create use a PWAD identifier at the start of the file instead of the normal IWAD." IWAD marked id's own files and PWAD, for the "patch" files as id's code called them, marked everyone else's. *Edge* adds that id also asked that add-ons work only with the registered version of Doom, not the free shareware episode, and that "most modders seemed to stick to this restriction".

id did not supply a level editor. It explained the file format to a few people in the community, and within a month an "Unofficial DOOM specs" document was online. Players wrote the tools themselves, most famously Raphaël Quinet's Doom Editing Utility, DEU. Dario Casali told *PC Zone* in 1998 that he found DEU on a magazine cover disk and "spent the whole night and the next day on the computer, just moving barrels and monsters about".

By 1995 fan levels were being sold in bulk. *Computer Gaming World* reviewed WizardWorks' *D!Zone*, a $29.95 CD-ROM of 900 new levels for Doom and Doom II with editing utilities for "the sound effects, music, graphics and PWAD files". id went further in 1996 with *Final Doom*, which *Wireframe* describes as "featuring no less than two whole campaigns designed by mod teams". One of those teams was Dario and Milo Casali. id had contacted them after seeing an eight-level episode they released online. "We were commissioned to build a full 32-level game," Dario remembered, "and we had under three months to get it done." They were paid a flat rate for each level.

In December 1997 id released the Doom source code itself "for your non-profit use", in Carmack's words, and within about six days, *Amiga Format* reported, the first ports had reached the Amiga.

## Quake and QuakeC

*[Quake](/vault/games/quake)* (1996) went further. As well as new maps and graphics, it included QuakeC, a programming language for the game's rules. *PC Gamer* explained in 1997 that id had allowed plenty of customisation from the game's console, and had also "thoughtfully threw in a whole programming language with which the hardcore programmers could do exactly what they wanted", and listed the results, from *Quess* (chess played with Quake's monsters) to *Qsoccer* and a racing game, *QuakeRally*. id's level-design tools were not released at first and "proved cumbersome when they actually did appear", *Edge* recalled, so players again built their own, of which Worldcraft "proved the most popular". From this came the first Quake multiplayer mods, including *Capture the Flag* and *Team Fortress*.

## Mods that became games

**Team Fortress** began in Australia, where Robin Walker and John Cook had played a Doom map called Fortress. They made their own version for Quake, released in 1996, which gave each player a class with its own weapons and abilities, from a Field Medic to a Spy. According to Sierra's *InterAction* magazine in 1999, it was downloaded 500,000 times. Walker and Cook formed a company to support it, and Valve's Gabe Newell then "acquired their little company, its technology, and its founders, making them all part of Valve". *Edge* saw buying a mod outright as "a sign of things to come". Valve moved the game to the Half-Life engine; by Christmas 1999 *PC Zone* was describing *Team Fortress Classic* for Half-Life as a leading multiplayer game, and *Team Fortress 2* later became one of Valve's most important series.

**Counter-Strike** was the work of Minh Le, a university student, with Jess Cliffe running the website and recording many of the voices. Le told *Retro Gamer* that the first version, released in June 1999 for *[Half-Life](/vault/games/half-life)*, had one mission type, hostage rescue, two character models, about six guns and, he believed, three maps. Players supplied many of the later maps. Le and Cliffe sold the rights to Valve in 2000, and the game left beta that November. Valve encouraged others to follow: by mid-2000 *Computer Gaming World* reported that the Half-Life software development kit, previously available only under a non-disclosure agreement, was about to be released to anyone, inviting readers to start on "that COUNTERSTRIKE killer you've been developing in your head".

**Defense of the Ancients** traces back to a custom map for *[StarCraft](/vault/games/starcraft)*, *Aeon of Strife*, made in 1998. It was rebuilt as a *[Warcraft III](/vault/games/warcraft)* map by a modder called Eul in 2003, extended by Guinsoo (Steve Feak) as *DotA Allstars*, and refined from 2005 by a developer known as IceFrog. Guinsoo went on to help make *League of Legends* at Riot Games; IceFrog joined Valve in 2009 to build *Dota 2*, released in 2013. When *Edge* visited in 2011, Valve estimated that more than 20 million people played DotA, and its project lead called it "the biggest game in the world that no one has heard of".

Other mods followed the same route. *Desert Combat*, a *Battlefield 1942* mod, led to its makers, Trauma Studios, being bought by DICE, which closed the studio about a year later. Dean Hall's *DayZ*, a zombie-survival mod of *ARMA II*, had a reported 400,000 players within a few months of its release in 2012, and *Wireframe* traces the battle royale games that followed partly to mods of *DayZ*.

## A path into the industry

Modding became a way in for individuals too. Dario Casali was flown to Seattle and hired by Valve as a designer on Half-Life. In 1997 *PC Gamer* printed the testimony of players whose lives Quake had changed, among them Tom Mustaine, a designer at Hipnotic Interactive, who said Quake had let him move from "system administration" into game development. *Wireframe* followed the same path in 2018 through later examples: the team behind *XCOM: The Long War*, hired by Firaxis to make downloadable content for *XCOM 2*, and Nick Pearce, whose *Skyrim* mod *The Forgotten City* took 1,700 hours to make, was downloaded 1.8 million times and became a standalone game.

## Tools from the developers

By 2003 *Edge* judged that "user-created content has won its war for acceptance": a game that failed to release its tools was more worth remarking on in a review than one that did. Developers now shipped editors and kits: Valve's Half-Life SDK and Worldcraft, and later Bethesda's Creation Kits for *Skyrim* and *Fallout 4*. Releasing tools cost money, and not every game grew a community to use them. Harvey Smith of Ion Storm told *Edge* that the tools for *[Deus Ex](/vault/games/deus-ex)* had been "popular with 500 people. It took a ton of money and time".

Distribution changed too. Doom's levels travelled by FTP site, bulletin board and CD-ROM. Today Steam hosts a Workshop for many of the games it sells, so mods can be found through the same storefront as the game. *Wireframe* noted in 2018 that ModDB was often "the only reliable site" for older mods, "especially those from the Doom/Quake era".
