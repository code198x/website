In October 1995 *Amiga Format* tracked down the man behind Aminet and asked him how it had started. "You wouldn't believe how puny it was in the beginning," said Urban Müller, spelled Mueller in the English-language magazines. In January 1992 Aminet was one Amiga 3000 running Unix, donated by Commodore Switzerland, with 50 megabytes of disk space. Four years later it held more than 30,000 files, and the Amiga magazines reported that it had overtaken every other FTP archive on the internet, whatever the platform. It never closed. In September 2026 its front page listed 85,614 packages, and new ones were still arriving every few days.

## A student's FTP site

Aminet grew out of a small archive that a student club ran on the donated Amiga. Müller took it over in January 1992, *CU Amiga* reported, and wrote the programs that handled the short `.readme` file that accompanies every upload, still the archive's trademark. In January 1994 *AmigaWorld* told its American readers that "the hottest FTP site is in Switzerland", at the address `amiga.physik.unizh.ch`, run by Urban Mueller, "author of the popular programming utility CShell".

Müller told *Amiga Format* that he had two aims. One was simply to run the FTP site. The other was to bring together Amiga software that was scattered across "at least a dozen Amiga sites, each with its own, unique collection. Finding stuff and checking for new releases was a pain." Soon after he took over, the biggest of those sites closed, and Aminet was "totally overrun by tons of people trying to access the archive all at the same time". Friends who ran computers elsewhere agreed to keep copies, and he wrote the scripts that kept those mirrors up to date.

Space ran out next: at first the archive could keep files for only a few weeks. A donation drive among its users raised enough for a 1.8-gigabyte drive within a week, and half of the money came from the CD-ROM publisher Walnut Creek. Then the university decided that Aminet's traffic was too much and asked it to leave. It found a new home on `wuarchive.wustl.edu` at Washington University in St. Louis, and Müller went on running it as a telecommuter.

## How it worked

Everything went into one tree of directories named by subject and then by type, such as `comm/term` for terminal programs, `dev/c` for C compilers, `game/shoot` for shoot-'em-ups, `docs/mags` for disk magazines and `util/libs` for libraries. Each archive came with its `.readme`, and Müller wrote the software that mirrored the archive and generated its indexes. Asked for his all-time greats in 1995, Müller named a terminal program, a C compiler, a Mac emulator and the MUI interface library, and said they all "rival or surpass their commercial equivalents".

By then the backbone was a set of about 30 Unix computers on the internet that picked up new files several times a day. "Many BBSs, mostly in Germany, offer Aminet files as well," Müller said, and counting their operators, about 200 people helped keep the copies correct. The archive held 3,300 megabytes of compressed files in 20,000 archives, "equivalent to about 7,000 floppy disks". The main site had up to 1,000 users a day, and he guessed 10,000 a day worldwide. More than 1,000 new archives were arriving every month.

Much of that was music. In November 1995 *Amiga Computing* wrote that "there are a vast number of mods, as the tunes are called, available from public domain sources like the Aminet". Aminet's `mods` directory held the modules made with [SoundTracker](/vault/tools/soundtracker), [ProTracker](/vault/tools/protracker) and their successors.

Müller did not think people should expect to earn a living from what they uploaded. "If you want to write Shareware for the money: forget it," he told *Amiga Format*. "There are at most half a dozen Shareware programs on the Amiga that have paid their programmers a real salary." He was sure the free software mattered, though: "For no other system, PC and Mac included, is there such a huge collection of FD software."

## The Aminet CDs

CD-ROM brought Aminet to Amiga owners who had no internet connection. Walnut Creek CDROM of Concord, California, announced an Aminet CD in *Amazing Computing* in October 1993, with "thousands of Amiga programs from the Aminet Internet Archive". *Amiga Format* reviewed it in March 1994 at £19.99, holding the archive's contents up to July 1993, and called it the most wide-ranging of the CDs on its pages.

Walnut Creek published the first two discs. From the third, dated July 1994, Müller arranged the discs himself, and they were published by Stefan Ossowski's Schatztruhe in Germany and the distributor GTI. The Italian *Amiga Magazine* explained the pricing in January 1995. The disc came in two boxes with the same contents: for each copy of the "Gold" version sold, GTI passed 3 marks to Müller to buy hard disks for the growing archive, while buyers of the "Share" version were asked to send him 10 marks themselves. The disc added AmigaGuide indexes, with a button beside each program to read its description or unpack it.

The discs became a regular series. In 1995 Müller said a user survey had chosen a new CD every two months over a quarterly one, with at least 500 megabytes of new files on each and room for as much again from other sources: *Aminet CD 7* carried almost 10,000 clip-art images. He also tried to rate every mod, game and demo on each disc, "or the users have to try them all". The multi-disc *Aminet Set* collections gathered the whole archive: *Aminet Set 1*, on sale in early 1995, was four CDs holding close to 4 gigabytes, with the Fred Fish disks included. The numbered series ran past *Aminet 44* in 2001, and a limited *Aminet 50* was on sale in 2003.

## The biggest archive

*Amiga Shopper* marked 16 May 1996 as the day Aminet "became the biggest in the world". With more than 30,000 files it passed the next two largest archives, the PC collection SimTel and the Macintosh collection Info-Mac, according to c|net's Virtual Software Library. At 5,052 megabytes, only one archive on the internet was larger by size, and that one held OS/2 software. *CU Amiga* reported the same record, and counted more than 30 mirrors and five gigabytes of Amiga files.

The load told on the machines. In October 1997 *CU Amiga* reported outages because `wuarchive` had filled its 70-gigabyte disk array, which was being upgraded to 115 gigabytes. In May 1999 *Amazing Computing* reported that after "one too many crashes" Müller had moved the main archive to a server in Germany, with the Washington University site carrying on as a mirror.

## After the Amiga years

Aminet outlasted the magazines that reported on it. According to public reference works, the main mirror lost its hard disk in 2004, and a volunteer team, including Christoph Gutjahr and Nicolas Mendoza, brought the archive back under the name `aminet.net` in February 2005. The site still credits "Urban Müller and the Aminet team". Its front page lists new uploads for the Amiga every few days, and on 25 September 2026 it counted 85,614 packages.
