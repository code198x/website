A year after *[State of the Art](/vault/demos/state-of-the-art)* won The Party in Denmark, [Spaceballs](/vault/groups/spaceballs) came back to the same party with more dancers. *9 Fingers* filled two disks with figures taken from video, dancing to a techno track, and it placed fourth. Britain's public-domain buyers ranked it higher: by the spring of 1994 *9 Fingers* was top of the demo charts that libraries sent to *[Amiga Format](/vault/magazines/amiga-format)*, and one of those charts put it ahead of all three demos that had beaten it at the party. *CU Amiga* thought it "even better than the original".

## Fast facts

- **Group:** [Spaceballs](/vault/groups/spaceballs), from Norway.
- **Released:** at [The Party](/vault/events/the-party), Herning, Denmark, 27 to 29 December 1993.
- **Placing:** fourth in the Amiga demo competition, with 1,512 points.
- **Platform:** Amiga, on two floppy disks, running from one drive.
- **Credits:** code by Lone Starr and a second Spaceballs coder, with a trackloader by President Screw; graphics by TMB Designs; music by Travolta and Vinnie.

## Fourth at The Party

The Party 1993 moved to Herning, and Spaceballs were among its hosts: the organisers' results name The Silents, Lemon. and Spaceballs as the organisers. Twenty-eight Amiga demos were ranked. Complex's *Origin* won with 2,340 points, ahead of *Full Moon* by Virtual Dreams and [Fairlight](/vault/groups/fairlight) with 1,860 and [Sanity](/vault/groups/sanity)'s *[Arte](/vault/demos/arte)* with 1,592. *9 Fingers* was fourth with 1,512, only 80 points behind *Arte* and more than a thousand ahead of the fifth-placed demo. The results list it in two parts, "9 Fingers 1" and "9 Fingers 2", of about 820 KB each.

The team was much the same as the one behind *State of the Art*. Demozoo credits the code to Lone Starr and a second coder, with a trackloader by President Screw: the routine that reads the demo's data straight off the disk, without going through AmigaDOS. TMB Designs drew the graphics, and Travolta and Vinnie wrote the music. A 2015 retrospective in *The Amiga Book* says the title came from the programmer hurting a finger during development and finishing the demo with nine.

## Dancers again

*9 Fingers* went back to the idea that had made *State of the Art* famous: dancers filmed on video and turned into animation, the technique called [rotoscoping](/vault/techniques/rotoscoping). *CU Amiga*'s reviewer described stylised video animations, mostly of dancers "going loopy to a decent techno backing track", and said the coders had done "a sterling job vastly improving the graphics over the originals". *The Amiga Book* called it "the spiritual successor to State Of The Art" and said it "lacks originality, of course, but makes up for it with better visuals, including photo-real dancers." Spaceballs described how they made *State of the Art*'s dancers in their disk magazine *R.A.W.*, but public sources do not describe how the *9 Fingers* animation was stored or drawn.

## Top of the charts

In Britain, [public-domain](/vault/distribution/public-domain) libraries sold demos by post for the price of copying a disk, and *9 Fingers* sold. *CU Amiga* reviewed it in March 1994 as the follow-up to *State of the Art*: "it's even better than the original!" The review ended by calling Spaceballs "still the best demo crew". One library's advertisement in *Amiga Format* that April was blunter: "If you liked State of the Art, you'll die! SERIOUSLY GOOD!"

*Amiga Format* printed a different library's top ten demos each month, and in April 1994 Telescan Computer Services put *9 Fingers* first. In May Choice Software in Sheffield did the same, with *Full Moon* second, *Arte* fourth and *Origin* sixth. The same issue ran Steve Bradley's feature on public-domain demos. Its caption called *9 Fingers* "a lesson in how to produce a fantastic rave demo. Funky and fun", and Bradley, complaining how few demos held a viewer's attention, wrote that "few demos match the standards of State of the Art, Jesus On E's and Nine Fingers." The two-disk set was still in libraries' lists of top demos in the autumn of 1995.
