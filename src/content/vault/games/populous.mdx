In February 1989 *The One* opened its preview of a new Electronic Arts game with a joke about the number of kinds of computer game. There were probably only seven, it said, "but Bullfrog Productions' Peter Molyneux reckons that his company has just invented the eighth." The game was *Populous*. It put the player above a small, tilted world as a god, with no direct control over anyone in it. The god's only real tool was the ground itself: raise it, lower it, flatten it for building or sink it under the sea. The people did the rest.

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** [Bullfrog Productions](/vault/companies/bullfrog).
- **Publisher:** [Electronic Arts](/vault/companies/electronic-arts).
- **Released:** spring 1989 for the [Amiga](/vault/systems/commodore-amiga) and [Atari ST](/vault/systems/atari-st), at £24.95.
- **Credits:** [Peter Molyneux](/vault/people/peter-molyneux) (design and programming), Glenn Corpes (graphics), Dave Hanlon (sound).
- **Worlds:** 500 in the Conquest game, plus a custom mode with worlds generated at random.
- **Two players:** two computers joined by a serial lead or a modem.
- **Sequels:** *Populous II* (1991), *Populous: The Beginning* (1998).

## Before Populous

Bullfrog grew out of an earlier company. *The One* reported in April 1989 that Molyneux and Les Edgar had started in 1985 writing business software for the Amiga under the name Taurus, before deciding that the future lay in games. Molyneux later told *[Edge](/vault/magazines/edge)* how Taurus got its Amigas. A Commodore executive rang to say how much he looked forward to "our product" on the new machine; neither side said what the product was. Only when Commodore offered to send five Amigas did Molyneux realise the call had been meant for Torus, a maker of network cards. Taurus "desperately needed the hardware", *Edge* wrote, so he kept quiet and took the machines.

Bullfrog's first jobs were small. According to *ACE*, before *Populous* the team had made only two games: a conversion of *Druid II: Enlightenment* to the Amiga for Firebird, and *Fusion* for Electronic Arts. *The One* said *Populous* had been in development for more than six months by the start of 1989.

## A world made of Lego

The idea was tested before it was coded. *ACE* and *The One* both reported that the team built a landscape out of Lego to work out the world's dimensions and perspective, and *ACE* added that the Lego version was playable. *Computer Gaming World*'s reviewer was told it had been designed "on a set of Lego bricks in a couple of evenings".

The team was small. *The One* named Molyneux as "master programmer", Glenn Corpes as the artist and Dave Hanlon as the author of the sound. Hanlon made the crackle of fire by crumpling an empty crisp packet. The magazine was told that about 40 per cent of the team's time had gone on the computer opponent's "artificial intelligence". Most of the code was written on the ST, although the Amiga version came out first.

## Playing god

The screen shows a close-up of part of the world, drawn in an isometric view (see [isometric projection](/vault/techniques/isometric-projection)), with a map of the whole world at the top and a panel of icons for the god's powers. Each world starts with a handful of people on each side. They wander until they find flat ground and build on it, and the more flat land around a building, the bigger it can grow, from a hut up to a castle. Bigger settlements produce more people and more of the god's power, which *Computer Gaming World* called "manna". Everything the god does costs it, even raising a single square of land.

The player steers the population without commanding it. A religious icon, the Papal Magnet, draws people to a spot, and the first to reach it becomes their leader. A leader can be turned into a knight, who marches off to kill the enemy's people and burn their houses. With enough power the god can use disasters: earthquakes, swamps, volcanoes and floods. A flood drowns everyone living on low ground, so the trick, *ACE* explained, was to settle your own people high up and wait for a careless opponent to fill the lowlands. The last power, Armageddon, sends both populations to a final battle.

There was no music. *The One* described a soundtrack of "eerie low-pitched wailing and rushing wind accompanied by a dull heartbeat" that sped up and slowed down with the player's power.

The Conquest game offered 500 worlds, reached by passwords. Winning well could skip the player several worlds ahead: *Computer Gaming World*'s reviewer played about forty games and found himself on world 137. The worlds came in four kinds, each with its own colours and rules: grassland, desert, ice and a world of rock and lava. On the ice, a castle produced no more power than a tent.

## Reviews

*ACE* gave the Amiga version an ACE rating of 963 in April 1989 and called it "the biggest game of the issue and indeed the year so far", ending: "If only every Amiga game was as good!" *The One* gave it 95%, and its reviewer liked that the consequences of each decision were shown close up: "you really do feel that you're shaping the course of the game". Its verdict ended, "It's what the seventh day was invented for." *[ZZAP!](/vault/magazines/zzap-64)* made it a Sizzler; one reviewer had not seen "such an original game since The Sentinel".

In the United States, *[Computer Gaming World](/vault/magazines/computer-gaming-world)* reviewed the $49.95 Amiga version in August 1989, crediting the design to Molyneux and Corpes. It found it "as addictive as any that you'll ever play", and pointed out that for all the talk of floods and plagues, "playing Populous is more like watching a Saturday morning cartoon, than sitting through 'Platoon'." By December it stood third in the magazine's list of top games, behind *F-19 Stealth Fighter* and *SimCity*.

## Awards and sales

*Amiga Computing* called *Populous* "last year's biggest success on the computer leisure scene" in March 1990. It listed the awards: the British industry's Best Original 16-bit Game of 1989, Programmer of the Year for Bullfrog, Best Strategy Game at France's Tilt awards, and Game of the Year and Game of the Decade from the Swedish magazine *Dator*. The Golden Joysticks, reported in *ACE* that June, named it Most Original Game of the Year.

The sales figures grew in the telling. *Amiga Computing* put sales across Europe on the Amiga and ST at more than 80,000 by early 1990. *[ST Format](/vault/magazines/st-format)* later reported that *Populous* was the biggest-selling game of 1989 and had sold 46,000 copies on the ST alone. By 1992 *Amiga Format* was saying it had sold more than a million copies on all formats, while *Amiga Power* in late 1993 gave a figure of more than 750,000 worldwide. No publisher's figure appears in these reports. Molyneux told *ST Format* in 1990: "We had no idea at all it would be as successful as it was. It was terrifying."

## Data disks and conversions

The first data disk, *The Promised Lands*, came out at the end of 1989 on one disk for both the ST and the Amiga. It added five new worlds: the Wild West, Revolution Française, Blockland, Sillyland and the Bit Plains. *The One* had also reported in 1989 that an IBM PC version was being written.

The consoles followed. *ACE* reviewed the Mega Drive version in November 1990 and reported that it would be Electronic Arts' first Mega Drive game in Britain. The Super Famicom version was made by the Japanese developer Imagineer. TecMagik published a version for Sega's 8-bit Master System in 1991. The same year *Raze* reported that the PC Engine version was the first game on that console to need a card with extra RAM as well as ROM, because of the amount of information *Populous* had to keep track of.

## Sequels

*Populous II: Trials of the Olympian Gods* (1991) set the fight in Greek myth. *Amiga Power* reviewed it in December 1991: 1,000 worlds, a pantheon of rival gods, many more powers, and a custom game in which the player could set the rules. It called the original "one of computer gaming's few true original ideas" and the sequel that idea "polished until it shines".

Molyneux saw his next games as the same idea at smaller scales. He told *Edge* in 1998: "After that there was Powermonger, which simulated a country – instead of a whole world – in a lot more detail. Moving forward from that was Syndicate, which was a city." By then he had left Bullfrog, which EA had bought, to found [Lionhead](/vault/companies/lionhead), and his *[Black & White](/vault/games/black-and-white)* was the next step on the same path.

Bullfrog made the third game without him. *Populous: The Beginning* (1998) is a prequel set before the gods, in which the player directs a tribe's shaman and her followers directly. *Edge* thought Bullfrog had needed "to prove that there is life after Molyneux" and found "an effortlessly playable game", played across small round worlds whose horizons curved away as the view rotated.

The name came back once more. *Populous DS*, developed by Genki, came out for Nintendo's handheld in 2008. In 2012 Molyneux's new studio, 22cans, raised money on Kickstarter for *Godus*, which it described as a spiritual successor to *Populous*.
