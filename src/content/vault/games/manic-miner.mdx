*Manic Miner* is a 1983 single-screen platform game for the 48K [ZX Spectrum](/vault/systems/zx-spectrum), written and designed almost single-handedly by [Matthew Smith](/vault/people/matthew-smith) when he was seventeen. Players guide Miner Willy through twenty caverns, each its own self-contained puzzle: collect every flashing key on the screen, then escape through the now-unlocked exit. Air ticks down constantly. One touch from any enemy or hazard kills. Every screen is named — *Central Cavern*, *The Cold Room*, *Eugene's Lair*, *Wacky Amoebatrons*, *The Final Barrier* — and each has a distinct enemy roster, platform layout, and gimmick.

The game's other defining feature is the music. Edvard Grieg's *In the Hall of the Mountain King* plays continuously from the moment Miner Willy lands in Central Cavern to the moment he dies, runs out of air, or beats the game. Other titles played a tune on the title screen and then went quiet; *Manic Miner* refused to stop singing, and it is the game usually credited with establishing that a Spectrum could hold a melody under gameplay at all.

## What the press said before it shipped

*Computer and Video Games* carried *Manic Miner* twice in successive months, and the two notices do not agree with each other. In **July 1983** it is "in the shops now at £5.95". In **August 1983** it is "a **sixteen screen** climbing game for the Sinclair Spectrum **due out shortly**".

Two things there are worth keeping. The magazine contradicts itself about whether the game was on sale, which is ordinary for pre-release trade copy and a caution against treating any single "out now" as a release date. And the game the August notice describes has **sixteen** screens, where the one that shipped has twenty — see below. Whether the caverns were still being written or the pre-release information was simply wrong, this library cannot say.

The review that settles the specification is *Personal Computer News* on **17 August 1983**, whose information box gives 48K Spectrum, £5.95, cassette, machine code, "Other versions None", and the publisher as **Bug-Byte, Mulberry House, Canning Place, Liverpool**.

## Origins

Smith wrote *Manic Miner* in his bedroom in Wallasey on the Wirral, Merseyside.
He was a self-taught programmer who had been doing small Spectrum work since the
platform launched in 1982, and [Bug-Byte](/vault/companies/bug-byte) had already
published one game of his — *Styx*, reviewed in the same issue of *Personal
Computer News* as *Manic Miner* and found wanting: "once you've done it a few
times there's nothing much left."

⚠ **The usual November 1983 release date is too late.** *Personal Computer News*
carried a full review in its issue for the week of **11-17 August 1983**, priced
£5.95, publisher given as Bug-Byte of Mulberry House, Canning Place, Liverpool.
The game was on sale and being reviewed by mid-August. By the December
1983/January 1984 *Personal Computer Games* it was at number 10 on the MRIB
retail chart, and *PCG* was reporting that "within a few weeks of release, *Manic
Miner* was the best-selling computer game in the country".

Smith then fell out with Bug-Byte over royalties and left to found
[Software Projects](/vault/companies/software-projects) with **Alan Maton**,
Bug-Byte's marketing manager. ⚠ Maton is often described as Bug-Byte's owner or
managing director; he was neither. *Personal Computer Games* described his rise
at the company as going "from despatch rider to marketing manager in a matter of
months", and Bug-Byte's founders, Tony Baden and Tony Milner, both stayed.

*Manic Miner*'s distribution moved with Smith. By CRASH's reader-voted Hotline
chart for **March 1984** it was at **number one**, up from 23 the month before,
and already credited to Software Projects rather than Bug-Byte.

## The twenty caverns

Each cavern is a single screen of platforms, conveyors, falling crumbling tiles, enemies that bounce or float in fixed patterns, keys to collect, and a final exit (the "portal") that unlocks once all keys are gathered. The screens are all visible in their entirety — no scrolling, no off-screen surprises — but their layouts are intricate. *Central Cavern* introduces the genre's vocabulary: walking, jumping, conveyor belts. *Eugene's Lair* introduces the floating green obstacle Eugene, who waits and dives. *Attack of the Mutant Telephones* puts the player among bouncing red telephones that pursue with unhinged determination. *The Final Barrier* is the twentieth and last, and gates the ending behind one more exact sequence of jumps.

The difficulty curve is brutal by modern standards but precisely-tuned: each screen teaches one new wrinkle, then the next combines that wrinkle with everything that came before. Several screens require specific routes; one — *Wacky Amoebatrons* — has an enemy pattern so tight that the player has perhaps a quarter-second window per cycle.

## The beeper music

Two tunes, and the game switches between them. The title screen plays a beeper
arrangement of Strauss's *The Blue Danube* under an animated piano keyboard whose
keys move in time — *Personal Computer News* called it "simply the best title
screen I've ever seen, with the waving palm trees of sultry Surbiton behind a
piano keyboard whose keys are moving up and down to the tune". Grieg's *In the
Hall of the Mountain King* then plays continuously through the game itself, from
the moment Willy lands in Central Cavern until he dies, suffocates or finishes.

Smith's music driver uses port `$FE` bit 4 — the beeper output — and runs from
the game's main loop, interleaved with the platform code. The music and the game
share one CPU and one speaker bit, so note durations have to survive whatever the
game logic is doing that frame. Smith also gave the player **a key to turn it
off**, which is either good manners or an admission; *PCN* noted the switch
approvingly.

### Three magazines, three composers

Here is the detail that says most about what beeper music sounded like in 1983. *Personal Computer News* heard the title tune as **Waldteufel's *The
Skater's Waltz***. *Personal Computer Games* heard it as "a bit of
**Tchaikovsky**". *PC Zone*, looking back in 2001, called it "a twisted metallic
version of the **Blue Danube**" — which is the right answer.

Three publications, three composers, one tune. A single square-wave channel
driven in gaps between game logic carries melody and nothing else: no timbre, no
harmony, no sustain, none of the cues a listener normally uses. Professional
reviewers, writing about the music approvingly, could not name it. That is worth
holding on to before treating "the Spectrum could play tunes" as a solved problem.

The achievement was real all the same. The Spectrum was considered a sound-poor
machine, and *Manic Miner* demonstrated that it could carry recognisable — if not
always identifiable — melody continuously underneath gameplay. See
[beeper music](/vault/techniques/beeper-music) for how the trick works.

## Technical achievement

Beyond the music, the game's animation is unusually fluid for 1983 Spectrum work. Miner Willy walks in a smooth four-frame cycle and jumps with believable physics. Enemies have multi-frame animations of their own. The 20 screens, all distinct, fit in 48K alongside the game code, the music driver, and the title sequence. Smith's compression and economy here became reference material — other Spectrum developers picked apart the binary to learn how he did it.

## Reception and legacy

The reviews are worth quoting accurately, because the usual summary garbles them.

*CRASH* did **not** score it. *Manic Miner* pre-dated the magazine — the first
issue appeared in February 1984, six months after the game — and it was covered
in issue 2's platform-game round-up rather than as a scored review. What that
round-up said is stronger than a number: "This is the best platform game around,
in fact it's probably the best arcade game for the Spectrum," and, on the
technical side, "it seems incredible that Bug-Byte managed to pack so much
animated detail into one 48K program".

*Personal Computer News*, first in print, gave it the highest compliment
available to a 1983 reviewer: *Manic Miner* is "original and amusing as well as
hard to master, and I haven't enjoyed a game so much since I first encountered
*Donkey Kong*." *Personal Computer Games* made it a Screen Star and closed the
piece with one word: "Superb."

Word of mouth did the rest. The twenty screens were memorisable, the game was
passed around schools, and completion-time records circulated.

⚠ The price is £5.95 in *PCN*, in *CRASH*, and in contemporary mail-order
advertisements. *Your Spectrum*'s first issue lists it at £7.95, alone against
the rest; treated here as an error.

### A joke that needs the company history to land

One of the caverns is called **Eugene's Lair**, and the thing that hovers and
drops on you in it is called Eugene. *Personal Computer Games* explained the joke at the time,
in brackets, as though everyone already knew: "Eugene Evans left Bug-Byte for
Liverpool rivals, Imagine."

Evans was Bug-Byte's head programmer until January 1983, when he left with David
Lawson and Mark Butler to found [Imagine](/vault/companies/imagine-software).
Smith named a boss after him, put it in a game Bug-Byte published, and Bug-Byte
shipped it. A year later Smith walked out too, and *CRASH* told him to "get off
your Eugene's Lair and back to the surface" while readers waited for
[*Jet Set Willy*](/vault/games/jet-set-willy). The joke outlasted everyone's
employment.

## Ports and re-releases

The game was ported to the C64, BBC Micro, Amstrad CPC, MSX, Dragon 32 and Atari
8-bit, and has appeared on compilations ever since. *Personal Computer News*
recorded "other versions: none" in August 1983 — for its first few months this
was a Spectrum game and nothing else.
