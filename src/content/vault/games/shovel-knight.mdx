*Shovel Knight* looks like a lost NES game, and it is not one. The day before it came out, Yacht Club Games' David D'Angelo published a list of the ways his team had broken the console's rules. The picture is 400 pixels wide, not 256. The palette has four colours the NES could never show. Sprites have more colours than the hardware allowed, nothing flickers when too many share a line, and the backgrounds scroll in five or six layers. The music was written for a sound chip that only Japanese Famicom cartridges carried. The aim, D'Angelo wrote, was "a rose-tinted view of an 8-bit game": the NES as players remember it, with "modern conveniences" where the old games had been cruel.

## Fast facts

- **Developer and publisher:** Yacht Club Games, a studio in Los Angeles founded by former members of WayForward.
- **Funding:** Kickstarter, March to April 2013. The campaign asked for $75,000 and raised $311,502.
- **Released:** 26 June 2014, on the Nintendo 3DS, Wii U and Windows; later on the PlayStation consoles, Xbox One and Nintendo Switch.
- **Music:** Jake Kaufman, written in [FamiTracker](/vault/software/famitracker).
- **Influences:** [Mega Man](/vault/games/mega-man), [DuckTales](/vault/games/ducktales), [Castlevania](/vault/games/castlevania), *Zelda II* and [Super Mario Bros. 3](/vault/games/super-mario-bros-3).
- **Sales:** 2.65 million copies by September 2019.

## Breaking the NES

D'Angelo's article, "Breaking the NES for *Shovel Knight*", sets out what the team kept and what it changed.

| NES rule | What *Shovel Knight* did |
|---|---|
| A picture 256 pixels wide | A virtual screen of 400×240, the NES's height in a 16:9 shape, each pixel drawn 4.5 pixels square on a 1080p television, "avoiding the black bars you would see in an NES virtual console game" |
| A fixed palette, which D'Angelo counted at 54 colours | The NES palette, plus four colours of Yacht Club's own: a dark purple, a deep red, a beige for a sheepskin cloak and a light brown to give the characters more variety |
| Three colours and transparency per sprite | Four or five colours per sprite, which NES artists such as Capcom's on *Mega Man* had imitated by laying one sprite over another |
| Eight sprites on a line, then flicker | No flicker at all, but levels designed so the screen does not get crowded |
| One background layer | Five or six layers of parallax scrolling |
| The NES's five sound channels | Three more, from Konami's VRC6, an expansion chip some Famicom cartridges carried |

What the team kept was the look of the period's habits. Large bosses still fight on a black screen with no background, as they did on the NES, where "the boss is the background". The [PPU](/vault/hardware/ppu) entry explains the rules being broken, and why counts of the palette differ: Ricoh's own specification describes 52 colours. The [APU](/vault/hardware/apu) entry lists the VRC6's extra channels: two pulse waves and a sawtooth. Jake Kaufman wrote the score in FamiTracker, which exports music as NES machine code.

## Playing it

The hero is a knight whose weapon is a spade. He swings it to dig and to fight, and pressing down in mid-air turns it into a pogo stick, so that he bounces off enemies' heads. That came from Capcom's *DuckTales*, where Scrooge McDuck bounces on his cane, and *Wireframe* later called *Shovel Knight* "an homage, of sorts". The other borrowings were as plain. Stages are picked from a map like *Super Mario Bros. 3*'s, each ends with a themed boss, as in *Mega Man*, and *Nintendo Life*'s reviewer, noting the health and magic upgrades to buy, found it so close to *Zelda II* that "Much of *Shovel Knight* plays like the game *Zelda II* should have been".

The modern part is the way it handles failure. "If you *do* die, will you return to the place of your death to collect your scattered bags of riches?" *Nintendo Life* asked. "If you manage to snag them, you lose nothing... but if you die again, they're gone for good." Checkpoints can be smashed for treasure; *Wireframe* called it "a particularly cunning risk-reward mechanic", since a broken checkpoint will not catch you when you die. Yacht Club described its difficulty curve in three tiers: "each room is harder than the last until you reach a checkpoint, each stretch of rooms between checkpoints is harder than the last until you reach a new level, and each level is harder than the last until you beat the game. ... If it's right after a checkpoint, it shouldn't be too challenging, and if it's right before a checkpoint, it should feel pretty scary!"

## The Kickstarter and after

Yacht Club began the project in January 2013 and took it to Kickstarter that March, asking for $75,000. The campaign raised $311,502, more than $300,000 as *Wireframe* recalled it, and its stretch goals promised more: a battle mode, a challenge mode, and campaigns in which players could play as three of the game's bosses. Yacht Club kept those promises over five years, for free. *Plague of Shadows* made the bomb-throwing Plague Knight playable, *Specter of Torment* gave the scythe-wielding Specter Knight a campaign of wall-running and slashing, and *King of Cards* gave King Knight a shoulder bash and a card game called Joustus. The original game was renamed *Shovel of Hope*, and everything was collected as *Shovel Knight: Treasure Trove* in 2019, alongside a fighting game, *Shovel Knight Showdown*.

*Wireframe* summed up the game in 2020: "Everything in *Shovel Knight* feels so right, from the shovel-bouncing mechanics, to the difficulty level, to the secret-laden level design... The best 2D platformer of the generation? Probably, yes." By 2019 the game had sold 2.65 million copies. Spin-offs followed, *Shovel Knight Pocket Dungeon* (2021) and *Shovel Knight Dig* (2022), made with the British studio Nitrome, and Yacht Club's next game, the top-down *Mina the Hollower*, had raised more than $1.2 million on Kickstarter by late 2022.
