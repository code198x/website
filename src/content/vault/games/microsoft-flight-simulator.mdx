The first version redrew its view out of the cockpit three times a second, and you could take off and land anywhere because, as a *Computer Gaming World* writer put it, the whole world was "a gigantic parking lot". Forty years later the same name was on a program that streamed satellite pictures of the entire planet so that players could fly over their own houses. *Microsoft Flight Simulator* began as somebody else's program. Bruce Artwick, a pilot, wrote it for his small Illinois company subLOGIC, and Microsoft licensed it for the new IBM PC in 1982. It is older than Windows, and Microsoft calls it its longest-running franchise.

## Fast facts

- **Creator:** Bruce Artwick, through his company subLOGIC of Champaign, Illinois.
- **subLOGIC's FS1:** advertised in 1980 for the Apple II and TRS-80, $25 on cassette.
- **Microsoft version:** for the IBM PC, 1982; $49.95 in 1983, needing 64K and the colour graphics adapter.
- **Milestones:** version 4.0 (1989), version 5 (1993), *Flight Simulator X* (2006), *Microsoft Flight Simulator* (2020), *Microsoft Flight Simulator 2024*.
- **Developers:** subLOGIC and Artwick at first; Microsoft's own Aces Game Studio until 2009; Asobo Studio of France from 2020.

## subLOGIC's flight simulator

In March 1980 subLOGIC advertised its "FS1 Flight Simulator" in *Byte* for the Apple II and TRS-80, at $25 on cassette, as "just one application" of its graphics software. Its advertisements promised "a real-time, 3-D" view "out of the cockpit", and the program came with a war game too. *Computer Gaming World* explained in 1982 that the Apple version, *A2-FS1 Flight Simulator with British Air Ace*, pitted the player against five enemy pilots, and that reviewers had called the dogfight "difficult", "challenging" and "next to impossible". Its writer's advice was to fly due west about 100 feet above the ground and shoot the enemy planes before they could get on your tail.

subLOGIC followed it with *Flight Simulator II*, which reached the Apple II, Commodore 64 and Atari computers at $49.95. *K-Power* reported in 1984 that within months of its release it was number one on *Billboard*'s and Softsel's best-seller lists and was selling about 10,000 copies a month. "Artwick is a pilot himself," the magazine added. "That's one reason his Simulator is amazingly accurate."

## Microsoft's flight simulator

The IBM PC version came out under Microsoft's name, though shop listings in 1983 still sold it as "MICROSOFT Flight Simulator (by Sub-Logic)". *SoftSide*'s reviewer in April 1983 found a program modelled on the performance of a Cessna 182 class aircraft, with radio navigation aids that "accurately imitate flying conditions", charts of the New York, Boston, Seattle and Los Angeles areas and northern Illinois, and thirty flight modes, ten of them preset. One was the British Ace dogfight, set in Europe in 1917. He enjoyed flying round the John Hancock Building in Chicago and buzzing Seattle's Space Needle, but on a black-and-white monitor land and water looked much the same, the mountains were flat, and the program crashed on him three times. He still called it "an entertaining program for anyone who ever wanted to pilot an airplane". In September 1983 *Electronic Fun with Computers & Games* described it as "the state of the art in home simulations".

*Flight Simulator* found a second job, as a test for the IBM-compatible computers that followed the PC. A May 1984 advertisement in *Byte* told buyers to check a machine's compatibility by seeing whether it could run "Business programs like Lotus 1-2-3, VisiCalc, or WordStar. Games like Flight Simulator." By the end of 1984 the New Zealand magazine *Bits & Bytes* was calling *Lotus 1-2-3* and *Flight Simulator* "the benchmarks of IBM compatibility".

## Growing up

Each version added more of real flying. *Flight Simulator 4.0*, released in September 1989, let players adjust an aircraft's flight characteristics and design experimental ones, added a sailplane, random weather and updrafts, other aircraft in the sky and air traffic control. *Computer Gaming World* noted that owners of 3.0 were "miffed" that Microsoft offered no upgrade. In 1990 *Compute!* reckoned that almost two million people had taken to the air on their home computers, and judged 4.0's instrument panel the most complete of any simulator it tested.

Version 5, released in 1993, cost $64.95 and took players from a Cessna Skylane to a Learjet and a sailplane, with instrument panels digitised from real aircraft and scenery that grew more detailed as the plane approached. *Computer Gaming World* called the series "long-standing and best-selling". Other companies sold scenery for it: Mallard's San Francisco disk used satellite photographs as the foundation for its landscapes. Microsoft went on to *Flight Simulator 95*, *98*, *2000*, *2002* and *2004*, and *Flight Simulator X* in 2006.

## Grounded, then back

In January 2009 Microsoft closed the Aces studio that made the series, as part of a round of 1,400 job cuts. A spokeswoman told *Computerworld* that the company had "nothing to announce around Flight Simulator specifically". Microsoft tried a free program called *Microsoft Flight* in 2012, which *Retro Gamer* described as "planned as Flight Simulator's successor".

The real successor began in 2016, when Microsoft's Jorg Neumann asked the French studio Asobo to prototype a flight over Seattle built from Bing satellite map data and procedurally generated detail, and then asked whether the method could be applied to the whole planet. *Microsoft Flight Simulator* came out for Windows on 18 August 2020. Within about two weeks Microsoft counted more than a million players and called it "the simulator's biggest launch in 38 years". It reached the Xbox Series consoles on 27 July 2021, and by December 2022 more than ten million people had played it. *Microsoft Flight Simulator 2024* followed in November 2024.
