In December 1987 Sega gave Master System owners in Japan a role-playing game that looked like nothing on the Famicom. The hero was a young woman, Alis, out to avenge her brother, killed by the soldiers of the tyrant Lassic. Her quest took her through space to three planets, and underground the screen turned into a first-person view of corridors that slid smoothly past as she walked, where *Wizardry*, the computer game that inspired it, simply jumped from one still picture to the next. *Phantasy Star* was, as *Famitsu* describes it, Sega's first role-playing game. It filled a 4-megabit cartridge, at a time when Sega was squeezing its arcade conversions into one or two, and it started one of the company's longest-running series.

## Fast facts

- **Developer and publisher:** [Sega](/vault/companies/sega).
- **Released:** 20 December 1987 in Japan, for the Mark III and Master System; in America and Europe by early 1989.
- **Cartridge:** 4 megabits, with a battery to save up to five games.
- **Team:** Kotaro Hayashida (planner and scenario writer), Rieko Kodama (graphics and character design), [Yuji Naka](/vault/people/yuji-naka) (programming, including the dungeons), Naoto Ohshima among the monster artists.
- **Setting:** the three planets of the Algol star system: Palma, Motavia and Dezoris.
- **Sequels:** *Phantasy Star II* (1989), *III* (1990) and *IV* (1993) on the Mega Drive; *Phantasy Star Online* (2000) on the Dreamcast.

## Making a Sega RPG

*Dragon Quest* had made the role-playing game a Japanese sensation, and Kodama admitted its success was "very mind-blowing". But she remembered the team's motive differently: "we wanted to make an RPG for the sake of trying something new - not because it was more profitable for the company." The planner and scenario writer, Kotaro Hayashida, said the team took its ideas from American computer games played on the Apple: "Phantasy Star borrowed ideas from both Ultima and Wizardry!"

The world was science fiction and fantasy at once, with robots and spaceships alongside swords, spells and dragons. Kodama, who had joined Sega in 1984 and drew under the credit name "Phoenix Rie", said she wanted "a universe like Star Wars" in which every planet had its own cultures and customs. She drew on *Star Wars* and *Alien*, table-top games like *Dungeons & Dragons* and Norse mythology. Her one regret was a technical one: the cartridge was too small for both Japanese alphabets, so the Japanese text is all in katakana.

Naoto Ohshima, later the designer of Sonic, was a new artist on his first project. On his first day he had drawn Superman on Sega's light-pen drawing system to learn how it worked, so when Kodama wanted a muscular warrior for the party she asked him to design Odin. The rest of his time went on monsters: "my Phantasy Star experience was just drawing one monster after another."

## The dungeons and the monsters

What players remembered were the dungeons. Hayashida found the 3D mazes of *Wizardry* clunky, because the view "simply pasted a new screen without any kind of transitional effect", so that "there was often no way of telling if you'd moved at all". *Phantasy Star* animated each step instead. To save memory the dungeon walls used "a rotation trick where each of the four corners is identical, so it only took one-fourth as much memory". The programming was Naka's. Takashi Shoji, then a young Sega programmer, remembered the "smooth movement of the 3D dungeon" and Naka "developing the dungeon animation at the development booth next door".

The monsters moved too. Hayashida believed *Phantasy Star* was the first console role-playing game of its kind with animated enemies. The Master System could not show sprites that large, so the team drew most of each big monster into the background and used sprites only for the parts that moved.

## The heroine

Alis sets out alone and gathers a party on the way: Myau, a Musk Cat; Odin, the warrior; and a wizard. The Western box art shows her with her companions, against a sunlit planet. Kodama, one of the first women in Japanese game development to become well known, said later that she had not thought much about being a woman while making games, but that her games "might have been a bit different from the ones that were made by an all-male team, both in terms of story and art style".

## Reaching the West

Sega announced *Phantasy Star* for America at the Consumer Electronics Show in Chicago in June 1988, and Tonka, which then handled the Master System in America, advertised it as "Sega's newest adventure role-playing video game", with "space travel to three worlds" and a built-in memory to "save up to five separate adventures". John Sauer, the Tonka copywriter who localised it, recalled that the translation came back from Japan "in very stilted English", and that he rewrote it into copy that read well.

British magazines admired it and winced at the price. *Computer and Video Games* reviewed it in March 1989, alongside *Y's*, as one of "two excellent RPGs released this month". Its reviewer found "puzzles, battles, lots of interaction and a fair bit of depth", and thought "real RPG fans should definitely check Phantasy Star out". His "only reservation" was "its monumental price": forty pounds seemed a lot, even for a big game with a battery-backed memory. He gave it 75%. In America, *Computer Gaming World* looked back in 1990 on a game that "was really different", praising its combat system and the way characters joined the party "each bringing unusual skills or magical talents", and passed on the "accepted wisdom" that the response to it had "propped up Sega long enough for it to introduce the Genesis".

In Brazil, Sega's partner [Tectoy](/vault/companies/tectoy) translated *Phantasy Star* into Portuguese.

## The series

The sequels moved to the 16-bit [Mega Drive](/vault/systems/sega-mega-drive). *Phantasy Star II* followed in Japan on 21 March 1989, with Naka and Ohshima working together in Sega's new AM8 department; *Phantasy Star III* came next, and *Phantasy Star IV*, directed by Kodama, returned to the Algol system a thousand years after *II*. In the spring of 1994 it stood at the top of *Edge*'s Japanese Mega Drive chart. At the end of 2000 Naka's [Sonic Team](/vault/companies/sonic-team) turned the series into *Phantasy Star Online* for the Dreamcast, in which up to four players from different countries explored together over the internet. *Phantasy Star Online 2* followed in 2012 and its large update *New Genesis* in 2021. Kodama, later a producer on Sega's *Sega Ages* reissues, went back to the original game for its Nintendo Switch version.
