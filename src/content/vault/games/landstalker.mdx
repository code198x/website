Landstalker looked like no other Mega Drive game. Where most adventures of the time were seen from straight above, Climax Entertainment drew its world in isometric 3D, at 45 degrees from above, so its hero could jump between platforms at different heights and pick up and carry objects. "We pioneered the quarter view in Landstalker," Climax's Shinya Nishigaki told *Edge* in 1996, "and decided this should become our speciality." The view gave Sega an answer to Nintendo's *Zelda*. It also gave players a new way to fall off things, because on a flat screen it is hard to tell how far away a platform really is.

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** [Climax Entertainment](/vault/companies/climax-entertainment)
- **Publisher:** [Sega](/vault/companies/sega)
- **Released:** Japan, 30 October 1992, as *Landstalker: Koutei no Zaihou*; America and Europe, 1993, as *Landstalker: The Treasures of King Nole*
- **Format:** 16-megabit cartridge with battery-backed saves
- **Heroes:** Nigel, an elf treasure hunter (Ryle in Japan, Niels in Germany), and Friday, a wood nymph
- **Re-releases:** Wii Virtual Console (2007), Steam (2011), and later Sega collections and subscription services

## Climax and the quarter view

Climax was a small company. When *Edge* visited in 1996 it had about 20 staff in an office block near Shinjuku station in Tokyo, and had made just three games. Its president, Kan Naito, had worked at Enix on the *Dragon Quest* series before. *Edge* traced *Landstalker* to Naito's "own childhood fascination with miniature toys such as plastic models, trains and action men type figures": he had experimented with radio-controlled figures, then tried out ideas on his personal computer, and those tests became the basis for the game.

## The treasure of King Nole

Nigel is a treasure hunter who works for pay. The wood nymph Friday comes to him for help: she is being chased by thieves who believe she knows where King Nole hid his treasure. Sega's own magazine, *Sega Visions*, previewed the American release in the summer of 1993 with "stereo sounds, humorous dialogue and a great three-dimensional graphic perspective", and promised that along the way the player would "restore peace to a politically divided island nation".

The German magazine *Video Games* explained how it played when it reviewed the German version in February 1994. The world is built from single screens that flip as Nigel walks off the edge. Three actions, hitting, picking up and jumping, get him across the island, with no menus of icons. He talks to the people he meets for information, finds stronger swords, helmets and armour, and works through "countless dungeons" for magic items and keys. As in *A Link to the Past*, the next part of the game opens only when the current task is done. Nigel's sword charges up between fights for a more powerful blow, Friday revives him when he dies and drops the occasional hint, and the game is saved in a book at the island's churches.

## The jumping problem

Almost every review raised the same complaint. *Video Games* called the game "the Zelda for the Mega Drive", but warned that the isometric view too often made you lose track: when you had to hop from one platform to another, it was often unclear where the next platform really was, and one misstep could cost a life. The diagonal controls, it added, were not to everyone's taste. Its reviewer still thought anyone patient enough to search the island for King Nole's treasure would not be put off, and that adventure fans would be busy with it "for a long time".

Others ranked it as a benchmark. When *Edge* reviewed Square's *Secret of Mana* in January 1994, its highest praise was that it was better than *Zelda* on the SNES and "better than *Landstalker* on the Mega Drive". The isometric view kept dividing opinion years later. Reviewing the Wii Virtual Console release in 2007, *Retro Gamer* said some saw the game as "a classic RPG, others a 45-degree annoy-'em-up", admired its "wonderfully colourful characters" and "enchanting backdrops", complained of "scenery blind-spots", and gave it 88%.

## The Stalker games

Climax built on the view. *Lady Stalker*, a Super Famicom game released only in Japan, followed; *Edge* thought it fell short of its predecessor. Climax then returned to Sega's hardware with *Dark Savior* for the Saturn, released in Japan in August 1996, which, *Edge* reported, took its inspiration from *Landstalker* while using the Saturn's 3D hardware to free the camera. Nigel himself returned in Climax's 1999 Dreamcast role-playing game *Climax Landers*, released in the West as *Time Stalkers*. *The Official Dreamcast Magazine* in America picked him out among the guest characters: "Lyle, the cocky elfin thief from Landstalker".
