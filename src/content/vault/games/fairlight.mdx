Written by **[Bo Jangeborg](/vault/people/bo-jangeborg)** in Sweden, published by
[The Edge](/vault/companies/the-edge) from Covent Garden, and reviewed by *CRASH*
in **November 1985** at **£9.95** for the 48K Spectrum.

Its full title is *Fairlight: A Prelude — the Light Revealed*, billed as the first
part of "the Chronicles of the Land of Fairlight". You are **Isvar**, trapped
inside Castle Avars, and the way out is to find the Book of Light.

## Fast facts

- **Author:** Bo Jangeborg. **Publisher:** The Edge. **£9.95**, 48K, machine code.
- **Built with:** [Worldmaker and Grax](/vault/tools/worldmaker), Jangeborg's own
  room-design tools.
- **Screens:** 80. **Skill levels:** one.
- **Sequel:** *Fairlight II: A Trail of Darkness* (1986).

## The score, resolved

*CRASH* reviewed *Fairlight* in **issue 22, November 1985, pages 26-27** — a full
scored review, not the preview that appeared in issue 20 — and the rating panel
reads:

| | |
|---|---|
| Use of computer | 89% |
| **Graphics** | **97%** |
| Playability | 90% |
| Getting started | 86% |
| Addictive qualities | 91% |
| Value for money | 92% |
| **Overall** | **95%** |

So **95%**, matching ZXDB. The 96% widely quoted for *Fairlight* has no source in the
review; the 97% is for graphics alone, which is the likeliest origin of it.

The number is worth having right for a reason beyond pedantry: the gap between 97%
for graphics and 89% for use of computer is the review's actual argument, and it
is the opposite of the usual one about this game.

## Objects that have mass

*Fairlight* is in the family [Ultimate](/vault/companies/ultimate)'s
*Knight Lore* opened, and its contribution is **physical objects** — which *CRASH*
described in the plainest possible terms:

> each object has a mass and obeys the laws of physics. **Push a chair and it will
> move quite a long way; push a table and it moves less far.**

Isvar has **five pockets**. Food is light enough to carry several pieces at once;
try to pick up a barrel and he has to put everything else down first. Objects can
be stacked into ramps and climbed. A door you cannot reach is solved by finding
something to stand on — not by finding the key that the designer decided opens it.

That is a different kind of puzzle from almost everything around it in 1985. A
key-and-lock puzzle has one solution and the designer knows it. **A physics puzzle
has as many solutions as the objects allow**, and the player gets credit for the
one they found. The *CRASH* reviewer's summary of the difference from Ultimate is
worth keeping:

> Ultimate first introduced us to realistic 3D graphics but sadly not a lot of
> game was bolted on to them. Now, thanks to The Edge, that gap has been filled.

Another reviewer put it less diplomatically: *Fairlight* "knocks
[Filmation](/vault/techniques/filmation-engine) and Filmation 2 into a cocked hat.
And there's a game behind the graphics too."

## Two design details worth stealing

**Colour as a map.** *CRASH*: "Each location in the castle is **colour coded** —
which helps you keep your bearings while you explore. All the open air locations,
for instance, are blue." On a machine with
[attribute colour constraints](/vault/techniques/colour-clash) you get one ink
colour per character cell anyway, so a room is effectively monochrome whether you
plan it or not. Jangeborg made that limitation carry information: the constraint
became the navigation aid.

**The frame rate is visible and honest.** "If a location is filled with other
moving figures, Isvar slows down a bit — but in an empty room he can really
motor!" The game does not hide its own cost. It also blanks the screen for a
couple of seconds between rooms while the new location is drawn, which every
reviewer noticed and most forgave.

## Written in Sweden

Worth restating because the 8-bit years get narrated as a national story:
*Fairlight* is a Swedish programmer's game, published from Covent Garden, reviewed
as British, and scored by a magazine in Shropshire.

Jangeborg went home afterwards — to Sweden and his own company, **Xcellent
Software** — but left his tools with The Edge.

## The sequel, and the reaction

*Fairlight II: A Trail of Darkness* (1986, £9.95) used the same Worldmaker system
with the playing area doubled; on a 48K machine it loaded in two parts, and on a
128K in one.

*CRASH* was notably cooler about it, and about the original in hindsight. One
reviewer in January 1987: "*Fairlight* only impressed me and many other people
because of its extremely detailed graphics but the game is much too hard for the
basic Spectrum owner." By April 1987 the magazine's line was blunter still: "THE
EDGE have been disappointing lately, *Fairlight II* was pretty but unplayable."

Set that beside the November 1985 verdict — "a stunning game, achieved with a new
programming technique" — and you have the whole arc of a technique-led studio in
eighteen months.
