"It is pitch black. You are likely to be eaten by a grue." The line is still in the *Zork I* source code, in the routine that describes a room, and it exists because players complained. In the first version of *Zork*, wandering in the dark meant falling into a bottomless pit, and players pointed out that a bottomless pit in an attic ought to be visible from the ground floor. Dave Lebling invented the grue instead. *Zork* was built that way, by a handful of programmers at MIT answering the people who played it, and the result was the adventure that turned into [Infocom](/vault/companies/infocom).

## Fast facts

- **Authors:** Tim Anderson, [Marc Blank](/vault/people/marc-blank), Bruce Daniels and [Dave Lebling](/vault/people/dave-lebling), of the Dynamic Modelling Group at MIT's Laboratory for Computer Science.
- **Original:** a DEC PDP-10 mainframe game written in MDL, begun in 1977 and updated until January 1981.
- **Commercial games:** *Zork I* (1980), *Zork II* (1981) and *Zork III* (1982), published first by Personal Software and then by Infocom.
- **Technology:** the Zork-language virtual machine, now called the [Z-machine](/vault/techniques/z-machine).
- **Sales:** about a quarter of a million copies of *Zork I* by early 1984.
- **Source code:** the 1977 MDL source published by MIT Libraries; the Infocom source of the trilogy on GitHub since 2019, and under the MIT licence since 2025.

## A game to beat Adventure

In the spring of 1977 the players at MIT's Dynamic Modelling Group had finished *[Adventure](/vault/games/colossal-cave-adventure)*. "By late May, ADVENTURE had been solved, and various DM'ers were looking for ways to have fun," Tim Anderson wrote in Infocom's newsletter, *The New Zork Times*, in 1985. Marc Blank was on a break from medical school, Anderson had just finished his master's degree and Bruce Daniels was bored with his PhD topic.

Dave Lebling had already written a command parser in MDL, a LISP-like language developed at MIT, that was "almost as smart as ADVENTURE's". Anderson and Blank used it for a four-room prototype. Then Anderson, Blank and Daniels sat down to write a real game while Lebling was away, and by the time he got back it had the thief, the cyclops, the troll, the reservoir and dam, the house, the maze and more. Files from June and July 1977 survive on the laboratory's backup tapes. "Zork" was a nonsense word used around the laboratory, usually as a verb, as in "zork the fweep".

Nobody announced the game. People logged in to the group's PDP-10, saw someone running a program called ZORK and asked about it. By mid-1978, *Electronic Games* wrote later, students were dialling in to MIT from terminals across the United States to play it. For a while the authors called it *Dungeon*, until "a certain company (which shall remain nameless)" claimed trademark rights to the name, in Anderson's words, and they went back to *Zork*. A programmer at DEC translated the game into FORTRAN in early 1978, and that version, still called *Dungeon*, spread to other computers.

The authors kept adding to it. Lebling gave fights the "full complexity of D&D-style fighting", with wounds, unconsciousness and different strengths for different weapons, and gave the thief his stiletto. By the end the game held about a megabyte, and it grew, Lebling wrote in *BYTE* in December 1980, "to strain even the megabyte address space of that machine". The last mainframe update was made in January 1981.

## Sentences, not two words

Most adventures of the time understood two words, a verb and a noun. *Zork* read whole sentences. Lebling's *BYTE* article gave a vocabulary of over 600 words, including 100 verbs, and an example of what the parser could handle: "Put all of the books but the green one under the rug". The parser reduced each command to a verb, a direct object and an indirect object. Lebling saw it as a trade: a clever parser let the authors write subtler puzzles, but it also made players expect the game to understand almost anything, and "only a small number of concepts can be implemented given the available space".

## Three games from one

In June 1979 three of the authors, Anderson, Blank and Lebling, joined seven colleagues from the laboratory to found Infocom, and *Zork*, already written and tested, became its first product. The mainframe game would not fit a home computer with 32K of memory, so Infocom did two things. Lebling drew a boundary round a part of his map that held about 100 locations. The *Zork I* source that survives defines 110 rooms. The rest was held back for sequels, with new puzzles added to fill the space. The game was then compiled for a virtual machine. Every computer needed only a small interpreter, and once that existed the game ran unchanged; in December 1980 there were interpreters for the Apple II, TRS-80, PDP-10 and PDP-11. In the virtual machine's instruction set, moving an object from one room to another took three bytes.

Personal Software, the publisher of VisiCalc, sold *Zork I: The Great Underground Empire* on floppy disk for the Apple II and TRS-80 at $39.95. *Retro Gamer* says a PDP-11 version came first, in November 1980, followed a month later by the TRS-80 version, and that Personal Software sold 1,500 TRS-80 and 6,000 Apple II copies. Jerry Pournelle, reviewing it in *BYTE* that December, admitted he had "wasted incredible amounts of time playing the original Zork on a PDP-10". Infocom disliked Personal Software's box, which showed a warrior hacking away, bought back the unsold stock and from October 1981 sold the game itself, with *Zork II* a month later.

Each part had its own villain. In *Zork I* a thief stalks the underground ruins, stealing the treasures you are trying to collect. In *Zork II* the Wizard of Frobozz appears at random and casts spells at you. *Zork III*, advertised in November 1982, ends with a meeting with the Dungeon Master himself, and *Electronic Games* found it "by far the most difficult to master".

## Hits and hint books

The trilogy sold for years. In a *Computer Gaming World* list of top sellers compiled from publishers' figures up to 30 June 1982, *Zork I* was second with 32,000 copies. Infocom's advertisements that year boasted that *Softalk*'s readers had voted *Zork I* and *II* the most popular adventures of 1981. In March 1984 *Electronic Games* reported that *Zork I* alone had sold about a quarter of a million copies, when 100,000 counted as a "superhit". *Retro Gamer* says that at the end of 1983 the three-year-old *Zork I* was still at the top of the Softsel chart, ahead of *Lode Runner* and *Ultima III*. By 1987 Infocom's advertisements claimed "nearly one million" copies of the trilogy.

Stuck players wrote in. Mike Dornbrook, an MIT friend of the authors and Infocom's first play-tester, answered them, then set up the Zork Users Group to sell maps and hints, charging $2 a question. In 1982, when the letters became too many, a friend suggested hints printed in invisible ink that a special pen would reveal one at a time. They became Infocom's InvisiClues.

## Later Zorks

The name outlived Infocom's text games. Infocom added role-playing statistics in *Beyond Zork* (1987) and pictures in *Zork Zero* (1988). After [Activision](/vault/companies/activision) closed Infocom's studio, it turned *Zork* into graphical adventures with *Return to Zork* (1993), *Zork Nemesis* (1996) and *Zork: Grand Inquisitor* (1997). *PC Gamer* reported in 1996 that *Return to Zork* had sold more than a million copies.

## The source code

*Zork*'s code is public at both ends of its history. MIT Libraries' Department of Distinctive Collections has published the MDL source of 1977 and 1978 versions of the mainframe game, recovered from the Laboratory for Computer Science's backup tapes, and releases them under the MIT No Attribution licence as far as MIT holds rights. In April 2019 the archivist Jason Scott posted the source code of Infocom's games, written in Infocom's own Zork Implementation Language, to GitHub. On 20 November 2025 Microsoft, which by then owned Activision, released the source of *Zork I*, *II* and *III* under the MIT licence through those same repositories.
