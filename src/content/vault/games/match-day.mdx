## The press could not agree

*Match Day* produced one of the widest splits of the period. *CRASH* gave it **86%** in February 1985; *Sinclair Programs* gave it **50%** the same month. *Sinclair User* landed at 6/10, *Your Computer* 4/5, *Personal Computer News* 7/10.

That spread is the interesting thing about it. Football games were judged against what readers already played in the playground, not against other software, and reviewers disagreed sharply about whether the two-player game carried the single-player one.

⭐ **Look at the first game's shape.** It enters at #9, drifts, and then climbs to #3 four months later and keeps returning there for a year. That is not how a hyped release behaves — a licence enters high and decays, as [*Cobra*](/vault/games/cobra) does. This is **word of mouth**: a game that sells because people who own it tell people who do not.

And then the sequel does what sequels almost never do here. Where [*Doomdark's Revenge*](/vault/games/doomdarks-revenge) peaks below its original, *Match Day II* goes to **number one twice** and holds the top three for seven months.

## Why the sequel worked

The difference is what the two sequels did with their audience.

*Doomdark's Revenge* got harder, narrowing to the people who had mastered the first. *Match Day II* got **better at the thing people already did with it** — two players, one room, an argument. A football game's audience is not primarily solitary, so improving the two-player experience widens the audience instead of narrowing it.

That is the general principle worth taking from the pair: **a sequel that deepens a solitary challenge sells to fewer people; a sequel that improves a shared one sells to more.**
