*Morrowind* begins with paperwork. The player arrives by boat as a released prisoner and is led to a census office, where filling in the forms is the character creation. Then the door opens. "You are set free to do as you please," *[Computer Gaming World](/vault/magazines/computer-gaming-world)*'s Ron Dulin wrote in 2002. "The world of Morrowind is your oyster." The world was an island, Vvardenfell, where people travel on the backs of giant insects, mushrooms grow as tall as houses and ash storms sweep the land. Bethesda built every part of it by hand, then gave players the tool it had built it with.

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** [Bethesda](/vault/companies/bethesda) Softworks.
- **Project leader:** [Todd Howard](/vault/people/todd-howard). **Lead designer:** Ken Rolston.
- **Released:** May 2002 for Windows PCs; June 2002 for the [Xbox](/vault/systems/microsoft-xbox). Published by Bethesda in America and by [Ubi Soft](/vault/companies/ubisoft) in Europe.
- **Setting:** the island of Vvardenfell, in the province of Morrowind, homeland of the Dunmer or dark elves.
- **Expansions:** *Tribunal* (2002), set in the city of Mournhold; *Bloodmoon* (2003), on the island of Solstheim.
- **Editor:** the Construction Set, shipped with the PC game.

## Six years to Vvardenfell

The game took a long time to arrive. *Retro Gamer*'s history of the series explains that work began after *Daggerfall* (1996) alongside two smaller *Elder Scrolls* games, but *Morrowind* was too big for the technology of the day and was put on hold in 1997. The team came back to it at the end of 1998 with a smaller map and a different method. *Daggerfall*'s world had been generated by the computer, huge and, critics said, empty. *Morrowind*'s would cover only Vvardenfell, but every part would be made by hand. *Retro Gamer* put the cost at "a staggering 100 man-years", and reported that Bethesda tripled its staff and spent a year building the editor.

Todd Howard, the project leader, told the German magazine *PC Player* in 2000 that the reaction to *Daggerfall* had praised its character system but called its world monotonous and empty. The answer was a smaller, fuller world, and an editor at its centre: early on, he said (in the magazine's German), the team decided to create "an actual role-playing operating system, instead of simply a game". *Computer Gaming World*'s preview in 2002 summed up the change in numbers: about 30 towns, against the 300 of *Daggerfall*.

## An island of insects and ash

The preview pictures showed what was different. Silt striders, "giant bugs that have been captured and are used for fast travel throughout the huge world of Morrowind, like insectoid yellow cabs". Guards in bonemold armour made of nine separate pieces. Weather with "fog, rain, and ash storms, as well as day and night cycles". Every object in the world, down to the wares on a market stall, could be picked up, moved, bought or sold. There were boats too, paid teleportation between towns, and spells such as Recall, which one *PC Gamer* reader used to return to the Mages' Guild.

The main story casts the player as the possible reincarnation of Nerevar, an ancient hero of the Dunmer, prophesied to save Morrowind from the immortal Dagoth Ur. The game did little to push players towards it. "There are so many tasks, so many people to help and hunt that you'll be hard-pressed to stick to any single goal for very long," *Computer Gaming World* found. Guilds, religious factions and political factions all had work to offer, and the player could join any combination of them or none.

There were no experience points in the usual sense. Skills improved by being used, and the character's level rose with them. *Wireframe* recalled the habit this created years later: "Need to get better at jumping? Jump everywhere, all the time, always." Players remembered the cliff racers too; *Computer Gaming World* warned that "you will learn to hate the abundant cliff racers, which swoop out of the sky with a high-pitched squeal".

## Freedom and its rough edges

Reviews were mixed. *Computer Gaming World* found the combat "a bit too basic", the characters "often generic and lifeless", the journal "an anal-retentive nightmare of confusion" and the crash bugs real. It still called *Morrowind* "the definitive open-ended RPG epic", describing moments such as "reaching the summit of a mountain and seeing the ruins of a buried city sprawled out beyond the mist below you". The British *PC Gamer* gave it 83%. *[Edge](/vault/magazines/edge)* gave it six out of ten, complaining that "despite the promise of utter freedom, play is still constrained by cumbersome menus, shoddy AI and an extremely unrefined and banal combat system", and that the world was "largely composed of different variations of brown".

Readers had their own stories. A reader's review in *PC Gamer* told how a simple errand for the Mages' Guild, to fetch herbs from a lake, turned into a walk down a volcanic cleft, a haul of clams and "unbelievably, a pearl", and a promotion to Journeyman, with five skills improving on the way "all without even looking at a statistic". "*Morrowind* is a role-player's dream," the reader concluded.

## Tribunal and Bloodmoon

Bethesda added two expansions. *Tribunal*, advertised for November 2002, sent the player away from Vvardenfell to Mournhold, a city of political trouble between a new king and the temple, with sewers full of undead. *Computer Gaming World* liked its side quests and its improved journal but found it "so confining" after the open island. *Bloodmoon* (2003) added the island of Solstheim, north-west of Vvardenfell, with its native Skaal, an empire trying to colonise it, hostile wildlife and werewolves.

## The Construction Set and the mods

The editor turned *Morrowind* into something players kept building on. *Computer Gaming World*'s review noted that it shipped with "a relatively easy-to-use editor", and that "mods and plug-ins of all varieties have already started to appear": "Too easy? Make it harder. Can't sell stuff? Give the merchants more gold." *Edge* noted that plug-ins with new features were already available on Bethesda's website.

The largest fan project began before the game was even out. Tamriel Rebuilt started in 2001 as an idea posted on the Elder Scrolls forums, by fans disappointed that the game would cover one island rather than the whole of Tamriel. It settled on building the mainland of Morrowind. Its first release came in 2006, and it was still releasing new regions in 2026. OpenMW, a free, open-source re-implementation of the game's engine, runs the original game on modern systems for players who own it; *Wireframe* marvelled in 2020 that it would even run *Morrowind* on a Nintendo Switch.
