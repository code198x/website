"This is the most astonishing console game I have ever seen!" *Nintendo Magazine System* told its readers in March 1993. *Star Fox* flew a spaceship through cities, asteroid fields and enemy battleships drawn in solid polygons, on a console built for flat sprites and scrolling backgrounds. The Super NES could not do that alone. The cartridge carried a second processor, the [Super FX](/vault/techniques/super-fx-chip), designed by the London company [Argonaut](/vault/companies/argonaut), and the game was made by Argonaut's programmers working inside Nintendo's offices in Kyoto with [Shigeru Miyamoto](/vault/people/shigeru-miyamoto)'s team. In Europe it came out as *Starwing*.

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** [Nintendo](/vault/companies/nintendo), under Shigeru Miyamoto, directed by Katsuya Eguchi, with [Argonaut Software](/vault/companies/argonaut).
- **Publisher:** Nintendo.
- **Released:** Japan, February 1993; North America, March 1993; Europe, as *Starwing*, June 1993.
- **Hardware:** Super NES, with the Super FX chip in the cartridge.
- **Price:** ¥9,800 in Japan; £49.95 in Britain.
- **Sales:** about four million copies.

## From NESGlider to Star Fox

Argonaut reached Nintendo through 3D. Its founder, [Jez San](/vault/people/jez-san), had made his name with *[Starglider](/vault/games/starglider)* in 1986, and his team got 3D graphics running on the Game Boy. "They immediately flew me to Japan to meet with them," San told *Retro Gamer* in 2006. Argonaut showed Nintendo a Nintendo Entertainment System version of *Starglider*, codenamed *NESGlider*, and moved it to the Super Famicom within a week of receiving one. "Our original idea was to do something not unlike the Super FX chip, but actually for the NES," San told *Super Play* in 1993. "This was back in 1990."

The chip became the Super FX, and the game changed on the way. Nintendo judged *NESGlider* "a bit too 'cult'", San said in 1994: "To get a 3D game to appeal to people you have to spend time introducing them to the 3D concept and easing them in gently, which is of course the whole idea behind StarFox. We limited your movement to more or less straight ahead." He told *Super Play* that Argonaut's first plan had been a world for the player to explore, but that Nintendo wanted its first Super FX game to appeal to players of all kinds.

## Who made what

The work was split between two countries. Four of Argonaut's London staff, Dylan Cuthbert, Krister Wombell, Giles Goddard and later Colin Reed, moved into a small office inside Nintendo in Kyoto, "working directly for Miyamoto", San said, while the chip and the 3D software were built in England. "We did most of the programming and all of the technology, and Nintendo did most of the design. They also did all of the characters." Nintendo's staff designed the stages, helped by a scripting system Argonaut's programmers wrote for them. San found Miyamoto's method unusual: "He doesn't like to design games in advance. He subscribes to the 'try something, then keep tuning, then try something else'" way of working.

## The game

The player flies Fox McCloud's Arwing fighter for the Star Fox team: Fox, Falco, Peppy and Slippy, animal pilots whose faces and messages appear on the radio as they fly alongside. The story sends them from their home planet, Corneria, to Venom, the base of the villain Andross. Three routes lead there, of rising difficulty, through planet surfaces, space battles, asteroid belts and the insides of enemy battleships, each ending with a large boss. The Arwing's laser could be upgraded to twin wing-mounted guns, it carried a few bombs, and its shields took hits until they ran out.

The Super FX made this possible, but the Super NES still set limits. The first chips ran at 10.74 MHz, which Nintendo's development manual described as "six times as fast as the Super NES CPU". The chip drew each frame into the cartridge's RAM, and the console then copied it to video memory. San admitted to *Super Play* that the frame rate "changes quite a lot throughout the game", while claiming the result was "faster than a 286 PC, and about on a par with a 386 PC". Before the chip, he told *Edge* in 1998, the game had run at one frame a second.

## Reception

*Nintendo Magazine System* reviewed an import copy in March 1993 and later gave the score as 96%. Its reviewers thought the game resembled Namco's arcade *Star Blade* and *Starglider*, and one of them, Angus Swan, called it "the most ambitious and accomplished project yet attempted on the Super NES - and a milestone in console 3D games". *Super Play* gave it 93%. Its preview had called the arrival of the Super FX chip "revolutionary".

The British version took some time. *Nintendo Magazine System* reported in April 1993 that the game would come out in May as *Starwing* at £39.95, then in May that the price would be £49.95 "due to rising costs". The name had to change because Ariolasoft had already published a game called *Starfox* on the Commodore 64 in 1987; *Computer & Video Games* and *Retro Gamer* both gave that reason. Nintendo later sold a Super NES pack with *Starwing* in Britain.

The game sold about four million copies, San told *Edge* in 1998. Its sequel, *Star Fox 2*, was almost complete but was not released at the time; Nintendo put it out in 2017 on the Super NES Classic Edition.

## Later games

The series moved to 3D consoles without Argonaut. *[Star Fox 64](/vault/games/star-fox-64)* (1997), called *Lylat Wars* in Europe, was followed by *Star Fox Adventures* on the GameCube, first developed by Rare as an unrelated game called *Dinosaur Planet*, and *Star Fox Assault*, made by Namco. On 25 June 2026 Nintendo released *Star Fox* for the Switch 2, a remake of *Star Fox 64* developed by Velan Studios.
