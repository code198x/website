Sega's *[Virtua Fighter](/vault/games/virtua-fighter)* gave the player three buttons: guard, punch and kick. [Tecmo](/vault/companies/tecmo)'s *Dead or Alive* kept the punch and the kick but swapped the guard for a button marked Hold. Blocking was done by pulling away from the opponent, as in a 2D fighter, and the Hold button did something else: pressed as an attack came in, it caught the blow and turned it into a counter. *Computer and Video Games* explained that its producer, Tomonobu Itagaki, wanted "to recreate the thrill of a Kung-Fu movie, where you see fighters repeatedly countering hundreds of moves, while trying to sneak in a couple of their own". In 1996 *Edge* had counted Tecmo among the arcade industry's "also-rans"; *Wireframe*'s history of the company says the game's success, "particularly on the PlayStation, helped pull Tecmo back from the financial brink".

## Fast facts

- **Developer and publisher:** [Tecmo](/vault/companies/tecmo); the series' team became known as [Team Ninja](/vault/companies/team-ninja).
- **Producer:** [Tomonobu Itagaki](/vault/people/tomonobu-itagaki), until he left Tecmo in 2008.
- **Hardware:** Sega's [Model 2](/vault/hardware/model-2) arcade board.
- **Released:** arcades, November 1996; Sega Saturn (Japan, October 1997); PlayStation (1998).
- **Main series:** six games, from *Dead or Alive* (1996) to *Dead or Alive 6* (2019).
- **Spin-off:** *Dead or Alive Xtreme Beach Volleyball* (Xbox, 2003) and its sequels.

## A licensed Sega board

*Dead or Alive* was built on Sega's own hardware. Sega licensed its Model 2 arcade board, the technology behind *Virtua Fighter 2*, to Tecmo and Jaleco, and *Edge* reported from the AOU arcade show in 1996 that this let Sega move its best work on to Model 3 and leave the competition with Namco to others: "hence Dead or Alive vs Soul Edge". *Computer and Video Games* called the result "this variation of the Virtua Fighter engine", and *Edge*, reviewing the Saturn version, noted that many of its attacks were "lifted from Sega's beat 'em up series".

## The Hold button

What made it different was the counter. "Counters may not be an innovation per se," *Edge* wrote, "but their usage as an integral part of gameplay certainly is. A battle between DOA adepts is an endless chain of feints, blocks and dodges." Depending on the attack, a Hold could become a dodge, a shove, or a quick strike that stopped the original attack. *Computer and Video Games* found it had "no Auto-Block of light-weight hits. If you don't see it coming, you'll be seeing stars."

The rest of the series built on the same idea. Team Ninja's own guide to *Dead or Alive 6* describes the fighting as a triangle: "strikes beat throws, throws beat holds, and holds beat strikes", with extra damage for the player who guesses right.

The first game had one more hazard. The edge of each ring was a Danger Zone, and a fighter knocked down on it was blown into the air by an explosion, ready to be hit again. *Edge* thought the arenas were the game's weak point and found this "a curiously unsatisfying feature".

## Saturn and PlayStation

Tecmo's own Saturn conversion came out in Japan in October 1997. *Edge* gave it eight out of ten, writing that its "hi-res, 60fps visuals will both delight and astound Saturn owners", though it found the cast of eight fighters and a boss "meagre". *Computer and Video Games* went further, saying Tecmo had proved "even more skilled than Sega at transporting Model 2 games". The PlayStation version of 1998 replaced the 3D arenas with flat backgrounds but added two fighters: Bass, "the Hulk Hogan look-a-like", and Ayane, hidden at first.

## Dreamcast and Xbox

*Dead or Alive 2* moved to Sega's next arcade board, [NAOMI](/vault/systems/sega-naomi), and on to the [Dreamcast](/vault/systems/sega-dreamcast), and was later ported, with extras, to the PlayStation 2. *Dead or Alive 3* was one of the [Xbox](/vault/systems/microsoft-xbox)'s launch games in the United States in November 2001. Itagaki told the *Official Xbox Magazine* that the tag battles of *Dead or Alive 2 Hardcore* had been "really for hardcore gamers", and that this time "we really wanted to make it easier and more accessible to casual players". Its arenas were part of the fight: a player could counter a strike and swing the opponent into a tree or a burning torch, and most levels had ground at different heights. The magazine judged its graphics better than the newest arcade *Tekken* and *Virtua Fighter*, and found that "Itagaki's claim that it was both easy and deep holds true."

## The fighters' looks

From the start, magazines commented on the women fighters' figures. *Computer and Video Games* opened its 1998 preview by saying "it isn't just the bouncing chests that keep you interested". *Dead or Alive Xtreme Beach Volleyball* (2003) took the women to an island holiday in bikinis, and *Edge*'s review asked outright: "is it intended to titillate, and does it objectify women in a potentially damaging way?" Yosuke Hayashi, who had taken over as head of Team Ninja, said in 2012 that the earlier games had "more of a comic-book feel, the proportions of the women were massive, more [like] pin-up images", and that *Dead or Alive 5* would aim for more realistic characters. *Wireframe* wrote in 2020 that the series' "puerile dedication to jiggling bits of anatomy has thankfully ebbed at least a bit in more recent years".

## Later history

Itagaki left Tecmo in 2008. *Dead or Alive 5* followed in 2012 under Hayashi, with stages that broke apart during fights, and *Dead or Alive 6*, published by Koei Tecmo in 2019, drew 62 per cent from *Wireframe*. The [Tecmo](/vault/companies/tecmo) entry follows the company through its merger with Koei.
