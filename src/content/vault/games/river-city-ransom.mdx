Most brawlers of the late 1980s marched the player from left to right until the boss. *River City Ransom* gave the player a city. Its high-school heroes could walk back the way they came, and the gang members they beat up dropped coins, which bought sushi, burgers, saunas and books that taught new fighting moves. Every thug had a name and something to say, and when one was knocked out he shouted a parting cry, quite often "BARF!". It came from [Technos Japan](/vault/companies/technos), the makers of *[Renegade](/vault/games/renegade)* and *[Double Dragon](/vault/games/double-dragon)*, and did not sell as well as either in the West. *[Retro Gamer](/vault/magazines/retro-gamer)* wrote in 2006 that it "would have been a Classic Game if only more people had played it back in the day."

## Fast facts

- **Japanese title:** *Downtown Nekketsu Monogatari* (roughly "Downtown Hot-Blooded Story"), Famicom, 1989.
- **Developer:** [Technos Japan](/vault/companies/technos); directed by Mitsuhiro Yoshida and Hiroyuki Sekimoto.
- **North America:** *River City Ransom*, NES, 1990, published by American Technos.
- **Europe:** *Street Gangs*, NES, 1991, published by Infogrames.
- **Players:** one or two at once.
- **Later versions:** PC Engine Super CD-ROM² (1993); *River City Ransom EX* for the Game Boy Advance (2004).

## Kunio goes shopping

*Downtown Nekketsu Monogatari* was part of Technos's long *Kunio-kun* series, which began with the 1986 arcade game that the West knew as *Renegade*. *Retro Gamer* called it, in effect, the *Renegade 2* that Technos made itself, since Ocean's *Target Renegade* was a British sequel by other developers. It was the series' first game made for the Famicom rather than converted from the arcades, and it drew Kunio and his classmates as small, big-headed figures in the "Super Deformed" style.

For America the script was rewritten and the students swapped their school uniforms for jeans and T-shirts. Kunio became Alex, of Cross Town High, and his friend Ryan joined him in two-player games. Ryan's girlfriend Cyndi had been kidnapped while shopping by Slick, a student at River City High, who had taken over the city with an army of gangs. The pair had to fight their way across the city to him.

## Stats, shops and gangs

The shopping made the game. Gang members dropped money when beaten, and the city's malls sold things that changed the fighters. *Retro Gamer* went round the shops: bookstores, where books (typically $26.95) taught techniques such as "Dragon Feet"; sushi bars and burger and chicken restaurants for stat boosts and stamina; drugstores; a toy shop whose dearer stock, over $50, included video games; Pop's Health Club, where $3.50 bought a sauna that raised willpower, stamina and power; and a record shop where every CD cost $9.95. Part of the pleasure, the magazine said, was not knowing what an item did until it had been bought. A player could run straight to the final fight and reach it in about ten minutes, but the later enemies were too strong to beat without first earning money and building up the fighters.

River City had nine colour-coded gangs, each with its own fighting style and dozens of named members: the cowardly Frat Guys, the slow Home Boys, the bare-knuckle Internationals, the Jocks, the Squids, the Mob and the Generic Dudes among them. Bosses guarded parts of the city, some of them hidden. Weapons lay in the streets or could be taken from enemies: wooden and metal clubs, chains, dustbins, tyres, crates, and, when nothing else was to hand, an unconscious enemy.

Two players could play at once, which *Retro Gamer* counted among the best things in it. One player could hold a crate while the other rode on it, a knocked-out partner could be picked up and used as a weapon, and a player who hoarded the dropped money might start a fight with the other.

## Street Gangs and after

In Europe Infogrames released the game in 1991 as *Street Gangs*. *Retro Gamer* found it identical to the American version apart from the title and the "crippling" borders and slowdown of the 50Hz PAL conversion, and it particularly disliked the box art.

The game went on being remade. Naxat ported it to NEC's PC Engine Super CD-ROM² in Japan in 1993, with better graphics, CD music and speech. After Technos closed, Million, the company holding its copyrights, licensed a Game Boy Advance version, and Atlus released *River City Ransom EX* in America in 2004. In 2002 an Atari employee, Armen Casarjian, found the "River City Ransom" trademark had lapsed and registered it, meaning to make a new game from scratch; he dropped the project, he said out of respect, when he found Atlus had made its own update.

In June 2015 Arc System Works bought the rights to all of Technos's games, *River City Ransom* among them. Conatus Creative's *River City Ransom: Underground* came out in 2017, and WayForward's *River City Girls* in 2019 turned the story round, with two schoolgirls as the heroes. *Wireframe* credited it with giving "a refreshing gender-flip to the long-running Japanese Kunio-kun series", and an older Japanese *Kunio* game was later released in the West as *River City Girls Zero*.

The game had at least one famous fan. Bryan Lee O'Malley, the author of the *Scott Pilgrim* comics, said in 2006 that he had put games such as *Super Mario* and *River City Ransom* into his books "because they were a huge part of the fabric of my youth". In 2025 he remembered renting it as a child and playing it all night with a friend: "It's like a totemic video game memory for me, especially co-op."
