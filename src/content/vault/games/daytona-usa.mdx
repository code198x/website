At the AOU show in Tokyo in February 1994, [Sega](/vault/companies/sega) lined up 13 linked cabinets of a new racing game, and *Edge* reported that the first reaction was that it was "better than *Ridge Racer*". *Daytona USA* was the first game on Sega's [Model 2](/vault/techniques/model-2) arcade board, which painted texture detail on to its polygons: decals on the cars, the sky reflected in their rear windows, and a cliff face carved with a giant Sonic the Hedgehog. Up to eight players could race each other on linked machines while the game's composer sang over the top.

It stayed in the arcades for years. When *Edge* met its director, Toshihiro Nagoshi, in 2000, he said operators were refusing to part with their cabinets. The home versions had a harder time: the 1995 [Saturn](/vault/systems/sega-saturn) conversion drew as much comment for its missing scenery as for its racing.

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** [Sega AM2](/vault/companies/sega-am2).
- **Director:** [Toshihiro Nagoshi](/vault/people/toshihiro-nagoshi).
- **Music:** Takenobu Mitsuyoshi.
- **Hardware:** Sega [Model 2](/vault/techniques/model-2), its first game.
- **Shown finished:** AOU show, Tokyo, February 1994.
- **Players:** up to eight, on linked cabinets.
- **Home versions:** Saturn (Japan, April 1995), *Championship Circuit Edition* for the Saturn (1996), Windows (1996), *Daytona USA 2001* for the Dreamcast, Xbox 360 and PlayStation 3 (2011).

## Building it

*Daytona* was made to launch a board. [Sega AM2](/vault/companies/sega-am2), the arcade department run by [Yu Suzuki](/vault/people/yu-suzuki), had put Sega into polygon 3D with *Virtua Racing* on Model 1. For Model 2 Sega went into partnership with General Electric in August 1992 to add texture mapping, and Suzuki told *Edge* in 1994 that the board took a year to develop and the game "a further year". Nagoshi told *Edge* in 2000 that Sega needed a launch title for Model 2, and that people had told him a racing game built around fun would not succeed.

*Edge* printed the credits with its first look at a 5%-complete demo in April 1994: "Director: T. Nagushi" (Nagoshi, misspelt) and "Music: T. Mitsuyoshi". One of the game's Japanese creators told the magazine that the game "throws 300,000 texture-mapped and shaded polygons around every second", against about 180,000 untextured ones on Model 1. Suzuki had wanted more players than the eight he got: "Originally, I had wanted to have 40 cars linked up, just like in a real race, but it just wasn't feasible."

## In the arcade

The finished game has three courses: a beginner's oval, a canyon course and a seaside city course. *Edge*'s show report described damage that changed a car's shape according to how hard it had hit something, an underside mapped with chassis and exhaust for when the car rolled over, and four viewpoints, as in *Virtua Racing*. The home versions named the courses Three Seven Speedway, Dinosaur Canyon and Sea-Side Street Galaxy. The oval puts 40 cars on the track at once, and a crash into the wall slows the car until it is repaired in the pits.

It became the arcade game to beat. Sega had prepared only a limited number of machines, *Edge* reported in 2000, expecting a small launch. *Edge*'s chart of dedicated arcade machines had *Ridge Racer* at number one in July 1994; from August 1994 to January 1995 the top place went to *Daytona*, with *Ridge Racer* usually second. Sega has not published earnings for the game, and the public sources give no reliable figure.

## The music

Mitsuyoshi sang on his own soundtrack, and the songs came with titles: "Let's Go Away", "Sky High", "The King of Speed" and a hidden fourth, "Pounding Pavement". Not everyone liked them. Reviewing the Saturn version in June 1995, *Edge* complained that "the karaoke-style wailing, repetitive samples and weedy collision effects become much more obtrusive and irritating outside the cacophonous atmosphere of an arcade". *Sega Saturn Magazine* reported in 1996 that Sega UK had received "many letters" from buyers of the game about its "inappropriate" music, although the magazine had "grown quite fond" of the songs. For the European release of the 1996 remake, Sega Europe's musician Richard Jacques remixed the tunes with little of the singing. Other players missed it: one *Computer & Video Games* reviewer of that remake said he preferred "the funky Japanese music and presentation".

## The Saturn version

AM2 converted the game for Sega's new console, and it went on sale in Japan in April 1995 at ¥6,800. *Edge* gave it eight out of ten. The magazine liked the racing, with its powerslides and rival cars that "intelligently block your path with violent swerving manoeuvres", and it thought the three courses gave the game more life than *Ridge Racer*'s one. But the Saturn game ran in a letterbox 10% smaller than the screen and drew its scenery "disturbingly late": "when you approach a corner you usually find that parts of the scenery are missing until the very last second". *Edge* put the "rough-and-ready looks" down to a rush job rather than to the Saturn itself. Five years later the magazine summed the port up as "handicapped by poor draw distances and substandard frame rates".

Sega tried again. The console team that had converted *Sega Rally*, directed by Ryuichi Hattori, spent 1996 on *Daytona USA: Championship Circuit Edition*, built on the *Sega Rally* engine. *Sega Saturn Magazine* reported that it ran at "a rock solid 30 frames a second" without the letterbox, and it added two courses, new cars and a split-screen two-player mode. *Computer & Video Games* was not won over in December 1996. One reviewer thought the cars no longer handled "anywhere near the way they do in the arcade", and another would rather have "the original Daytona. Even the badly-bordered UK version, bad clipping and all." A Windows version was scheduled for release in Britain on 16 November 1996.

## Later versions

Nagoshi produced the arcade sequel, *Daytona USA 2: Battle on the Edge*, on the Model 3 board in 1998, and told *Edge* he had not wanted to make it sooner: "we'd accomplished everything we wanted with Daytona". Having become president of Sega's Amusement Vision studio, he then oversaw a Dreamcast remake of the original. *Daytona USA 2001*, developed by Genki, had the three arcade courses, two from *Championship Circuit Edition* and three new ones, and *Edge* found no drop below 60 frames a second. With a steering wheel plugged in, the magazine said, it became "an almost perfect replica of the coin-op". Nagoshi still ranked the original first: he had produced *Daytona USA 2* but not directed it, he said, whereas "I did on Daytona USA … For me the original version appeared clearly as the best choice."

Sega released the arcade game again for the Xbox 360 and PlayStation 3 in October 2011, redrawn at 720p and with a karaoke mode that, as *Eurogamer* put it, "overlays the lyrics of Takenobu Mitsuyoshi's stirringly cheesy soundtrack to the game, inviting you to sing along". In November 2016 Sega showed a new *Daytona* arcade game at the IAAPA Attractions Expo in Orlando, the first new arcade game in the series since *Daytona USA 2*.
