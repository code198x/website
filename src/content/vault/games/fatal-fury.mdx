In 1991 the man who had designed the first *[Street Fighter](/vault/games/street-fighter)* found himself competing with its sequel. Takashi Nishiyama had left Capcom for SNK, and while Capcom's *[Street Fighter II](/vault/games/street-fighter-ii)* filled the arcades that year, he led SNK's reply. *Fatal Fury* reached arcades on 25 November 1991 as a cartridge for SNK's [Neo Geo](/vault/systems/neo-geo), known in Japan as *Garou Densetsu*, "Legend of the Hungry Wolf". *Computer and Video Games* could see where the idea had come from, and still gave it 94%. Its arenas had two planes of play, and its hero, Terry Bogard, and his enemy, Geese Howard, are still fighting more than thirty years later.

## Fast facts

- **Developer and publisher:** [SNK](/vault/companies/snk).
- **Released:** 25 November 1991 in the arcades, on the Neo Geo MVS; also on cartridge for the home Neo Geo.
- **Led by:** Takashi Nishiyama, with the planner Hiroshi Matsumoto, both formerly of Capcom.
- **Fighters:** Terry Bogard, Andy Bogard and Joe Higashi, against a line of opponents ending with Geese Howard.
- **Home versions:** Super Nintendo and Mega Drive, both published by Takara.
- **Sequels:** *Fatal Fury 2*, *Fatal Fury Special*, *Garou: Mark of the Wolves* (1999) and *Fatal Fury: City of the Wolves* (2025); the characters also joined *[The King of Fighters](/vault/games/king-of-fighters)*.

## From Street Fighter to SNK

Nishiyama had made his name at Irem with *[Moon Patrol](/vault/games/moon-patrol)* and *[Kung-Fu Master](/vault/games/kung-fu-master)*. He joined Capcom in 1986 and made the original *Street Fighter* (1987), for which, *Edge* reported, he borrowed the idea of the Hadoken fireball from the energy weapon of the anime *Space Battleship Yamato*. SNK then hired him and Hiroshi Matsumoto, the game's director and planner. Accounts differ on when he moved: *Wireframe* has said both "shortly after" *Street Fighter* came out and 1988, and Fabien Sanglard's history of Capcom's CPS arcade board says 1990.

At SNK he became head of development. The first Neo Geo games had military and sports themes, but *Wireframe* wrote that Nishiyama "still had unfinished business in the fighting genre", and *Fatal Fury* was SNK's first one-on-one fighting game for the system. Sanglard names him as its director, and *Retro Gamer* describes the series as "helmed by producer Takashi Nishiyama and planner Hiroshi Matsumoto". *Street Fighter*'s projectile came with him: *Edge* noted that the Hadoken idea turned up again in *Fatal Fury*.

## Revenge in South Town

The story is a revenge plot. Terry and Andy Bogard's father, Jeff, was killed by Geese Howard. The brothers trained for ten years, then entered a fighting tournament run by Geese's organisation to get at him. Their friend Joe Higashi joined them; as *Computer and Video Games* noted, he had no quarrel with Geese and was only there for the glory. Players choose one of the three and fight their way across South Town to Geese's headquarters, meeting opponents such as Duck King and Geese's armed henchman, Billy Kane, to decide who will be the "King of Fighters". Every couple of fights there is a bonus round.

Each fighter has a few special moves, entered as combinations of joystick movements and button presses. The Sega magazine *Sega Visions* picked out Terry's Burning Knuckle, a punch that does "major damage from a distance", and his Power Wave for dealing with opponents who fight on the ground.

## Two planes

What set *Fatal Fury* apart was depth. The arenas have two lines of play, one in the foreground and one in the background, and fighters can be on either. In the first game, as the Japanese site GAME Watch explained, only the computer's fighters could switch lines freely; a player could only follow an opponent who had jumped across. It was also unusual in what happened when a second player put a coin in: instead of starting a fight between them, the game had the two players team up against the computer's fighter first, and then fight each other.

Not everyone was convinced by the planes. Reviewing the Mega Drive version, *Games Master* called them "an unsuccessful attempt to split the playfield into two horizontal planes". SNK kept the idea and opened it up: in *Fatal Fury 2*, SNK's own *Official Neo Geo Newsletter* explained in 1993, players could "jump from foreground to background to avoid enemy attacks". *Wireframe* later counted the "two-lane battle arena" among the things *Fatal Fury* gave SNK's fighting games.

## Reception

British magazines reviewed the Neo Geo cartridge early in 1992. Paul Rand of *Computer and Video Games* gave it 94% overall, praised its huge, well-animated sprites and warned that "even on Easy mode, Fatal Fury is no pushover", though "the most enjoyment is gleaned from battling against a human opponent". *Zero* was less impressed: "Nothing leaps out of Fatal Fury to lift it above the hundreds of other beat 'em up games on the market. Average."

The home console versions came from Takara. *Super Play* reviewed the Japanese Super Famicom version in February 1993 as the first of a wave of *Street Fighter II* "pretenders". Rich Pelley found that the gameplay "struggles hard, but still doesn't compare", while judging it "the best of the current crop of pretenders". *Games Master*'s Andy Lowe, reviewing the Mega Drive version that May, found it "an anaemic and ultimately unsatisfactory experience" without the instant appeal of *Street Fighter II*. On the home Neo Geo itself the game was expensive: *Retro Gamer* reports that the cartridge sold for $250 in America.

## The series

*Fatal Fury 2* followed; SNK's newsletter was previewing it in December 1992, and in 1993 it reviewed a sequel with eight selectable fighters, new characters with three special moves each, and free movement between the planes. *Fatal Fury Special* came next, in 1993; Nobuyuki Kuroki, who joined SNK that year, remembered the new staff "debugging 24 hours a day on alternating shifts" to finish it. In 1994 *The King of Fighters '94* put Terry, Andy and Joe together as the *Fatal Fury* team in SNK's crossover series. *[Garou: Mark of the Wolves](/vault/games/garou-mark-of-the-wolves)* followed in 1999.

Then the series stopped for 26 years, until SNK released *Fatal Fury: City of the Wolves* on 24 April 2025. In between, Terry Bogard had become a guest fighter in Nintendo's *Super Smash Bros. Ultimate*, released on 6 November 2019. The original 1991 game was reissued for modern consoles in 2022 in the Arcade Archives series. Nishiyama himself later became president of Dimps, the company that developed *Street Fighter IV* for Capcom.
