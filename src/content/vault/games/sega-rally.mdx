Sega's arcade racers of the early 1990s ran on smooth tracks. *[Daytona USA](/vault/games/daytona-usa)* went round oval tracks, and Namco's *Ridge Racer* ran on smooth tarmac. *Sega Rally Championship* took its cars off the road. Its stages ran through desert, forest and mountains, over bumps, ramps and potholes, and its two cars slid and bounced on the loose surfaces as real rally cars do. Players liked it, and so did other developers. Guy Wilday, producer of Codemasters' *Colin McRae Rally*, told *Edge* in 2005 that the basic premise of his own game "was based around the car handling in *Sega Rally*": "Everyone who played it loved the way the cars behaved on the different surfaces, especially the fact that you could slide the car realistically on the loose gravel."

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** Sega AM3, a new arcade team whose earlier games included *Jurassic Park* and *Star Wars Arcade*.
- **Director and designer:** Kenji Sasaki.
- **Producer:** [Tetsuya Mizuguchi](/vault/people/tetsuya-mizuguchi).
- **Main programmer:** Sohei Yamamoto.
- **Hardware:** an enhanced Sega [Model 2](/vault/techniques/model-2) board.
- **Arcade release:** early 1995 in Japan, reaching the UK in the spring.
- **Cars:** Toyota Celica and Lancia Delta; on the Saturn, a hidden Lancia Stratos.
- **Home versions:** [Saturn](/vault/systems/sega-saturn) (Japan, 29 December 1995; UK, January 1996) and Windows.

## Building a rally game

AM3 showed the game at the Nicograph show in Tokyo at the end of 1994, and *Edge* visited the team for its March 1995 issue. "We wanted to make a racing game that was very different to all the others out there," Sasaki told the magazine. "We were really impressed by rally as a sport in America and Europe and felt it could work well as a videogame." In 2003 he recalled that the idea had faced two taboos in Japan: nobody wanted to make a game about an everyday "box-shaped car", and people were uneasy about rallying itself.

The cars were real. *Edge* reported in 1995 that the game had the Lancia Delta that won the 1992 World Rally Championship and a 1994 Toyota Celica, and that engineers from both companies had helped without any licence fee being discussed. Mizuguchi told a less smooth story in 2003: "We asked Toyota and [Lancia owner] Fiat for help with testing but they turned us away several times. We kept trying and after they saw what we had they came onboard." For the scenery, he remembered a three-week drive from the American west coast to Mexico, "filming and grabbing all the textures we could". He also said that the engine sound of the Lancia Delta was the sound of his own car.

The programmers found the sliding hardest. "The skidding of the cars through bends was the most difficult point to realise," Yamamoto told *Edge*, and "the jumps that the cars take over bumps were also hard work." Sasaki was open, years later, about how far the result was from reality: "our cars in *Sega Rally* were drifting in a way a real WRC car would not," he said in 2005, but "users were delighted and found our game very 'realistic'." He had lowered the speed compared with other racers and "focused everything on controls".

## In the arcade

The coin-op's cockpit view had a rear-view mirror, like *Ridge Racer*'s, and a top speed of around 200 km/h, slower than *Daytona*. Mizuguchi described the cabinet's "active shock generator": the seat moved "to relay the relationship with the road" and reacted to knocks from other cars, and two motors drove the seat and the steering feedback. Up to four linked players could race. *Edge* counted the translucent car windows, through which a player could see the other driver and sometimes the cars beyond, as a sign that AM3 had pushed the [Model 2](/vault/techniques/model-2) board further than *Daytona* had.

There were three stages, Desert, Forest and Mountain, and a bonus fourth, Lakeside. Sasaki said in 2003 that the Mountain stage came from a moment when he had lost sight of why cars were exciting at all, so he drove his own car up into the mountains, enjoyed it, and put the experience into the game.

## The Saturn conversion

Sega's first Saturn conversion of *Daytona USA* had been criticised for its jerky frame rate and late-appearing scenery, so the *Sega Rally* conversion mattered. Mizuguchi produced it, and Atsuhiko Nakamura, head of Sega's consumer software team, directed it. Nakamura explained that "we're making a new game using the same data. The program itself is completely different." Mizuguchi added that the two machines drew different numbers of polygons and used different textures: "The Model 2 uses some monochrome texturing while the Saturn uses full-colour textures."

The result ran at 30 frames a second, half the coin-op's rate, and *Edge* called it "easily the most impressive example yet of the Saturn's abilities". The Saturn version kept an arcade mode, added a split-screen two-player mode and let players adjust a car's tyres, suspension and steering response. Winning on Lakeside unlocked a third car, the rear-wheel-drive Lancia Stratos. Its team told *Sega Saturn Magazine* that it was "very difficult to drive", and that players' opinions had "confirmed that if another car was to be added it should be the Stratos". Asked how their game compared with *Ridge Racer*, they said that Namco's cars seemed to drift "on invisible rails", while theirs was "based on physical calculations", such as "the friction of the road or the shock absorbers".

The conversion came out in Japan on 29 December 1995, under the arcade's full title, *Sega Rally Championship 1995*. *Edge* gave it eight out of ten in February 1996. It found the arcade mode "a lonely experience", since opponents were spread so far apart that races felt like a race against the clock, and it teased the "desert" stage for its green fields, mud and grazing cows. But the tracks "demand a totally different style of driving from the smooth tarmac of the city racers", the handling was "a victory in simulating momentum", and it concluded: "*Virtua Fighter 2* and now this? Happy new year, Saturn owners." In the same issue *Edge* named it the best driving game of 1995. A Windows version followed; *PC Zone* put a one-track demo on its cover disc in May 1997 and advised players who found the controls "slippery" that "you're driving over slick runny mud and damp gravel".

## Sequels

Mizuguchi's team made *Sega Rally 2* for Sega's Model 3 arcade board in 1998, with Sasaki directing, and it came to the [Dreamcast](/vault/systems/sega-dreamcast) the following year. After that the series stalled. *Sega Rally 2006*, a PlayStation 2 game released in Japan, earned five out of ten from *Edge*, which called it "an unworthy *Sega Rally* heir".

Sega then set up a new studio in England, Sega Racing Studio near Solihull, founded in April 2005, and put Guy Wilday in charge. The man whose *Colin McRae Rally* had started from *Sega Rally*'s handling now remade it. His *Sega Rally* (2007) for the Xbox 360, PlayStation 3 and Windows had tracks that deformed as the cars drove over them, leaving ruts in mud and snow and rubber on tarmac. *Edge* gave it seven out of ten, and noted that the original game's cars could be unlocked. An arcade version, *Sega Rally 3*, was developed alongside it.
