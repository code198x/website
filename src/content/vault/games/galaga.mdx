In *Galaga* the enemy can take your ship. Every so often a Boss Galaga drops out of the formation, hovers above the player and spreads a tractor beam down the screen. Get caught and the fighter tumbles up into the beam and is carried back to the formation as a prisoner, at the cost of a life. Shoot the Boss while it dives, though, and the captured fighter comes back down and locks on beside the new one: two ships, moving together and firing together. Namco released it in September 1981 as the sequel to *[Galaxian](/vault/games/galaxian)*, and *Wireframe* has called that rescue "one of the first – if not the first – power-ups in a shooting game".

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** [Namco](/vault/companies/namco); in arcades in September 1981.
- **Designer:** Shigeru Yokoyama.
- **Predecessor:** *[Galaxian](/vault/games/galaxian)* (1979).
- **American licensee:** [Midway](/vault/companies/midway).
- **Hardware:** three Z80 processors sharing memory, with Namco custom chips for the starfield, the explosion sound and input.
- **Home versions include:** Sega's SG-1000, Namco's own versions for the Famicom and MSX, the NES, the Atari 7800 and the Game Boy.

## From Galaxian to Galaga

Namco asked Yokoyama for another fixed-screen shooter "in the *Galaxian* vein", Ryan Lambie writes in *Wireframe*, and he designed it in about eight weeks. Looking at *Galaxian*'s diving aliens, he thought: "Rather than just firing bullets at the player, what if the enemies could use a tractor beam to suck up their ship?" He later said the beam came from a science-fiction film whose name he could no longer remember. His first plan was that a rescued ship would simply be an extra life, until he saw that this was not much different from earning one by scoring points. The double ship was the answer.

It came with a catch. Two ships meant twice the firepower and twice the target, and a careless player could shoot the captured fighter instead of the alien holding it. *JoyStik*'s 1983 guide warned that a captured fighter shot down was worth 1,000 points, "but it is much more valuable if you can free it". Some players let a Boss take their ship on purpose, trading a life for the chance of a double ship.

## Playing it

The aliens do not start in formation: they fly in from the top and sides in lines, loop around the screen and settle at the top, and only then start peeling off to attack. The entry patterns are always the same for a given stage, which made memory as important as reflexes. In 1985 [Julian Rignall](/vault/people/julian-rignall), who had won *Computer and Video Games*' 1983 arcade championship, explained how to play it for the magazine. There were three kinds of alien, he wrote: blue ones that loop back at the bottom of the screen, red ones that "swerve crazily down the screen straight past you", both worth twice as much in the air as in formation, and the Boss Galagas, worth 400, 800 or 1,600 points when diving "depending on the size of escort". A Boss takes two hits, turning blue after the first.

From the fourth stage on, a red or blue alien may flash and split into three, heralded by a sound; destroying all three is worth a bonus of up to 3,000 points. After the second stage and on every fourth stage after that comes a Challenging Stage. Forty aliens fly in, in five groups of eight, and they neither shoot nor bomb. Each hit scores 100 points, and hitting all 40 earns a 10,000-point bonus. *Retro Gamer* counts the Challenging Stages among *Galaga*'s "pioneering features", an idea "subsequently stolen by countless games".

*Retro Gamer* notes that the player's ship gained "the ability to shoot multiple times in quick succession", and that rapid fire was "most definitely required". *JoyStik* thought *Galaga* "may be the only shooting game in which you rarely need to aim", because a player who knows where each wave will arrive can wait for it.

## Inside the cabinet

*Galaga* runs on the same kind of board as Namco's *Bosconian*: three Z80 processors sharing memory. MAME's notes from the schematics describe custom Namco chips alongside them, among them a starfield generator (05XX), an explosion sound generator (54XX) and an input chip (51XX). The reverse-engineering site Computer Archeology, which has disassembled the program, describes the work split: one Z80 runs the game, a second moves the aliens and their shots, and a third plays the sound.

That split explains the game's best-known flaw. Arcade players found that if they cleared the first stage except for one or two of the bees in the left-hand columns and then dodged their fire for a long time, the aliens eventually stopped shooting for the rest of the game. *JoyStik* printed the trick in October 1983: wait "anywhere from about five to 20 minutes — we don't know why it isn't always the same", and once the survivor has passed through the top of the screen four times without firing, "none of the bugs will fire at you". Computer Archeology's disassembly found the reason. The game keeps a fixed set of slots for the aliens' shots and marks an unused one with a horizontal position of 0. A shot fired while an alien is at position 0, which happens most often to aliens diving from the edges of the formation, is never moved and never cleared. Each one permanently fills a slot, and when every slot is full, no alien can fire.

## Midway and the arcades

[Midway](/vault/companies/midway), which already built *Space Invaders* and *Galaxian* under licence, took *Galaga* for America. MAME holds several Midway sets marked "Namco (Midway license)". *Retro Gamer* found one difference in them: operators could set a switch so that an extra credit bought a single-player game with more ships, usually seven instead of three, a feature Midway also tried on *Wizard of Wor*, *Solar Fox* and *Omega Race* (see [difficulty design](/vault/techniques/difficulty-design)).

The game was a hit. *Electronic Games* listed *Galaga* (Midway) fourth among the most popular coin-ops in February 1983, and *JoyStik* wrote that October that it had been "one of the ten most-played games in the country for over a year". In Britain, Julian Rignall's prize for winning *Computer and Video Games*' 1983 championship was a *Galaga* machine.

## At home

Sega made a version for its SG-1000 console. *Sega-Galaga* was, in *Retro Gamer*'s words, "pared down to the bone": no Challenging Stages and no splitting aliens, and a formation so large that it attacked "in large numbers almost from the off". Namco published its own version for the Famicom in 1985, and *Edge* named *Galaga* among Namco's "first big Famicom sellers". Namco's home label, Namcot, also sold it on MSX cartridge. *Nintendo Power* previewed the American NES version in September 1988, promising players could "relive arcade memories" with a Challenging Stage "every third assault" and a hit-ratio count at the end.

Atari's 7800 cartridge sold for $19.95. Reviewing it for *ANALOG Computing* in June 1988, Joyce Worley described aliens that "twist and turn like living things, making them devilishly hard to hit", and decided that "*Galaga* for the home lives up to its coin-op antecedents". A Game Boy cartridge pairing *Galaga* with *Galaxian* went straight into *Nintendo Power*'s Game Boy chart in September 1995.

## Sequels and revivals

Namco carried the idea on. *Gaplus* followed, and *Galaga '88*, released in December 1987, added short scrolling sections between waves and, as *Zzap!64* described it, a "giant" ship for a player who rescued a fighter while already flying a double ship. *Wireframe* noted the cost: capturing a ship a second time made the player's craft "even larger and more vulnerable to attack".

The original stayed in print. *Edge* reviewed *Namco Museum Vol. 1* for the PlayStation in February 1996 and called *Galaga* "an incredibly tough and addictive blast". Namco also used it as a loading screen: the PlayStation *[Tekken](/vault/games/tekken)* let players shoot their way through three stages of *Galaga* while the fighting game loaded, and *Edge* called it "arcade-perfect". By 2006 it was on sale again on the Xbox 360's download service.
