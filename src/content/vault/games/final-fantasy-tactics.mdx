*Final Fantasy Tactics* was a war game wearing a famous name. [Square](/vault/companies/square) put the jobs, chocobos and spells of its *[Final Fantasy](/vault/games/final-fantasy)* series onto a small 3D battlefield divided into tiles, and asked the player to win fights that could last half an hour. Most of the people who made it had come from a small developer called Quest, whose *Tactics Ogre* on the Super Famicom *Edge* called "the definitive SNES wargame". *Edge* thought the *Final Fantasy* label was a blessing for a genre as unfashionable as the console wargame, and a risk too: it could "easily mislead" players who knew the series only from *[Final Fantasy VII](/vault/games/final-fantasy-vii)*. The game went to the top of the Japanese charts in 1997. In America the *Official U.S. PlayStation Magazine* named it the best strategy game of 1998. Britain never had it, except on import.

## Fast facts

- **Developer and publisher:** [Square](/vault/companies/square), for the [PlayStation](/vault/systems/sony-playstation).
- **Released:** Japan, June 1997; North America, January 1998. No European release on the PlayStation.
- **Director:** Yasumi Matsuno, whose earlier work was Quest's Ogre Battle series.
- **Character designs:** Akihiko Yoshida.
- **Music:** Hitoshi Sakimoto and Masaharu Iwata.
- **Genre:** [tactical RPG](/vault/genres/tactical-rpg), with 20 jobs for ordinary characters.
- **Later versions:** *The War of the Lions* (PSP, 2007); *The Ivalice Chronicles* (2025).

## From Quest to Square

Square had a playable version on its stand at the Tokyo Game Show in the spring of 1997, where *Edge* noted its resemblance to Quest's *Tactics Ogre*. The resemblance was not an accident. Matsuno's earlier work had been Quest's *Ogre Battle* series, and *Edge*'s review found the new game "almost a carbon copy of *Ogre*" apart from its landscape of polygons, made by a team that was almost identical "from character designer to music composer". Square told *Edge* that it had cut out "the often-tedious aspects of simulation games" to win over players who had avoided them.

## How the battles work

Each battle takes place on a small map of raised tiles that the player can rotate. Units move and act on their turn, and *Edge* found that "everything, from the elevation of your character on the landscape compared to that of its target to the compatibility of their respective zodiac signs" counted towards whether an attack landed. A single skirmish could take "the best part of 20 or 30 minutes".

Turns follow Square's Charge Time system, a relative of the Active Time Battle in the main series (see [Active Time Battle](/vault/design/active-time-battle)). Every unit has a gauge that fills at its own speed and acts when it is full, so the order of play changes as the battle goes on. *Edge* thought this went a long way to "minimising the board-game feel of taking turns".

Characters grow through jobs, among them Knights, Archers and Chemists. Every action, from a sword stroke to drinking a potion, earns experience and job points, and characters learn and master the abilities that go with each job. Even using an item has to be learned: *Edge* pointed out that a potion, which any *Final Fantasy* character could drink, "can here only be accessed if a character has learned how to use it". The 2007 PSP version counted 22 jobs and over 400 abilities.

## The story

The game is set in the kingdom of Ivalice soon after the Fifty Years' War. The prince is too young to take the throne, and two dukes, Goltana and Larg, compete to rule as regent, Goltana's forces under the banner of the Black Lion and Larg's under the White. The player is Ramza Beoulve, a young knight of a noble family, who leads a band of mercenaries through the war that follows. *Edge* in 1998 was not impressed: it found the story of "knights and kings" and two warring houses predictable, and short of the airships and crystals of the main series. Others read it differently. *Retro Gamer* later said the narrative "reads like Hamlet", and *Edge* itself, nine years on, wrote that it was "austere and weighty". The *Official U.S. PlayStation Magazine* admitted it was still "trying to figure it out".

## Reception

*Edge* gave the PlayStation original nine out of ten in April 1998: "for those who enjoy a more cerebral videogame challenge, Final Fantasy Tactics delivers exactly what it sets out to with considerable aplomb." The editor of the American collectors' magazine *Digital Press* reported needing 85 hours to finish it. When the *Official U.S. PlayStation Magazine* gave it the award for the best strategy game of 1998, Matsuno accepted it as director: "I've created and developed games based on influences from the U.S., so knowing that this particular Japanese strategy game has been accepted so well by the U.S. audience is exciting."

Its English text was a weak point. *Edge*, previewing the PSP version in 2007, called the PlayStation translation "awful".

## Later versions

Square Enix released *Final Fantasy Tactics: The War of the Lions* for the PSP in Japan in May 2007 and in America that October. It was also sold in Britain, at about £30, the first time the game had been released there. The PSP version added widescreen cutscenes drawn in Yoshida's style, new jobs and characters, among them Balthier from *Final Fantasy XII*, two-player battles over the PSP's wireless link, and a new English translation. *Edge* gave it nine out of ten and called it "one of videogaming's greatest achievements, one finally properly served by an excellent English translation". *Retro Gamer* gave it 85% and blamed the camera and loading times.

Spin-offs followed on Nintendo's handhelds: *Final Fantasy Tactics Advance* on the Game Boy Advance and *Tactics Advance 2* on the DS, which *Edge* found lighter in tone "in the absence of Yasumi Matsuno". On 30 September 2025 Square Enix released *Final Fantasy Tactics: The Ivalice Chronicles* for current consoles and the PC. It offered an enhanced version with a revised script and full voice acting alongside a classic version using *The War of the Lions*' translation, and Matsuno returned to work on the script.
