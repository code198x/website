Miss the duck in *Duck Hunt* and a hunting dog rises out of the long grass and laughs at you. *Club Nintendo*, Nintendo's British club magazine, found in 1989 that "everybody that we have met who have played the game seem to want to shoot the dog more than the actual ducks". Many NES owners met him, because *Duck Hunt* came in the box: first in the NES Deluxe Set with the [Zapper](/vault/hardware/nes-zapper) light gun, then on the same cartridge as *[Super Mario Bros.](/vault/games/super-mario-bros)*. Behind the joke is a clever trick. The Zapper has no idea where it is pointing, and *Duck Hunt* has to work that out one frame at a time.

## Fast facts

- **Publisher:** [Nintendo](/vault/companies/nintendo).
- **Released:** Famicom, Japan, 1984; NES, United States, October 1985, with *Hogan's Alley* and *Wild Gunman*; Britain, 1987.
- **Controller:** the Zapper, or the Famicom's revolver-shaped light gun.
- **Modes:** Game A, one duck; Game B, two ducks; Game C, clay pigeons.
- **Music:** [Hirokazu Tanaka](/vault/people/hip-tanaka).
- **Arcade:** *Vs. Duck Hunt*, on Nintendo's Vs. System.

## Three shots a duck

The rules fit on the back of the box. The dog sniffs through the grass, a duck flies up, and the player has three shots to bring it down. *Club Nintendo*'s guide spelled it out: in Game A one duck flies at a time, in Game B two, and "either way, you have only three shots to fire, so make sure that each one counts". Each hit duck is held up by the "jubilant (and often comical) dog"; a miss sets him laughing. Ten ducks make a round, and "to proceed to the next round, you have to hit at least six out of every ten". The ducks get faster as the rounds go on. Game C swaps the ducks for clay pigeons, thrown away from the player so that they shrink into the distance. In Game A a second player can take a controller and steer the duck, which *Retro Gamer* later described as a way for "duck-welfare enthusiasts" to save the birds.

The British press was polite rather than excited. *ACE*, reviewing the new NES in its first issue in October 1987, found that the Zapper still worked from five metres away and that "it actually feels like a pistol". By 1992 *Total!* called *Duck Hunt* "a mildly entertaining Zapper game", fine "if you get it bundled free with the Action Set but don't buy it separately".

## How the Zapper finds the duck

The Zapper reports two things: whether its trigger is pulled, and whether its light sensor can see a bright spot at that instant. It cannot tell the game where it is aimed. Games like *Duck Hunt* get round this with the television. As *Wireframe* explained, pulling the trigger "would cause the entire screen to go black and an enemy sprite to become bright white". If the sensor sees light, the gun is pointing at the target. With two ducks in the air, each one flashes white for a single frame in turn, so the game can tell which one was hit.

The NESdev Wiki, the community reference for NES hardware, gives the full sequence. When the trigger is pulled, the game first checks during the vertical blank, while the screen is dark, that the gun sees *no* light. A gun that sees light then is pointing at a lamp or a window, not the screen. Then, for each target, it draws a black screen with one white box where that target is, and watches the sensor for the whole frame.

The trick depends on a CRT television. A CRT draws its picture as a moving spot of light, about 15,700 lines a second, and the Zapper's sensor has a filter tuned to that rate, so it ignores the steady light of a lamp. A modern flat screen holds its picture steady, which the filter treats much like room light, and it delays the picture while processing it, so the white box arrives after the game has stopped looking. That is why *Duck Hunt* fails on most modern televisions. The [NES Zapper](/vault/hardware/nes-zapper) entry has the detail.

## The first Duck Hunt, 1976

Nintendo had used the name eight years before the Famicom game. Its *Kôsenjû Duck Hunt* ("light-ray gun Duck Hunt") was an electro-mechanical toy from 1976, one of the last that Nintendo made, and the product of a decade of light-gun toys from [Gunpei Yokoi](/vault/people/gunpei-yokoi)'s team. It cost ¥9,500 and had no screen. A battery-powered projector threw a flapping duck onto the living-room wall, and a motor-driven mirror made it fly. The player fired a toy shotgun that gave out a burst of light.

The sensor was in the projector, not the gun. Nintendo's American patent for the design, which names Takao Ohta as inventor and was granted in 1980, puts a photoelectric cell on the projector's own optical axis. Light from the shotgun that struck the projected duck reflected off the wall and travelled back through the same lens and mirror onto the cell. On a hit, the projector switched to a picture of a wounded duck, the mirror dropped it down the wall and a mechanism inside played a quack. The Famicom game turned this round: the television became the target and the sensor moved into the gun.

## Duck Hunt in the arcade

Nintendo also put the game into arcades on its Vs. System, which ran games on common hardware and changed only the software. *Computer Gamer* found *Vs. Duck Hunt* in British arcades in April 1986, with a gun attached to the cabinet by a cable. Its screens alternated between ducks and clay pigeons, and it added the revenge that home players never got. In a bonus round, ducks flew out of the undergrowth, the dog sometimes came out with them, and "you can get your own back by shooting at him". He then limped back "on crutches and with his arm in a sling".

## Bundles and later life

In America the Deluxe Set of 1986 packed *Duck Hunt* with *Gyromite*, the Zapper and the robot [R.O.B.](/vault/hardware/rob); a November 1986 mail-order advert listed it at $149. Mattel's British launch in 1987 kept the same pairing in its Deluxe set. Nintendo then combined *Duck Hunt* and *Super Mario Bros.* on one cartridge for the Action Set, which *Total!* priced at £100 in Britain in 1992 with the console, two joypads and the Zapper. In 1988 *Nintendo Power* announced the Power Set, whose single cartridge held *Duck Hunt*, *Super Mario Bros.* and *World Class Track Meet* for the [Power Pad](/vault/hardware/power-pad).

The game outlived the Zapper and the CRT. A version played with the Wii Remote came to the Wii U's Virtual Console in 2014. The same year the dog and a duck became a playable pair in Nintendo's *Super Smash Bros.* games for the Wii U and 3DS, and they returned in *Super Smash Bros. Ultimate*.
