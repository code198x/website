The team that made *Super Mario Bros.* thought it would be their last big cartridge game. Nintendo's Famicom Disk System was due the following year, and its disks held a megabit, far more than the cartridge they were working with. "I wanted to put in as much as possible that would make people think, 'Nintendo sure does know cartridges!' and 'How did they do that?!'" [Shigeru Miyamoto](/vault/people/shigeru-miyamoto) said in 2010. They fitted a scrolling world, secret rooms, warp pipes and a redesigned hero into 40 kilobytes, and had 20 bytes left over at the end. The game went on sale in Japan on 13 September 1985; four months later shops were putting up signs saying it had sold out.

## Fast facts

- **Made by:** [Nintendo](/vault/companies/nintendo), in the development division Miyamoto had run since 1984.
- **Designers:** Shigeru Miyamoto and Takashi Tezuka, who drew all the levels between them.
- **Programming:** Toshihiko Nakago and his team. **Music:** [Koji Kondo](/vault/people/koji-kondo).
- **Released:** Famicom, Japan, 13 September 1985. NES, United States: public sources place it in the New York test market of late 1985. Britain, 1987, packed with Mattel's basic NES set.
- **Size:** 256 kilobits (32K) of program and data, 64 kilobits (8K) of graphics.
- **Sales:** "almost six million" by mid-1987, according to *CRASH*; more than 40 million, according to Nintendo.

## A plumber and his brother

Mario had been a carpenter in *[Donkey Kong](/vault/games/donkey-kong)*. When Miyamoto sketched him again for the new game, David Sheff writes in *Game Over*, someone mentioned that he looked more like a plumber, so Miyamoto made him one. Plumbers work on pipes, so large green pipes became obstacles and doorways to secret places. Mario also got a brother, Luigi, taller and thinner and dressed differently so that two players could tell them apart on the screen.

The game grew out of *[Mario Bros.](/vault/games/mario-bros)* (1983), Miyamoto's arcade game with a black background and a screen that did not scroll. For the Famicom he wanted the opposite: a bright background, bigger characters and a screen that moved. The first test program was a square moving across a scrolling screen. Scrolling cost something. Two players could share a *Mario Bros.* screen, but not a moving one, so in *Super Mario Bros.* they take turns.

Miyamoto's first written specification, dated February 1985, is odd to read now. In it the player pressed up on the D-pad to jump, and the A button was for attacking, with a rifle and a beam gun among the planned weapons. At one point Mario was going to ride a cloud and shoot from it. Within a week Miyamoto had drawn a planning sheet with a blue sky instead of a black one, and Nakago later said that was the moment the team knew they had something.

## Everything they knew

Miyamoto called the result "a grand culmination" of Nintendo's earlier games, and his specification said so. It listed refinements of *Donkey Kong*'s slopes, lifts, conveyor belts and ladders, *Donkey Kong Jr.*'s ropes, logs and springs, and *Mario Bros.*' enemies, frozen platforms and POW blocks. The characters twice the size of *Mario Bros.*' came from *Devil World*. From *[Excitebike](/vault/games/excitebike)* came the technique of scrolling only part of the screen, and the idea for warp zones: its arcade version let good players start at an advanced track. In *Super Mario Bros.* a skilled player could jump over the end of a level, find a hidden room full of pipes and skip whole worlds.

Much of the rest came from Miyamoto's own life, as Sheff tells it. Near his flat in Kyoto there was a manhole cover set into a wall. He walked past it every day and never found out where it led. In the game, a pipe in the ground or a block in mid-air can lead somewhere nobody expected.

## Drawing the levels

Every level was drawn on paper by Miyamoto or Tezuka, who had joined Nintendo as an artist shortly before. Tezuka described the routine to Satoru Iwata in 2010. He designed a level imagining how players would move through it, then showed it to Miyamoto, who would say things like "When an enemy appears here, the player will run this way, but we don't want the player to hit Mario's head on that." The team could only see a working version of a map once a day, and there were no tools for trying out a change straight away, so a sloppy design wasted a day.

The first level is the best-known result. Mario starts on open ground, walking right, and the first thing to come at him is a Goomba, which dies when he lands on it. Iwata called it "always there for you in the beginning so you can keep stomping on it". The first block marked with a question mark is just above. Hit from below, one of these blocks releases a mushroom that makes Mario twice the size; big Mario can break bricks and survive one hit. The game has no instructions on the screen. A player learns its rules by meeting them one at a time, in the order the designers chose.

## 40 kilobytes

The whole game had to fit in 32K of program and level data, plus 8K of graphics: the patterns for every tile and sprite. Miyamoto and his team treated that as a puzzle. The bushes on the ground and the clouds in the sky are the same shapes; the Famicom colours each part of the background from one of a few four-colour palettes, and swapping the palette turns a cloud into a bush. The small castle at the start of a level is the top of the big castle at the end. Nakago said the upper storey of the big castle has a door, "but we insist it's a window!"

By the end they were counting bytes. Nakago told Iwata that a block cost two or three bytes, so twenty spare bytes meant ten more blocks, and Miyamoto kept asking for ten more. When Kondo wanted music for the ending, he said he could loop a tune in 40 bytes. That left 20 bytes, and Miyamoto used them for a crown, a reward for players who built up ten or more extra lives. Normally, he said, a team kept about 100 bytes back in case a bug needed fixing.

The screen itself uses one of the NES picture chip's tricks. The score, coin count and timer stay still at the top while the world scrolls underneath. The program waits for the chip's "sprite 0 hit" signal, which it has arranged to happen just below the status bar, and changes the scroll at that moment. The [PPU](/vault/hardware/ppu) entry explains how.

## Sold out

In Japan it sold out. For Iwata's 2010 interviews, Masayuki Uemura brought a photograph from the *Asahi Graph* of January 1986 showing a shop sign: "Super Mario Bros. and Portopia Renzoku Satsujin Jiken are sold out. No further stock scheduled. Our apologies." The same magazine ran a strategy guide for adults. Nintendo's Hiroshi Imanishi said of the Famicom's uncertain early years: "That one game blew away the uncertainty that had hovered up to that point."

In America the game reached shops with the NES, which Nintendo test-marketed in New York from late 1985 and sold nationally in 1986. By 1988, Sheff notes, it was included with the console. *N64 Magazine*'s history of Nintendo put it plainly: *Super Mario Bros.* "was the game that sold the NES in the west".

## In Britain

British players met Mario in the arcades first. In March 1986 *Computer and Video Games* reported a coin-op *Super Mario Bros.* from Nintendo, and predicted that it "should be a great success and remain in the arcades for a long time".

The home version arrived in the summer of 1987, when Mattel launched the NES in Britain. The basic set cost £129.99 with *Super Mario Bros.*, soon cut to £99.99. *CRASH* and *ZZAP!64* ran the same group review of the console, and it split the reviewers. Ciaran Brennan thought most of the software was "nothing more than variations of the Donkey Kong theme". Julian Rignall disagreed: "The fiendishly addictive and incredibly playable Super Mario Brothers is a fine example – arcade perfect (flawless actually)." He called it the finest game he had ever played. In its first issue that October, *ACE* said the game was "almost worth buying the console just for this one game", and pointed to its four million Japanese buyers.

*Computer and Video Games* printed a complete players' guide to the NES game in September 1987. Owners of home computers got imitations. When *CRASH* reviewed the Spectrum version of Rainbow Arts' *The Great Giana Sisters* in August 1988, it said "comparisons with Super Mario Bros are inevitable", and US Gold withdrew the game after what *ACE* called "pressure from Nintendo who felt it looked a bit too much like *Super Mario Brothers*".

## Sales

The figures grew with the console. *CRASH* reported "almost six million copies to date" in July 1987, and *ACE* four million Japanese buyers three months later. By 2010 Nintendo said the original game had sold more than 40 million copies, many of them packed with the NES. Sheff counts eight Mario games produced by Miyamoto between 1985 and 1991, selling 60 to 70 million copies between them.
