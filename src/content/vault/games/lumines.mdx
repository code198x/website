"Puzzle games accompanying a handheld's launch are seldom the most outwardly alluring of titles," *Edge* wrote in February 2005, "but Lumines was the exception to end all rules." It was the first game from [Q Entertainment](/vault/companies/q-entertainment), the studio [Tetsuya Mizuguchi](/vault/people/tetsuya-mizuguchi) had set up after leaving Sega, and, in the magazine's words, "the only completely original PSP launch title". Mizuguchi told *Edge* why the machine suited him: "It's more like an interactive Walkman than a games machine." *Lumines* was built to be played with headphones on. Every block that lands and every square that clears makes a sound that fits the track playing underneath, and the screen keeps time with the beat.

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** [Q Entertainment](/vault/companies/q-entertainment). **Producer:** [Tetsuya Mizuguchi](/vault/people/tetsuya-mizuguchi).
- **Publishers:** Bandai in Japan; Ubisoft in North America, Europe and Australia.
- **Released:** with the PSP in Japan in December 2004, at ¥5,040; March 2005 in North America, as *Lumines: Puzzle Fusion*; September 2005 in Europe.
- **Music:** tracks made for the game, with licensed songs by Mondo Grosso and the singer Eri Nobuchika.
- **Later versions:** PlayStation 2, Xbox 360, mobile phones and Windows, then sequels from 2006 onwards.

## How it plays

Blocks fall in squares of four tiles, two by two, made up of two colours. The player turns and places them so that tiles of one colour form solid squares. In most falling-block games a completed shape vanishes at once. In *Lumines* it waits for the timeline, a line that *Hyper* compared to a windscreen wiper, which sweeps across the playfield to the beat of the current track and erases every finished square it passes. Until it arrives, a player can build the group bigger for more points, or use it as a temporary platform for the next one. Some blocks carry a marker; when one is part of a finished square, it clears every touching tile of the same colour as well.

The music sets the pace. The playfield changes its "skin", the background, block colours, sound effects and song together, every few levels, and the timeline's speed follows the tempo of each new song. A slow track gives time to set up long chains; a fast one sweeps the board before the player is ready. *Hyper*'s reviewer said only two block colours were ever in play, which kept the rules simple, and that the music was "genuinely fused into the whole". He reported his own best Challenge score as 931,755, reached at level 225.

Challenge mode was the main game, and there were also Time Attack, a Puzzle mode for building given shapes, single-skin play, and a two-player mode over the PSP's wireless link. Beating the computer in the versus mode unlocked its skin and avatar.

## Reception

*Edge* gave it eight out of ten. It thought minor lapses, such as a track that "loops like a skipping record" while the player waits too long on a chain, and moments of silence between skins, kept *Lumines* short of the completeness of *[Rez](/vault/games/rez)*. But it said the game "expands upon its concepts in ways even Mizuguchi followers couldn't have expected", and, to the question of whether the PSP could encourage new experiences, "it's a resounding 'yes'". *Edge*'s PSP hardware review said you would not hear what the machine's audio could do until you plugged in good headphones, "preferably in conjunction with Lumines".

In America, *Electronic Gaming Monthly* called it the PSP's "hip 'n' happenin' answer to *[Tetris](/vault/games/tetris)*". Its reviewers liked the strategy of setting up combos and the music, but complained that the Challenge mode "takes waaaay too long to ramp up", so that "you'll snooze through the first 20-plus minutes". *Hyper* in Australia scored its sound at 94 and ended: "Funky, colourful, sexy, timeless. Lumines has arrived."

## Tetris by another name

The game began as something else. Mizuguchi had wanted to make a *Tetris* game with music for the PSP, but, as he told *Push Square* in 2025, the *Tetris* licence "was unavailable, and that's that". He added: "I don't know if I have the confidence to say we would've made the best PSP Tetris game." His team made its own puzzle instead. In 2018 his next company, Enhance, published *Tetris Effect*.

## The series

*Lumines* spread quickly. By 2006 *Edge* was describing the "unstoppable spread of the Lumines brand to mobile phones, PS2 and Live Arcade": *Lumines Live!* came to the Xbox 360's download service, and the PSP sequel *Lumines II* (2006) added new modes and more licensed pop songs, which *Retro Gamer* found "very disappointing", advising readers to stick with the original. Q went on to *Lumines Supernova* for the PlayStation 3 in 2008 and *Lumines Electronic Symphony* for the PS Vita in 2012, whose logo and typeface came from Build, the London studio of Michael C. Place, who had worked on *[Wipeout](/vault/games/wipeout)*'s graphics at The Designers Republic.

In January 2015 the mobile company Mobcast and Mizuguchi's Enhance bought the *Lumines* rights from Q Entertainment. Enhance released *Lumines Remastered*, made by Resonair, for current consoles and Windows in 2018, and a new game, *Lumines Arise*, in 2025.
