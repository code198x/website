In 1995 Square put three famous names on one cartridge. Hironobu Sakaguchi had made *[Final Fantasy](/vault/games/final-fantasy)*; Yuji Horii and Akira Toriyama had made its great rival, Enix's *[Dragon Quest](/vault/games/dragon-quest)*, Horii as designer and Toriyama as artist. *Edge*, previewing the Japanese release, called them "a team of designers who have attained almost legendary status in their native land", and reported that *Chrono Trigger* was "currently the most eagerly awaited game in Japan". It went on sale on 11 March 1995. *Nintendo Power* reported two million cartridges sold in Japan in two months.

The game was a time-travel story. A boy called Crono and his friends discover that in 1999 a creature called Lavos will break out of the Earth and plunge the world into a dark age, and they set out through the centuries to stop it. Two ideas made the game stand out: its battles happened in the places you walked through, with no cut to a separate battle screen, and you could face Lavos at almost any point, with a different ending depending on when.

## Fast facts

- **Developer and publisher:** [Square](/vault/companies/square) (Square Soft in America).
- **Japanese release:** 11 March 1995, Super Famicom; America followed in the summer of 1995.
- **Cartridge:** 32 megabits, which *Super Play* called Square's "biggest and most ambitious game to date".
- **Principal creators:** [Hironobu Sakaguchi](/vault/people/hironobu-sakaguchi), [Yuji Horii](/vault/people/yuji-horii) and Akira Toriyama (character design); Masato Kato was the story designer.
- **Music:** Yasunori Mitsuda, with pieces by [Nobuo Uematsu](/vault/people/nobuo-uematsu).
- **Later versions:** PlayStation (1999), Nintendo DS (2008), and later phones and PC.
- **Sales:** more than five million across all versions by March 2025, by Square Enix's count.

## Three names on one cartridge

*Super Play* reported in January 1995 that the game had first been discussed two years earlier, when the three men met at a software conference in America. *Edge* put the work at "two years" by "a team of designers". Horii, who had written every *Dragon Quest* from the first to the sixth, was its chief designer, *Edge* said; Toriyama, famous in Japan for *Dragon Ball*, designed the characters; Sakaguchi was Square's vice president and the director of the *Final Fantasy* series. Masato Kato was the game's story designer, the credit a PlayStation magazine gave him in 1999 when he went on to write *Chrono Cross*.

The mixture showed on the screen. *Super Play*'s import feature thought the spiky-haired hero had "a very simple, suggestive Dragon Quest flavour", that some monsters could have come out of *Dragon Ball Z*, and that the backgrounds belonged to *Final Fantasy*. It reported that Sakaguchi had wanted the characters drawn larger than usual, to show off Toriyama's designs.

## Six eras

Crono's own time is AD 1000. From there the party reaches the Middle Ages in AD 600; 12,000 BC; the age of the dinosaurs in 65,000,000 BC, where the cavewoman Ayla lives; AD 1999, the day Lavos rises; and AD 2300, a ruined future where the party finds the robot Robo. The companions are Marle, Lucca the inventor, Frog, Robo and Ayla. A place outside time, the End of Time, links the eras, until the party finds a time machine of its own.

The game plays with cause and effect. As *Super Play* explained, you can make something happen in one period, then go back to the same place in a later or earlier one and find the situation changed.

## Battles on the map

*Nintendo Power* explained the combat to American readers in July 1995: "The difference is that battles take place in the world in which you move around, not on separate battle screens. This means that you can skirt around enemies and avoid fights altogether." Fights were still chosen from menus, with a gauge for each character like the one in *Final Fantasy III* (the American name for *Final Fantasy VI*), and they could run in real time or wait for your orders, a version of Square's [Active Time Battle](/vault/techniques/active-time-battle).

Two things were new. Where the enemies stood on the screen now mattered, as *Edge* noted, and two or three characters could combine their special techniques into one Combo attack, Crono and Frog's together, or Crono and Lucca's. *Edge* saw it as a real effort by Square to rework the turn-based fight.

## Endings, and starting again

Lavos can be fought early, long before the story expects it, and the ending changes with the moment you choose. *Nintendo Power* reported in July 1995 that "more than ten endings have been discovered so far"; its review the next month said ten. Once the game is finished it can be started again with the same, stronger party, which *Nintendo Power* described as starting over "with a powered-up Crono". The menu option was called "New Game +", and *Super Play* was still explaining to readers in 1996 how to use it to reach the endings that turn on Frog's story.

## The music

Yasunori Mitsuda was a sound programmer at Square when he was given the score. He later recalled that he had gone to Sakaguchi saying he would quit unless he could compose, and Sakaguchi answered: "There's a project starting right now actually, that I was thinking of entrusting to you." Mitsuda said he knew the game "was expected to sell 2 million copies", and that while writing it he developed "an extremely painful stomach ulcer"; Nobuo Uematsu, *Final Fantasy*'s composer, "supported me and wrote a few songs".

*Nintendo Power* called it "a fresh and innovative sound track", already "slated to become a CD in Japan". Not everyone agreed about every piece: *Super Play*'s reviewer found the music superb at times but the battle theme, the tune heard most, "quite weak".

## Reception

*Nintendo Power* gave it the full treatment in August 1995: "a gem—polished, brilliant and beautifully presented. It belongs in every game library." Its short verdict was "You'll be spoiled for every other RPG."

British magazines had to review imports, and they were more measured. *Super Play* thought it "maybe not quite the earth-shattering monumental game I was expecting", but "a worthy addition to the Square library" and worth buying the moment it reached America. *Edge* reviewed the American version in November 1995 and gave it seven out of ten. It found the game "more lightweight than Final Fantasy in almost every department", though "a welcome oasis in the barren wasteland of translated Japanese RPGs".

## Later versions and legacy

The game kept selling. A New Zealand magazine reported in 1999 that the Super Famicom version had sold more than 2.5 million copies. That year Square brought it to the PlayStation in Japan with new animated scenes, and released the sequel, *Chrono Cross*, written by Masato Kato. The PlayStation version reached America in 2001 as part of *Final Fantasy Chronicles*. A Nintendo DS version followed in 2008, overseen by Kato, with a revised translation, two new areas and a new ending. By March 2025 Square Enix counted more than five million copies shipped across all versions.

Its influence shows in the games that set out to recreate it. Thierry Boulanger, whose studio Sabotage made the retro RPG *Sea of Stars*, told *Wireframe* in 2020: "Chrono Trigger is my yearly pilgrimage. And every time, I notice new details, and how seamless it is."
