*Ocarina of Time* had to solve a problem every earlier Zelda had avoided: how do you fight with a sword when the enemy can be anywhere around you in three dimensions? Nintendo's answer was to let the player hold the N64 pad's Z trigger near an enemy. "Holding the Z-trigger when close enough to an enemy activates a thirdperson, locked-on perspective," *Edge* explained, "with strafing a simple matter of moving left or right." The camera stayed on the target, Link circled it, and the fight became something you could read. "Z-targeting" was copied for years afterwards. In 2011 *Edge* could still say that Ocarina "proved Nintendo's Z-targeting had successfully tamed the wild frontier of 3D thirdperson combat".

The game took three years and a large team, and it arrived late in 1998 to reviews few games have matched. *Edge* gave it ten out of ten and called it "a work of genius".

## Three years in the making

The game was first meant for the 64DD, Nintendo's planned magnetic-disk drive for the N64; *Edge* reported in 1997 that it had been "originally slated for a 64DD release" before it moved to cartridge. It became one of the largest projects Nintendo had attempted. *Edge*'s Christmas 1998 editorial counted "a core team of nearly 50 staff" over three years, about 120 people including peripheral staff, and Nintendo's own motion-capture studio, where almost every animation was recorded and "technicians even built an artificial horse". The game had "four separate directors, each dedicated to a specific element such as character creation or combat", and only when their pieces were done could Miyamoto, as producer, bring it together.

Miyamoto described the arrangement to *Nintendo Power* in the summer of 1998: "I was a director for the development of Mario 64, but I'm the producer this time. There are four different directors for Zelda. I have to listen to the opinions and ideas of each of them. It's a tough job coordinating all of their efforts." The sword fighting was captured from a professional actor who did samurai sword stunts at Eiga Mura, a film-studio theme park in Kyoto. Yoshiaki Koizumi, described by the British magazine *Arcade* as Zelda's graphics director, explained the lighting: "Light source calculation is being done for every frame."

[Eiji Aonuma](/vault/people/eiji-aonuma), who had joined Nintendo in 1988 and directed his own Zelda-like game, *Marvelous*, was mainly responsible for the dungeon design. He later traced the dungeons to an interest in "the relationship between architecture and 3D shapes". *Edge* would remember him as "the chief architect of Ocarina's notorious Water Temple".

## Seven years apart

Link starts the game as a boy in the Kokiri forest. He collects three gems and the Ocarina of Time and opens the Temple of Time, and then the story jumps forward. "Link finds himself in a changed world seven years later," *Nintendo Power* explained before release, and must travel "back and forth in time" to finish the quest. The magazine was keen to correct one misunderstanding: "the Ocarina isn't the agent of time travel at all. Rather, it is the Master Sword that alters Link's time line."

The ocarina still mattered. Link plays "simple ditties" on it with the pad's yellow C buttons at key points in the game, and *Edge* thought that "an ingenious touch that adds a new dimension to gameplay". Hyrule itself impressed the magazine most: "rising and setting suns, flowing rivers, waterfalls, lakes, deserts, towns", vast open expanses "with no pop-up", sandstorms and "the melting hues of sunrise and sunset". Jumping was handled automatically, "a conscious attempt to differentiate the gameplay from Mario's". Its only real complaint was the "occasionally ponderous bouts of text". The review closed: "the game singlehandedly restores the faith in both the creative might of Nintendo and in the power of the videogame as an entertainment medium. A work of genius."

## Reception and sales

The game sold quickly everywhere. *Edge* reported first-week sales in Japan of "a reported 386,234 copies", ahead of Konami's *Metal Gear Solid*, and American pre-orders of 325,000. In the UK it went on sale on 11 December 1998 with an initial 250,000 cartridges. A month later *Edge* noted that it had taken 39 days to become the best-selling game of 1998 in the United States.

*Edge*'s ten was only the third it had given. By the magazine's tenth birthday in 2003 the list stood at four: *[Super Mario 64](/vault/games/super-mario-64)*, *Gran Turismo*, *Ocarina of Time* and *[Halo](/vault/games/halo)*. The review aggregator Metacritic gives the game a score of 99 from 22 critics.

## Later versions

A remixed version, *Master Quest*, was a leftover from a planned 64DD expansion, *Ura Zelda*, that never came out; Nintendo released it on the GameCube as a promotional bonus alongside *The Wind Waker*, with the dungeons rearranged. In 2011 Grezzo remade the game for the Nintendo 3DS as *Ocarina of Time 3D*, released on 17 June in Britain. *Edge* found it "inch-for-inch Ocarina", with sharper textures and models redrawn to match the original concept art, and the lock-on moved to the L button: "L-targeting (migrating from N64's Z)".
