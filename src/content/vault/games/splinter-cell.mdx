Most action games light the way forward. *Splinter Cell* asks you to put the lights out. Its hero, Sam Fisher, is safest where the screen is darkest, and a meter tells you how visible he is to the guards. Shooting out a lamp, or finding the light switch in time, became, in *[Edge](/vault/magazines/edge)*'s words, "an essential skill". Ubi Soft's Montreal studio built it for the Xbox around a real-time lighting system that its own art director doubted would run. It reached the shops in November 2002, up against the Xbox version of Konami's *Metal Gear Solid 2*, and *[Computer Gaming World](/vault/magazines/computer-gaming-world)* thought it had overtaken its rival to become "the darling of both consumers and critics".

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** Ubi Soft Montreal; the PlayStation 2 version by Ubisoft's Shanghai studio.
- **Publisher:** [Ubi Soft](/vault/companies/ubisoft), under the Tom Clancy name.
- **Released:** [Xbox](/vault/systems/microsoft-xbox), November 2002; PC early in 2003; [PlayStation 2](/vault/systems/sony-playstation-2) and GameCube in 2003.
- **Hero:** Sam Fisher, voiced by Michael Ironside, an agent of the National Security Agency's fictional "Third Echelon".
- **Engine:** Epic's [Unreal Engine](/vault/tools/unreal-engine).

## Making it

In August 2000 Ubi Soft bought Tom Clancy's "multimedia outfit", as *Edge* called Red Storm Entertainment, and Ubi Soft's Montreal studio set out to make a new stealth game under the Clancy name. Clancy himself did not work on it. "Even though he wasn't involved in this development directly," said Mathieu Ferland, the game's senior producer, "the Clancy universe was a very, very important guideline for us." He told *Edge* how the team used the name as a test: "Each decision we were making was: 'Is it Clancy? Does it feel serious, realistic, not too futuristic?'" Developers went on courses with the local police's tactical response group, and weapons consultants showed them police tasers, which became Fisher's "sticky shocker", a non-lethal round for his rifle.

The game's look came from its technology. *Splinter Cell* was planned as an Xbox exclusive, using the console's power and Epic's Unreal Engine for dynamic lighting. Hugo Dallaire, the art director, remembered that "most people were sceptical that this powerful system could run in realtime, including me", and the frame rate was still "in terrible shape" two weeks before the deadline. Lighting also changed how the levels were designed. "As soon as lights can be switched on and off and can cause the player to be detected or provide places for him to hide," said Clint Hocking, a designer on the game, "then suddenly how the level is lit is more important to the level designer than it is to the artist."

Fisher's signature move arrived late. The split jump, in which he braces himself between two walls and waits for a guard to pass beneath, was added after one of Ubisoft's marketing artists drew it for a magazine cover. "There were only a dozen places to use it in the game," Ferland admitted, but it became, in *Edge*'s words, "the perfect signature move".

## Playing it

Fisher works alone, with "a few voices in his earpiece" for company, through nine missions. *Computer Gaming World* listed their settings as "Georgian police stations and Chinese embassies to CIA headquarters and besieged oil rigs". He can see in the dark with night-vision and thermal goggles, peer under doors with a fibre-optic camera, hang from pipes, climb poles, and grab guards to question them. *Edge* thought the goggles gave the game "a unique and remarkable visual character", from the video grain of the night vision to a set piece in a freezer room seen through the thermal imaging. Its sniper rifle had a breath meter: holding the trigger made Fisher hold his breath, and for a few seconds his aim stopped drifting.

*Computer Gaming World* singled out the "onscreen visibility meter", which "lets you know just how hard it is for enemies to spot you, making stealth-work less of a guessing game and more of a science". It also noticed the noise: "How fast you move determines how much noise you make."

*Edge* gave the Xbox version seven out of ten. It saw the debt to Hideo Kojima's games, praised the light and the set pieces, and disliked the trial-and-error sections, the clumsy aiming and a linear route through every level. Asked whether *Splinter Cell* was "an MGS2-killer for the Xbox", *Edge* thought not, but called it "an often compelling and creative attempt to push the genre in new directions". *Computer Gaming World* was keener on the PC version: "one of the best stealth-action games ever". The Australian *PlayStation 2* magazine noted that the PS2 version, made in Shanghai, added a new mission in a power plant and more than 30 minutes of new cut-scenes, and called it "one of the best PS2 games so far". It sold strongly too: Hocking recalled seeing early sales "numbers that were higher than Halo".

## The series

*Pandora Tomorrow* (2004) added Spies vs Mercs, a multiplayer mode that set stealthy spies, played in the third person, against heavily armed mercenaries seen in the first person. *Edge* later pointed out that it was designed in France, by Gunther Galipot and Ubisoft's Annecy team. *Chaos Theory* followed in 2005 and *Double Agent* in 2006.

*Conviction* (2010) changed direction. *Edge* found that "as Splinter Cell goes from covert to overt, a new-found dynamism emerges", though "the much-loved Spies Vs Mercs mode is absent". *Blacklist* (2013), made by Ubisoft Toronto, brought Spies vs Mercs back and gave players the choice between stealth and open combat. In December 2021 Ubisoft announced a remake of the first game, also led by Ubisoft Toronto and built on its Snowdrop engine. An animated series, *Splinter Cell: Deathwatch*, with Liev Schreiber voicing a "visibly older" Fisher, began on Netflix on 14 October 2025.
