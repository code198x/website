## Overview

[Mike Singleton](/vault/people/mike-singleton)'s *Lords of Midnight*, published by [Beyond](/vault/companies/beyond-software) in 1984, gives the player a world of some **4,000 locations** viewable from **eight directions** on a 48K Spectrum — and does it by storing a map and drawing the view when you look.

## Fast facts

- **Author:** Mike Singleton. **Publisher:** Beyond.
- **Two games in one:** a quest (get Morkin to the icecrown) and a wargame (hold Xajorkith with Luxor's armies), each interfering with the other.
- ***Sinclair User***, September 1984: "it is claimed that there are **32,000 possible views**… the response time is excellent, with **pictures generated almost instantaneously**".

⭐ **Two years in the charts, and a change of category.** It enters the all-games Top 50 at #2, spends 1985 in the general Hotline chart, and by late 1986 is back at #4 — in the **Adventure** chart. The game did not change; the market's idea of what it was did, as the arcade tide came in and long strategic games were reclassified as a specialist taste.

That trajectory is the argument for recording chart runs, not peaks. A peak of #2 tells you it sold; the shape tells you it kept selling for two years and was re-shelved on the way.

## Landscaping

The technique is the reason the game exists at all. Storing 32,000 pictures is impossible by three orders of magnitude on this hardware. Storing **a map plus a library of parts** — mountain, forest, tower, keep — and assembling each view on demand is not.

What it buys is a world that feels vastly larger than the tape, which is the effect players remember. What it costs is that every view is built from the same components, so the landscape is coherent instead of varied — a trade Singleton took deliberately, and the reason *Sinclair User* remarked on the speed and not the pictures.

The [companion entry](/vault/people/mike-singleton) covers the method; this one covers what it was for.

## The other half

*Sinclair User* also caught the design: two game types running at once, sharing a world and pulling against each other. Pressure on Morkin is pressure off the armies. Commanders have morale and **can decline to fight** — "if a commander is very frightened, he will be less likely to consider joining a battle".

A strategic dilemma produced by one rule, not by special cases, in 1984, on a machine with 48K.
