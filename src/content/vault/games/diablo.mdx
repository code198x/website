*Diablo* started life as a turn-based game. You took a step, and then the monsters took theirs. It was Blizzard that asked Condor, the small studio making it, to switch to real time, and David Brevik, who had been planning a game like it since school, made the change himself. "The moment I put the change in, clicked on a skeleton and my character walked over and smacked it to pieces, the clouds parted," he told *[Edge](/vault/magazines/edge)*. The finished game gave the player a town, a single dungeon of 16 levels that was built afresh for every new game, and a mouse. Left-click to walk, left-click to fight, left-click to pick up what the monster dropped. It shipped with a free way to play it over the internet, and it was a hit, though several of the reviewers who first played it were not convinced.

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** Condor, renamed Blizzard North when [Blizzard](/vault/companies/blizzard) bought it during development.
- **Publisher:** Blizzard Entertainment; Zablac in the UK at launch.
- **Released:** at the turn of 1997 for Windows 95. Blizzard's official date was 3 January 1997, but Brevik has said shops on the American West Coast were selling it on 31 December 1996. Later versions for the Macintosh and, from Electronic Arts, the [PlayStation](/vault/systems/sony-playstation).
- **Designers:** David Brevik (lead programmer), Erich Schaefer (art lead) and Max Schaefer.
- **Players:** one, or up to four over a network, a modem, a serial cable or Blizzard's free Battle.net service.
- **Classes:** Warrior, Rogue and Sorcerer.
- **Expansion:** *Hellfire* (1997), made by Synergistic Software for Sierra.

## Condor and the turn-based dungeon

Condor was founded in 1993 by Brevik and the brothers Max and Erich Schaefer. Its first job, on the day of its first meeting, came from Sunsoft: the Mega Drive version of *Justice League Task Force*. The Super Nintendo version was being written by a studio in Irvine that became Blizzard, and the two teams met at a trade show. Blizzard had just released *[Warcraft](/vault/games/warcraft)* and wanted more PC games, and Condor had one ready to pitch. "I started working on it in high school around 1985," Brevik told *Edge*. "The biggest influence was playing *Moria* and *Angband* on Unix machines." Erich Schaefer pointed to *[X-COM](/vault/games/x-com-ufo-defense)* instead, for "the size of the characters, the camera angle and the tile-based random maps". For a short time the team also considered making the characters out of clay and photographing them, as *Primal Rage* had; Max Schaefer later called it something that "would have been a disaster".

The first version worked like the [roguelikes](/vault/genres/roguelike) Brevik played, *[Rogue](/vault/games/rogue)* and *[NetHack](/vault/games/nethack)*: everything happened in turns. Blizzard suggested real time. The two tellings of the switch differ in length. *Edge* reported in 2010 that Brevik locked himself in his office for a week to rewrite the code. At the Game Developers Conference in 2016 Brevik said that he first asked Blizzard for a milestone payment, got it, and then had real-time combat working within a few hours: every action already took a number of turns, "so I just made the turns happen 20 times a second, and it all worked magically".

## Blizzard North and Battle.net

About six months before the game was due, Condor ran short of money. "The taxman was literally at the door, threatening to shut us down," Erich Schaefer told *Edge*, and Blizzard offered to buy the studio. Max Schaefer said the budget until then had been "under half a million dollars". With Blizzard's backing they asked how to make the game "as big as possible", and the answer was Battle.net, built by Blizzard's own team in Irvine. *Diablo* had been designed for play over a local network; Battle.net let a player click a menu button and join people anywhere in the world, at no charge beyond an internet connection.

*[Computer Gaming World](/vault/magazines/computer-gaming-world)* found the setup "unbelievably painless". Four months after launch *PC Zone* reckoned that about 150,000 people were playing on Battle.net, and warned British readers that two hours a night would cost around £40 a month in phone calls, since there was not yet a European server. The same article described what the players had made of it: traders, clans, bounty hunters, and "Player Killers" who turned on their own parties and took their victims' ears as trophies. Games were hosted on the players' own machines, and edited characters were common. "It was foreign to us to have a hit game, to have a million people playing," Condor's founders told *Edge*. "It meant that thousands of people were cheating, and we were totally unprepared for it."

## Reviews

The British reviewers were cool. *Edge* gave it seven out of ten in February 1997 and called it "essentially a *[Gauntlet](/vault/games/gauntlet)* clone" that had also borrowed its random dungeons from *Rogue*. It admired the light-sourcing and the monsters' intelligence, which could make small creatures flee when a comrade died, but concluded that "it's a shame that Blizzard doesn't appear to have the creative flair and imagination to complement its unquestionable talent for producing slick, clever games". *PC Gamer* gave it 74%. Its reviewer, Steve Faragher, also called it a *Gauntlet* clone, "fun but unoriginal", and thought it had been designed for Americans with free local phone calls; it was "well worth buying if you can get at the multi-player side of things". *PC Zone*'s buyers' guide later rated it 88: "standard hack 'n' slash fare, but it's pulled off with such style that the overall game experience is addictive."

*Computer Gaming World*'s headline in March 1997 crowned it "the new king of action-RPGs". Greg Fortune praised the interface and the combat, and a panel alongside his review wished the town of Tristram felt more like a place where people lived. Multiplayer, he wrote, made it "an instant must have for everyone on the staff". The designer of *[Ultima](/vault/games/ultima)*, [Richard Garriott](/vault/people/richard-garriott), told *Edge* that it was "fabulously beautiful", but "fantasy C&C", a hack-and-slash game rather than a role-playing game in the *Ultima* sense.

## Sales

Blizzard said in September 1998 that *Diablo* had been the best-selling game in its first month in the shops and the best-selling role-playing game of 1997, and had sold "nearly 2 million copies worldwide". *Computer Gaming World* counted it among Blizzard's "back-to-back million-sellers" later that year. *Edge*'s 2010 retrospective summed up what came next: "Massive sales followed, and with them came clones, expansions and a sequel."

## Hellfire, the PlayStation and the sequels

The expansion, *Hellfire*, did not come from Blizzard. Synergistic Software made it for Sierra, and *Computer Gaming World* reviewed it in April 1998. It added a Monk character, new areas, faster walking in town and more items, but no multiplayer. The PlayStation version, published by Electronic Arts in 1998, replaced the mouse with direct control from the joypad and let two players share a screen. *Edge* thought it "a relatively painless introduction to the often confusing world of computer roleplaying games".

Blizzard North made *Diablo II* (2000). In June 2003 Brevik, the Schaefers and Blizzard's Bill Roper left to found Flagship Studios, which made *Hellgate: London*; the Schaefers went on to make *Torchlight*. Blizzard continued the series with *Diablo III* (2012) and *Diablo IV* (2023), which Blizzard announced as its fastest-selling game. The original returned in March 2019 on GOG.com, bundled with *Hellfire* and in two versions: the original, and one updated for modern Windows.
