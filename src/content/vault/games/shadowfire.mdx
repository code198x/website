Written by **Denton Designs** for [Beyond](/vault/companies/beyond-software): a real-time squad rescue in which you command five characters aboard an enemy ship — and give every order through **icons**, with no typing at all.

- ***CRASH*** describes it as "*Shadowfire*, **the first fully icon**[-driven]" game.
- **Sequel:** *Enigma Force*, also icon-driven.

## Getting rid of the parser

In 1985 a game with depth meant a game you **typed at**, and typing at a game meant guessing which verb the author had implemented. "GET LAMP" works; "PICK UP LAMP" does not; the player is playing the parser, not the world.

*Shadowfire* removes the problem by removing the words. Every action is an icon, every target is selected, and **nothing can be phrased wrongly**. What you lose is expressive range; what you gain is that the interface stops being an obstacle and the game can run in real time, which a typed game cannot, because typing is slow.

That combination — real time, no text, a squad with individual skills, and a clock counting down — is why it stands out from everything around it, and why Beyond could sell a strategy game to the general chart instead of the adventure one.

## What removing the words cost
*Sinclair Programs* reviewed it and found the trade-off precisely, which no
other review in this Vault does.

Its opening is the strongest statement of the design anyone made at the time:

> Beyond have taken the traditional text adventure, smashed it to pieces, thrown
> it on the scrapheap and put something radically different in its place.
> *Shadowfire* is a completely text free adventure, allowing quicker decisions,
> faster movement and more action than ever before.

Then the complaint, and it is not the one you would predict:

> First impression of *Shadowfire* is of a complex, unapproachable game. Using
> no text in the game has meant that the **instruction manual is difficult to
> follow**, especially without the game in front of you.

The difficulty did not disappear when the parser did. It **moved into the
documentation**. A verb-noun game can be explained in a sentence because the
reader already speaks the language; a game of thirty on-screen icons has to
teach its own vocabulary before anything else can happen, and a printed manual
is a poor place to do that.

The reviewer's conclusion is the part worth keeping, because it vindicates the
design and indicts the manual in the same breath: the "initial panic caused by
complicated instructions is **quite unjustified**, for the icons are, in
practice, far more easy to use" than the alternative.

The same review confirms the clock independently of *CRASH* — "you have only an
hour and forty minutes" — and records that as many as **thirty images** can be
on screen at once.

## Denton Designs

Denton were ex-[Imagine](/vault/companies/imagine-software) people who set up in Liverpool after that company's collapse, and they appear in the *CRASH* reader ranking of Ocean and Imagine talent as a **programming house in their own right**, credited with *The Great Escape* and *Where Time Stood Still*.

An icon interface, a real-time squad and two of the era's best-regarded Spectrum games is a strong body of work for a studio formed out of a bankruptcy.
