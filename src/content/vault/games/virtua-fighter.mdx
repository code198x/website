In 1994 *Edge* went looking for the reason Japanese arcades had gone quiet around one cabinet. *Virtua Fighter*, it found, had been "snubbed by many as being clumsy and shallow" when it appeared, and now Japanese coin-op fans had "gone crazy over the game". Its fighters were built from flat-shaded polygons, blocky next to the hand-drawn sprites of *[Street Fighter II](/vault/games/street-fighter-ii)*, but they moved like people. A camera swooped around them, a thrown wrestler landed with his weight, and a fighter knocked out of the ring lost the round. When Sega put the game on its new [Saturn](/vault/systems/sega-saturn) that November, *Edge* judged it the only worthwhile game at the launch.

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** [Sega AM2](/vault/companies/sega-am2), led by [Yu Suzuki](/vault/people/yu-suzuki).
- **Hardware:** Sega Model 1, the board that ran *Virtua Racing*.
- **Arcade release:** Japan, 6 December 1993, after a showing at the Amusement Machine Show that August.
- **Controls:** a joystick and three buttons: punch, kick and guard.
- **Fighters:** eight, plus a hidden boss, Dural.
- **Home versions:** Saturn (Japan, 22 November 1994; North America, May 1995; Europe, July 1995) and 32X (1995).
- **Sequels:** *Virtua Fighter 2* (1994, Model 2), *Virtua Fighter 3* (1996, Model 3), *Virtua Fighter 4* (2001), *Virtua Fighter 5* (2006); *Virtua Fighter Crossroads* announced for 2027.

## Fighters out of polygons

AM2 had put Sega into polygon 3D with *Virtua Racing* in 1992. After it, Suzuki told *Edge* in 1994, "we split AM2 into two departments — one team worked on *Virtua Fighters* while the other concentrated on *Daytona GP*". The fighting game stayed on the Model 1 board: an NEC V60 main processor, "actually pretty slow" at 16 MHz by Suzuki's account, with four Fujitsu signal processors doing the 3D maths, about 180,000 polygons a second, and a display of 496×384. The 3D was not new to fighting games: *Edge* called Distinctive Software's *4D Sports Boxing* for the PC "pioneering" and "polygon-based" in 1995. What was new was fighters that looked and moved like trained people, in an arcade, fast enough to fight with.

Speed cost detail. *Edge* asked why *Virtua Fighter* looked plainer than *Virtua Racing*, especially its backgrounds, when both ran on Model 1. "We doubled the frame rate in *Virtua Fighters*," Suzuki replied, "because fighting games require a faster response." Accounts of the result differ. *Edge* itself later described the 60 frames a second of *Virtua Fighter 2* as "twice the speed of the original", and Sega Retro gives the first game 30 frames a second, half *Virtua Racing*'s.

The movement came from people. AM2 modelled the fighters in SoftImage on Silicon Graphics workstations, and Suzuki said it had "also used SoftImage's Flock Of Birds motion capture system for the animation in *Virtua Fighters*". The arcade game had about 700 motions, he said, combined into what he called "an infinite variation". The first version shown in public was far from finished. Sega Retro, which has traced the development, dates serious work to Christmas Eve 1992 and says a 20%-complete version appeared at the Amusement Machine Show in August 1993, where *Edge* reported that "Sega's pride and joy, *Virtua Fighters*" had outshone *Super Street Fighter II*. The Japanese release followed on 6 December.

## Three buttons

*Street Fighter II* had six buttons. *Virtua Fighter* had three, "punch, kick and guard", and *Edge* thought that "doesn't compromise the gameplay, though. In fact, it amplifies it." The depth came from direction-and-button combinations: every fighter had at least ten special moves and most close to 20, *Edge* counted, with throws for when two fighters were close and attacks for a fighter lying on the ground.

The fighters moved on a single line, as in sprite-based games, and could not circle each other; only throws and falls sent them elsewhere in the arena. The arena was a raised square, and a fighter pushed off its edge lost the round by "ring out". *Edge* called the result "basically a 2D beat 'em up with 3D graphics", and praised its "elegant simplicity".

The eight fighters came from different martial arts, named in the game's character profiles:

| Fighter | Style in the game's profiles |
|---------|------------------------------|
| Akira Yuki | Hakkyoku-ken (Chinese Bajiquan) |

Beat all eight and the game sent in Dural, "a metallic polygon woman", as a final opponent.

## The Saturn version

Sega gave the conversion to AM2 itself. At the Tokyo Toy Show in June 1994 it showed a version *Edge* put at 30% complete, with "crude and blocky fighters" meant only as placeholders. The Saturn could draw about 90,000 to 100,000 polygons a second against Model 1's 180,000, *Edge* reported, and Suzuki had already told Sega's press brochure that "there will be a reduction in the number of polygons compared to the coin-op". His answer was the Saturn's ability to texture individual polygons, and a promise about the moves: "We can do it, and we've just discovered a trick to replicate the movements 100%."

The Saturn went on sale in Japan on 22 November 1994, and *Virtua Fighter* went with it at ¥7,800. *Edge* gave it nine out of ten. It called the game "Sega's most important release for years" and "arguably the first true 'next generation' console game". "In the arcades, *Virtua Fighter* made people stop and look," the review ended. "On the Saturn, it will make many people stop, look at their bank balance and then fork out for Sega's new machine." Its one complaint was that polygons sometimes disappeared and characters broke up in the replays.

The prediction came true in Japan. By the end of December the Saturn had sold 500,000 units against the PlayStation's 300,000, and *Edge* reported that "despite the technical superiority of the PlayStation, *Virtua Fighter* has got a firm grip on the nation's wallets". Sega's own American magazine, *Sega Visions*, claimed in April 1995 that Sega of Japan had sold as many copies of the game as it had Saturns. In the West the game came with the console: in the United States for $449 in May 1995, and in Europe, at £399, from 8 July 1995.

A version for the 32X, Sega's add-on for the Mega Drive, followed in 1995. *Computer & Video Games* said it "doesn't look as good as on Saturn" but "could be the best version yet", and a reader was told that "the animation is just as smooth, and there are NO glitches!"

## Sequels

*Virtua Fighter 2* (1994) moved to the [Model 2](/vault/techniques/model-2) board, with texture-mapped fighters at 60 frames a second. Suzuki unveiled *Virtua Fighter 3* in Tokyo on 26 July 1996, on the new Model 3 board, and it added a fourth button that let every character dodge into or out of the screen. *Virtua Fighter 4* was unveiled at the AOU show in February 2001 on Sega's Naomi 2 board, and its PlayStation 2 version of 2002 added a Kumite mode of "simulated arcade opponents and customizable characters", which *IGN Unplugged* thought gave it the edge over *Tekken 4*. AM2 readied *Virtua Fighter 5* for Sega's Lindbergh arcade board in 2006.

The first game's staff spread its influence. Seiichi Ishii, who had worked on *Virtua Racing* and *Virtua Fighter*, moved to Namco and worked on *[Tekken](/vault/games/tekken)* and *Tekken 2* before leading Dream Factory, which made *Tobal No. 1* for Square.

*Virtua Fighter 5 R.E.V.O.*, a remaster, reached the Switch 2 in March 2026, the series' first Nintendo release. A new game from Sega's Ryu Ga Gotoku Studio, the makers of the *Yakuza* series, was announced at The Game Awards in December 2024; in June 2026 it was named *Virtua Fighter Crossroads*, a story-driven game with a new fighter, Cielo, due in 2027.
