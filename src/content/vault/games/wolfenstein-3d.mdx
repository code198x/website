**Wolfenstein 3D** is [id Software’s](/vault/companies/id-software) 1992 first-person shooter, released through [Apogee](/vault/companies/apogee-software). You guide B. J. Blazkowicz through a Nazi stronghold, searching for exits while fighting guards and conserving ammunition and health.

Its [shareware](/vault/distribution/shareware) release gave players an episode to copy and try, with further episodes available to buy. That distribution connected a technically striking PC game to an audience that could pass it on directly.

## A useful restriction

The walls follow a square grid. A room can look convincing from inside even though its plan is far simpler than a freely modelled building. The engine uses that restriction to find visible walls quickly.

<Figure src="/images/vault/games/wolfenstein-3d/grid-and-view.png" alt="A top-down grid shows rays travelling from the player to walls. Nearby wall hits produce tall columns in the view; distant hits produce shorter columns." caption="Original schematic of grid raycasting. Wall distance determines the height of each screen column; this is an explanation, not a game capture." rendering="smooth" />

For each column of the view, a ray travels outward until it meets a wall. The distance determines how tall that wall column appears, and which strip of its texture to draw. Enemies and objects are drawn as scaled pictures within the scene. The released renderer separates the wall pass from those scaled objects.

## Speed through a designed space

This arrangement gives the game its directness: turn, read the next corridor, act. It also limits the architecture. The world cannot freely stack rooms or slope its floors as a later polygon environment can.

The team’s next major shooter, [Doom](/vault/games/doom), changed the representation to allow more varied spaces. [Ultima Underworld](/vault/games/ultima-underworld), also released in 1992, put a first-person view to a different purpose, combining exploration with conversation and role-playing systems.

John Carmack’s source-release notes describe the raycasting architecture and its compromises. That code makes the design inspectable: a small set of geometric rules supports the impression of a much larger place.
