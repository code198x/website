*Aladdin* on the Mega Drive was sold on a promise that it would look like the film. Disney's animators drew for it, and [Virgin Games](/vault/companies/virgin-games)' team in California worked out how to get their drawings onto a 16-megabit cartridge. Before release, *Computer Gaming World* reported that "all of the movement in the game looks like the animation in the film".

Sega, which published it, expected to sell more than a million. Tim Chaney, who ran Virgin, said it earned the company more than $100 million, and Virgin's Stephen Clarke-Willson later put its sales at four million copies.

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** [Virgin Games](/vault/companies/virgin-games), USA.
- **Publisher:** [Sega](/vault/companies/sega), which distributed the game for Virgin.
- **Programming:** led by [David Perry](/vault/people/david-perry).
- **Released:** autumn 1993; *Edge* gave the British release as October, at £45.
- **Cartridge:** 16 megabits.
- **Structure:** eleven stages and three bonus rounds, by Sega's count.
- **Other versions:** Amiga 1200 (1994) and PC. Capcom made a different *Aladdin* for the Super NES.

## Disney's drawings on a Sega cartridge

Virgin launched the game at the summer Consumer Electronics Show in 1993. *C&VG* reported a "$250,000 breakfast launch hosted by Disney chairman Jeffrey Katzenberg" and a stand where "the sneak previews wowed many a passing punter". *Computer Gaming World* noted that the heads of all three companies, Disney, Sega and Virgin, turned up for a Mega Drive cartridge.

What impressed was the animation. *Computer Gaming World* reported that "Virgin has developed a process for bringing cel-based animation to the Genesis", and that Disney had let the animators who worked on the film give Virgin access to their cels. *Edge* later wrote that the game was drawn "with the help of 30 artists on loan from Disney", and *PC Gamer* that Perry's team "were sent six million dollars' worth of animation cells – with Disney drawing 2,000 especially for the video game". Sega's own magazine claimed film-quality cel animation as "a first for a cart-based game".

Getting the drawings into the machine was the hard part. *Retro Gamer* describes a technique called Digicel, which compressed the animation frames so that they would fit into the cartridge's small memory; Perry's own name for his approach to animation was "Animotion". Clarke-Willson remembered sending Virgin's animator Mike Dietz to Disney World "to work directly with the Disney animators there on game console animation timing, which is different from the timing used in traditional animation". By his account about fifty people made the game in about seven months, after a year of research into getting traditional animation into a console game.

## Playing it

Aladdin carries a scimitar for close fights and apples to throw at range. The stages follow the film: the Agrabah market, where palace guards, knife-throwing jugglers and snake charmers wait; the desert; the Cave of Wonders and a fast escape from it; the Sultan's dungeon; a magic-carpet ride; the inside of the Genie's lamp; and a final battle with Jafar. Sega's guide pointed out comic touches, such as a camel in the market that, when Aladdin jumps on it, fires "dirt wads" at the nearest guard. Genie tokens collected on the way paid for spins on a bonus machine, and an Abu token opened a bonus level.

*Edge* thought that in a crowded market a platform game had to "literally pounce from the screen, seize you by the throat and deny you air", and that "Aladdin does just that". It found the animation "astounding" and the whole game moving "so smoothly and so fast that it's easy to see where all those megs went". It noted that the sword fights "end very quickly", but there were so many that it hardly mattered.

## The other Aladdin

Capcom made its own *Aladdin* for the Super NES, released that December. Shinji Mikami, later the director of *Resident Evil*, was one of its planners. *Edge* was unimpressed by its seven sections, which it found too few and too easy, and it had heard that Capcom had gone back to improve its animation after seeing Virgin's game at the summer show. *Wireframe* noted in 2019 that the Super NES game was "less fondly remembered than Virgin Interactive's Mega Drive version", though it had since "built up a decent following".

## Success

*Retro Gamer* records that the Mega Drive game won a string of awards and was bundled with the console. Clarke-Willson credited Sega's distribution for much of its sales, "That alone could account for twice the sales", and put the final figure at four million. Its success, *Retro Gamer* says, persuaded Perry to leave Virgin and found his own studio, Shiny Entertainment, to keep more of the rewards of his work; its first game was *Earthworm Jim* (1994). Virgin followed *Aladdin* with *The Lion King* (1994), which *Amiga Format* called "Virgin's Aladdin-alike".

Virgin converted *Aladdin* to the Amiga 1200 in 1994, and to the PC. The Amiga version scored 92% in *The One* and in *Amiga Action*, 90% in *Amiga Format* and 86% in *Amiga Power*. In 2019 the Mega Drive game was re-released with *The Lion King* in *Disney Classic Games: Aladdin and The Lion King*.
