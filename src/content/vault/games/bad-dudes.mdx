*Bad Dudes* is remembered for one line. A Secret Service agent tells the two heroes that "President Ronnie has been kidnapped by the ninjas", then asks: "Are you a bad enough dude to rescue Ronnie?" When *Wireframe* looked back at [Data East](/vault/companies/data-east) in 2019, it called the game "a legend more for its out-of-game antics than the in-game action", famous for its name and its "all-too-subtle use of 'President Ronnie'". The game behind the line was a solid two-player brawler of 1988. Its heroes fought across trucks, sewers and a speeding train, and the reward at the end was a burger with the President.

## Fast facts

- **Developer and publisher:** [Data East](/vault/companies/data-east), arcade, 1988.
- **Titles:** *DragonNinja* in Japan; *Bad Dudes Vs. DragonNinja* in North America; *Dragon Ninja* on British home computers.
- **Players:** two at once.
- **Stages:** seven, from the city streets to an underground factory.
- **First boss:** Karnov, the fire-breather from Data East's own *Karnov*.
- **Home versions:** Ocean's Imagine label (Spectrum, Commodore 64, Amstrad CPC, Amiga, Atari ST); Data East (IBM PC, Apple II, Commodore 64 in America; NES, 1989).

## In the arcade

*DragonNinja* appeared in Japan in the spring of 1988 and reached British arcades that summer. *Computer and Video Games*' arcade reporter Clare Edgeley recommended it as much for the gameplay as for the graphics, and warned that "it takes two players and a hell of a lot of 10 pences to defeat each deadly foe". *The Games Machine* thought the second player gave a familiar formula "much needed boost" by allowing for teamwork.

The two heroes, named Blade and Striker, punch, kick and jump, and can hold back a punch to release a charged blow. Most stages have two walkways, an upper and a lower, and the fighters jump between them. Defeated ninjas drop knives, chains and other weapons, and energy and time pick-ups. The route runs from the city streets to the tops of speeding lorries, then through a sewer, a forest, a freight train and a cave to the factory where the President is held.

Each stage ends with a boss. The first is Karnov, the bald, fire-breathing strongman of Data East's arcade game *Karnov*, whom *Your Sinclair* called "a wobbly bellied geezer". The last stage sends the earlier bosses back in a line, before a final fight with the DragonNinja on a helicopter in flight. *Retro Gamer* described that ending: the President steps out of the landed helicopter, and instead of a kiss for the rescuers there is a promise of burgers.

## Home versions

In Britain, Ocean converted the game under its [Imagine](/vault/companies/imagine-software) label as *Dragon Ninja*, and the versions came out around the turn of 1989. *Zzap!64* gave the Commodore 64 version 87 per cent. Its programmers, among them Steve Wahid, with music by [Jonathan Dunn](/vault/people/jonathan-dunn), had captured whatever it was that made the arcade game appealing, one reviewer wrote, and the scrolling truck section stood out; the only real gripes were the missing two-player mode and the lack of sound during play. *Your Sinclair*'s Peter Shaw walked through all seven Spectrum levels and thought it would be one of the best ninja games of the season, though the Spectrum lost touches such as the ninjas' changing faces.

*CRASH* disagreed, giving the Spectrum version 46 per cent. The small sprites, about half the size of *Double Dragon*'s, made room for the two floors, and the 128K machine loaded every level at once. But the two-player option had gone, and so had the difficulty: crouching and punching beat practically any enemy, and one reviewer reached the seventh level on his second go. Mark Jones, an Ocean artist, later recalled that he "helped out with Dragon Ninja, which didn't look very good and got bad reviews". Commodore 64 owners took to it: in May 1989 *Zzap!*'s readers voted it into the top ten of their Commodore 64 chart.

In America, Data East published the computer versions itself as *Bad Dudes*. *Computer Gaming World* reviewed it in May 1989 with *Double Dragon* and *Renegade*, and found the IBM PC version the best of the three for graphics and action, while the Apple II version "suffered greatly" from the machine's limited graphics. The NES version followed in 1989, published by Data East, and was in *Nintendo Power*'s readers' top 30 by that summer; the console version dropped the President's first name.
