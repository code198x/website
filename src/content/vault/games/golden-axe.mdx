In the summer of 1989 Sega, *The One* noted, was "formerly known mostly for its hydraulic stomach-turners": arcade cabinets such as *After Burner* that threw the player about in a moving seat. *Golden Axe* was a different kind of machine. It took the side-on street fight of *[Double Dragon](/vault/games/double-dragon)*, moved it into a sword-and-sorcery world, and gave the player magic to collect and monsters to ride. "The gameplay is first rate," *The One* decided, "a real improvement over *Double Dragon*." Within a year Sega had squeezed it on to a home cartridge for the [Mega Drive](/vault/systems/sega-mega-drive), and by the end of 1990 it was on every British home computer that mattered.

## Fast facts

- **Developer and publisher:** [Sega](/vault/companies/sega). Makoto Uchida, who had made the arcade *[Altered Beast](/vault/games/altered-beast)*, is credited with the arcade *Golden Axe*.
- **Arcade:** 1989, on Sega's System 16B board, with a Motorola 68000 running the game.
- **Players:** one or two at once.
- **Mega Drive:** Japan, December 1989; Britain, 1990. It added a Duel mode and a practice round.
- **Home computers:** Virgin Mastertronic, 1990, for the Amiga, Atari ST, Commodore 64, Spectrum and Amstrad CPC.

## Three heroes and Death Adder

Death Adder has seized the kingdom, kidnapped its king and princess and killed members of each hero's family. *The Games Machine* introduced the three who set out after him when it previewed the coin-op in September 1989:

| Hero | Weapon | Magic |
|------|--------|-------|
| Ax Battler, a barbarian | Sword | Earth: an earthquake |
| Tyris Flare, an Amazon | Sword | Fire |
| Gilius Thunderhead, a dwarf | Axe | Lightning |

The choice mattered. *Edge*, looking back at the coin-op in 2003, found the dwarf tougher and the Amazon "blessed with greater magic power". *ACE* counted the levels: up to four of earth magic for Ax, six of fire for Tyris and three of lightning for Gilius. *Zero* put the trade-off more bluntly: Gilius "is best in combat but his magic potential is dismal".

## Magic, mounts and thieves

The magic is a smart bomb. "Magic is effectively a smart bomb, burning, burying or electrifying everything on screen when summoned," wrote *The One*. Its strength depends on how many potion jars the player is carrying, and the jars come from small thieves, called elves in some reviews and imps in others, who run across the screen and drop a potion when kicked. Between stages the heroes camp for the night, and the thieves come back to steal what they can. *Edge* thought it "equally possible to lose precious health or magic tokens while the party sleeps".

Some enemies ride beasts, and knocking a rider off lets a hero climb on. *The Games Machine* described "fire-spitting dragons, or a strange creature called a Chicken Legs which whips you with its tail"; a *GamePro* guide to the Mega Drive version listed three mounts, the tail-whipping chicken-legged beast, a fire-breathing dragon and a dragon that spits fireballs.

The fighting itself was more than a punch button. *Edge* liked the way the run and jump combined with the attack to give "barges, flying kicks and falling strikes", which gave a choice of how to deal with a crowd. In two-player mode the heroes can hurt each other: "Not only can you damage the enemy," *Zero* warned, "but your partner too."

*Edge*'s 2003 retrospective gave the coin-op seven out of ten. It found the game got repetitive, with "fewer and fewer surprises as it advances", but concluded that "playing as Sega intended — short and sharp — is no bad way to part with ten pence".

## On the Mega Drive

The Mega Drive version came out in Japan at the end of 1989 and reached Britain as an import in early 1990, at around £30. *Zero*'s review in April 1990 offered a test: "If you were to enclose a Mega Drive in a coin-op cabinet and place the system in an amusement arcade, few people would notice the difference. It really is that good." *ZZAP!64* went further that autumn, telling its readers that "*Golden Axe* on Megadrive beats the coin-op!", though "for a price": the console cost £189.99.

The cartridge added things the arcade did not have. *GamePro*'s guide described "a Duel round that lets you battle different enemies one on one in a coliseum, as well as a beginner practice round". When Sega launched the Mega Drive officially in Britain in the autumn of 1990, *Golden Axe* was at the top of *Mean Machines*' Mega Drive chart: the magazine's first issue reported that it "has sliced and diced its way to the top", ahead of *Ghouls 'n' Ghosts* and *The Revenge of Shinobi*.

## The home computer conversions

Virgin Mastertronic, which distributed Sega's consoles in Britain, also held the rights to convert its arcade games. *ACE* reviewed the results in December 1990, and Neil Young, a software producer at [Probe Software](/vault/companies/probe-software), explained the approach: "Our brief was not to enhance or modify *Golden Axe*, but stay within the limits of the target machine."

- **Amiga:** written by the Dementia team, with Richard Costello programming, Kevin Bulmer leading the graphics and [David Whittaker](/vault/people/david-whittaker) adapting the music from the coin-op. *Amiga Action* reported that the backdrops were drawn by hand rather than digitised, and that the final level, with too many sprites to fit at once, was split in two, making six stages instead of five. *ACE* thought it "very close to the original coin-op" but found the play repetitive.
- **Commodore 64:** programmed by Mark Kelly with graphics by [Steve Crow](/vault/people/steve-crow). When *ZZAP!64* visited Probe's Croydon office, Kelly said Sega had given them very little to work from, so he played through the whole coin-op while Crow photographed the screens. "Memory is the biggest killer," he explained, "so we had to make the dragons vanish when you're hit."
- **Spectrum and Amstrad CPC:** programmed by David Shea, with graphics by Jason Green.

In Germany the Amiga version was indexed by the federal office that judged media harmful to young people, which meant it could not be advertised or sold openly. Torsten Oppermann, who worked in marketing at Sega's German arm, recalled that it was "deemed too violent".

## Sequels

*Golden Axe Warrior* (1991) turned the series into a top-down adventure for the [Master System](/vault/systems/sega-master-system); *Computer and Video Games* warned that it was "in fact a sprawling medieval RPG" and gave it 42 per cent. *Golden Axe II* followed on the Mega Drive in 1991, an arcade sequel, *Golden Axe: The Revenge of Death Adder*, in 1992, and *Golden Axe III* on the Mega Drive in 1993. *Golden Axe: The Duel* later turned the series into a one-on-one fighting game. In 2006 *Edge* reported that Secret Level, a studio Sega had just bought, was making a new *Golden Axe* for the PlayStation 3 and Xbox 360; it came out as *Golden Axe: Beast Rider*.
