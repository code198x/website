In the autumn of 1984 Acornsoft took half a dozen journalists to Cambridge, locked them in a room with a BBC Micro and a game they were not allowed to take home. "It is still so secret that even I don't have a copy!" wrote Tony Quinn in *[Acorn User](/vault/magazines/acorn-user)*. What he had played was *Elite*: a 3D space game in which you flew a trading ship between thousands of planets, bought low, sold high, fought pirates and slowly earned a combat rank. There was no score and no ending. Its authors, [Ian Bell](/vault/people/ian-bell) and [David Braben](/vault/people/david-braben), were two undergraduates at Jesus College, Cambridge.

## Fast facts

- **Authors:** Ian Bell and David Braben.
- **Publisher:** [Acornsoft](/vault/companies/acornsoft) for the [BBC Micro](/vault/systems/bbc-micro) and Acorn Electron; [Firebird](/vault/companies/firebird) for most other home computers.
- **Launched:** September 1984, at Thorpe Park.
- **Price at launch:** £14.95 on cassette and £17.65 on disc for the BBC Micro, £12.95 for the Electron (Acornsoft's advertisement).
- **The universe:** eight galaxies of 256 star systems each, generated by the program instead of stored.
- **Ranks:** nine, from "Harmless" to "Elite".
- **Sequel:** *Frontier: Elite II* (1993); the modern series continues with *[Elite Dangerous](/vault/games/elite-dangerous)*.

## A universe in 22K

*Elite* puts you in the seat of a Cobra Mk III, docked at a space station above the planet Lave with 100 credits to spend. You buy a cargo, launch, and fly to another system within your seven-light-year fuel range. Food sells well on rich industrial worlds; computers sell well on poor agricultural ones. Narcotics and firearms pay better still, but trading in them brings the police. On the way there are pirates, bounty hunters and the alien Thargoids, and every kill moves your rating a little further from "Harmless".

All of it had to fit in a 32K machine that also needed memory for its screen. Bell told *Acorn Programs* in December 1984, "If the BBC had more memory, we would probably still be writing it. As it is, there is not a single free byte in the program." Braben called it the BBC's "hideous shortage of memory". Looking back in 2000, Bell remembered the struggle of "compressing all the data down to 22K".

The idea started as a 3D combat game. "I wanted to follow that with a 3D simulation game and it seemed logical to set it in space," Bell said, following his earlier Acornsoft game *Freefall*. "Then Braben added the trading ship scenario and things just developed from there." Braben later explained why: flying about and shooting "felt very empty… To make it a satisfying experience we had to have some motivation. That's where the trading game came from."

## Galaxies from a few numbers

*Elite* does not store its planets. Each system is described by three 16-bit numbers, six bytes in all, called seeds. The program derives the next system's seeds from the current one with a repeated step, which Mark Moxon's annotated edition of the source calls "twisting" and bases on the Tribonacci series (a relative of the Fibonacci sequence in which each number is the sum of the three before it). Bits of each seed then become the system's name, position on the chart, economy, government and technology level. The same seeds always give the same system, so Lave is still Lave when you come back, and each galaxy costs almost no memory.

Acornsoft's advertisements promised "2,000 planets in eight galaxies". The first design had many more. In 2000 *Edge* reported that the galaxies "originally numbered two to the power of 48", cut to eight after Acornsoft, "not completely happy with every single design feature", had its say. "Ridiculous numbers of galaxies," Braben said. "I like the idea that you're in a Douglas Adams-type universe – an insignificant dot on an insignificant dot feeling – but you don't want too much of that sort of thing."

## Two screen modes at once

The BBC Micro version shows its space view in two colours and its dashboard in four. A BBC Micro normally shows one screen mode at a time, and the choice is set in the Video ULA's control register (the chip that turns screen memory into pixels). Acorn's *Advanced User Guide* lists the value for each mode. Mode 4 (two colours) and mode 5 (four colours, at half the horizontal resolution) use the same video clock rate, and the register value is what tells them apart: the number of characters per line and the width of each pixel.

*Elite* draws on a custom screen of 256 × 248 pixels based on mode 4, and changes the register while the picture is being drawn. At each vertical sync it starts a timer in the System VIA, one of the machine's two 6522 chips, set to run out just as the television beam reaches the dashboard. The interrupt that follows switches the Video ULA from two colours per pixel to four, and the next frame begins in two colours again. The result is a sharp black-and-white space view over a coloured control panel.

## Launch and the box

Acornsoft launched *Elite* to the press "inside Space Station Zero at Thorpe Park funfair", *Acorn Programs* reported. It was the flagship of 20 Christmas titles backed by a £150,000 advertising campaign.

The package was part of the appeal. For its money the buyer got a flight training manual, a poster of the ships, and *The Dark Wheel*, a novella by the science fiction writer Robert Holdstock that opens with the trading ship *Avalonia* leaving its berth above Lave. There was also an entry card for a competition: players who reached the rank of "Competent" could send in a code from the game for a certificate and a chance of a prize. Braben later told *Edge* that people bought the game just to enter. "Did you buy the game?" he asked them. "'Erm, no,' they said, 'but when I realised I was in with a chance I thought, right, I can do better than that'. They went out and bought the game just to get the entry card."

The price was not settled even in the launch issue of *Acorn User*: Acornsoft's advertisement asked £14.95 on cassette, £17.65 on disc and £12.95 for the Electron, while the review, written from the preview, printed £14.95, £17.95 and £14.95.

## Sales

Two months after launch *Acorn Programs* reported 13,000 copies sold in two weeks and forecast Christmas sales "in the region of 100,000 — more than double those of any other Acornsoft game so far". Later figures come from the authors and vary: *Edge* put the total at 750,000 copies in 1994, and in 2000 reported that Bell "estimates that some 600,000 copies were eventually sold", with a first production run of 50,000. No audited total has been published. When the Gallup software chart began, *ST Format* recalled in 1989, *Elite* was its first number one.

## Firebird and the other versions

Bell and Braben had given Acornsoft the rights only for Acorn's own machines, the BBC Micro and the Electron. "They didn't realise we were serious about it," Braben told *Edge*. The rest went to British Telecom's label Firebird, for what *ZZAP!64*'s first issue called, on rumour, "a six-figure sum".

- **Commodore 64** (Firebird, spring 1985): converted by Bell and Braben themselves while they studied for their finals, according to *ZZAP!64*, which made it the Gold Medal game of its first issue in May 1985. The 64's larger memory held the whole game at once, where the BBC cassette version had "certain features trimmed", and the conversion added the Trumbles: "cute little furry guys" that breed in your cargo bay with "a cacophony of breeding squeaks".
- **ZX Spectrum** (Firebird, late 1985): converted by Torus, the team behind *Gyron*, and arrived "a mere three months late", in *CRASH*'s words. *CRASH* gave it 92% and noted that it was the first major release protected by [Lenslok](/vault/techniques/lenslock), a plastic lens needed to read a code off the screen. Many buyers struggled with it.
- **Amstrad CPC** (Firebird): an *Amstrad Action* Mastergame in February 1986.
- **MSX and IBM PC** (Firebird): the PC version, by the Leeds company Real Time, let players choose solid-filled ships instead of wireframes.
- **Atari ST and Amiga**: the ST version was reviewed by *ACE* in November 1988, and the Amiga version was on sale from Firebird at £24.95 by August 1989.
- **Nintendo Entertainment System** (Imagineer): *Total!* scored it 96% and called it "probably the best space flight sim ever".

By 2000 *Edge* counted 17 formats.

## Reputation

When *ACE* chose its all-time greats in March 1991, it summed *Elite* up in its first clause: "Probably responsible for selling many BBCs". It called the game "the archetypical space fighting-and-trading game which has been much imitated but (many would argue) never surpassed". Braben told *Edge* in 2000 that people still stopped him to say they had failed their exams because of it, "but more say, 'I got into the industry because of you'".
