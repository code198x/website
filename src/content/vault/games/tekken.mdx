*Tekken* began as an experiment in moving a polygon person. Katsuhiro Harada, who joined its team in his first year at Namco, told *Edge* in 2013 that it "wasn't designed as a fighting game initially": the aim was "to prove that they could create a 3D character that would respond to your inputs", and to do it at 60 frames a second, twice the speed of Sega's *[Virtua Fighter](/vault/games/virtua-fighter)*. The experiment ran on new hardware, the System 11 arcade board that Namco and Sony had built from the PlayStation's chips. When the PlayStation version arrived in 1995 it was, for once, what *Edge* called "arcade perfect".

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** [Namco](/vault/companies/namco). Director and lead designer: Seiichi Ishii.
- **Hardware:** Namco System 11, the first game on the board.
- **Arcade release:** Japan, December 1994.
- **Home version:** PlayStation (Japan, March 1995, ¥5,800; Europe and North America, November 1995).
- **Controls:** a joystick and four buttons, one for each limb: left punch, right punch, left kick, right kick.
- **Fighters:** eight, plus a boss, Heihachi, and further hidden characters in the PlayStation version.
- **Sales:** a million PlayStation copies by the end of 1995; *Lotek64* called it the first game on the machine to reach that figure.

## A Virtua Fighter veteran at Namco

Seiichi Ishii had worked on *Virtua Racing* and, as a motion designer, on *Virtua Fighter* at Sega's AM2 before moving to Namco, where he directed *Tekken* and worked on *Tekken 2*. Harada remembered him as "completely focused on the 3D, the models and the framerate". Namco was not trying to make a fundamentally different game from *Virtua Fighter*, Harada said: "Our entire focus was on pushing the technological boundaries." *Ridge Racer* had put textures on polygon cars; *Tekken* would put them on polygon people.

Harada's own job was to "balance the game, work on timing and frame data". He admitted the result was rough: "there were certain moves that couldn't be blocked. In truth, it wasn't very well balanced at all."

## System 11

The board had its roots in Namco's deal with Sony. According to Namco's Shegeichi Nakamura (the spelling *Edge* printed), speaking in 1995, *Tekken* was already planned for the System 22 board that ran *Ridge Racer*, to fight Sega's *Virtua Fighter 2* on Model 2. "If we had done that, the machine's price would have been expensive. But when Sony came along we decided to go for a low-cost system — in short, we've left the big arcade stores to Sega and VF2 and *Tekken* has been sold to smaller arcade centres." System 11 was two boards: one carrying the PlayStation's graphics processor and CPU, the other Namco's own sound chips, with extra video memory and RAM.

*Edge* first saw the game half-finished in January 1995, after it had drawn crowds at the Amusement Machine Show in Tokyo and been shown privately in America under the name *Rave War*. It called *Tekken* ("Iron Fist") "a conventional — albeit impressive — *Virtua Fighter* clone", and noticed the controls: "there's a single button for each hand and foot. In theory, this makes the fighting more instinctive and special moves easier to learn."

## One button per limb

*Street Fighter II* had given players light, medium and heavy attacks. *Tekken* asked instead which limb to use. Each fighter's four limbs had their own character: "Certain characters can manage only a jab with their left arm, for example, while their right arm is able to deliver a meatier, more substantial blow," *Edge* explained. A floored fighter could get up, roll towards or away from danger, or "flip directly back into the fray with an attack". Throws and slams switched the camera to the best angle to watch them.

The eight fighters were a mixed bunch, as *Edge* described them: Kazuya, the lead character and the one "those starting out will opt for"; Paul, "the American member of the troupe"; Law, "a Bruce Lee type"; King, who "merely dons an intricate face mask for battle"; Michelle, with "a penchant for intricate but solid slams and throws"; Nina, "Tekken's only British-bred fighter"; Jack, "a cybernetic organism"; and Yoshimitsu, "a metal-clad warrior with sharp blades at each arm", who could spin until he fell over. Kazuya's father Heihachi waited at the end as the boss. The fights took place in real places, among them Venice, Fiji, the Acropolis and, as *Edge* noticed with some surprise, Lake Windermere.

## The PlayStation version

The PlayStation game went on sale in Japan in March 1995 at ¥5,800, and *Edge* gave it nine out of ten. It ran at 60 frames a second, "the same speed as the *Virtua Fighter 2* coin-op". Its fighters, *Edge* judged, moved better even than *Virtua Fighter*'s motion-captured ones: those "move with a breathtaking level of accuracy, they still fall way short of *Tekken*'s standards". The home version added a pre-rendered introduction, arranged versions of the arcade music on CD, nine extra boss characters to unlock, and a playable copy of Namco's *Galaga* during the eight-second load. "*Tekken* rips up the rule book," the review ended, "and delivers an experience that even its £2000 coin-op namesake can't match." Its complaints were flat backgrounds and the odd polygon glitch.

Later that year *Edge* named *Tekken* among the games that used "modern motion capture techniques", with *Virtua Fighter* and Lore Design's *Highlander*; see [motion capture](/vault/techniques/motion-capture).

The *Official UK PlayStation Magazine*'s first issue, in November 1995, reported that the Japanese version had been "one of the biggest selling titles" for five months, with the PAL release due that month at £45 to £50. By the end of 1995, the German magazine *Lotek64* later wrote, *Tekken* had sold a million copies, something no PlayStation game had done before. *Wireframe* put the point in a sentence: the game "showed home gaming was no longer in the shadow of its coin-munching brethren".

## The series

Namco moved fast. *Edge* previewed *Tekken 2* in September 1995, four months after the PlayStation release, again on System 11, with redrawn backgrounds, redesigned fighters, two new characters, Lei and Jun, and Heihachi and Kazuya swapping roles as playable fighter and boss. It reached the PlayStation in 1996, and in November that year Sony's Teruhisa Tokunaga told *Edge* that "*Tekken 2* in Japan has exceeded one million units". *Tekken 3* (1997) moved to the new System 12 board; *Edge* later held up its fighters shadow-boxing before a bout as "a shining example" of motion capture, performed by "two real martial artists", one of them "an expert in Capoeira-based combat". *Tekken Tag Tournament* followed in arcades in 1999, and on the PlayStation 2 in 2000.

Ishii left to found Dream Factory, which made *Tobal No. 1* for Square in 1996. [Katsuhiro Harada](/vault/people/katsuhiro-harada) stayed and became the series' public face, as its director and producer. *Edge* reported in 2013 that the series had sold "some 44 million copies worldwide". *Tekken 7* went on to sell more than 11.8 million copies, the most of any game in the series, and *Tekken 8*, released in January 2024, sold 2 million in its first month, Bandai Namco said. In December 2025 Harada announced that he would leave Bandai Namco at the end of the year, as the series approached its 30th anniversary.
