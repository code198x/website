Konami put the name *Life Force* on three different games. The first was not really different at all: it was *[Salamander](/vault/games/salamander)*, Konami's 1986 follow-up to *[Gradius](/vault/games/gradius)*, renamed for North American arcades. The second, released in Japan in 1987, reworked *Salamander*, bringing back the *Gradius* power-up gauge and redrawing its backgrounds so that the whole game seemed to take place inside a living body. The third was the NES cartridge of 1988, a two-player journey into a monster that eats galaxies. The *Salamander* entry tells the story of the original coin-op; this one covers the two games that were new under the *Life Force* name.

## The North American coin-op

In North America the 1986 coin-op carried the *Life Force* name with little else changed, according to *Retro Gamer*. Public sources say Konami added an opening text that set the game inside a giant alien organism. In its 2025 *Gradius Origins* collection Konami still lists this version under *Salamander*, as "NA Ver. (LIFE FORCE)", and gives the Japanese *Life Force* a place of its own.

## The Japanese Life Force

The *Life Force* that appeared in Japanese arcades in 1987 was a "graphically modified variant" of *Salamander*, in *Retro Gamer*'s words. It made two main changes. It put back the "weapon power-up bar" of *Gradius*: instead of collecting each weapon directly, the player picked up capsules that moved a highlight along a gauge and chose when to spend them. And it "rejigged the stage backgrounds (and a number of bosses) to suggest a more unified (and far more garish) bio-organic theme". Konami's own description of the game, written for *Gradius Origins*, picks out "new in-game voices and a strategic power-up system". *Wireframe* called it *Salamander* "in a slightly reworked guise".

*Retro Gamer* was not impressed. The new look "looked a fair bit worse than the original and was less fun to play, despite some additional music tracks and speech", though it was just as hard as *Salamander*. For a long time the Japanese *Life Force* was difficult to play outside Japan. Konami put it, arcade-perfect, on *Salamander Deluxe Pack Plus* for the Sega Saturn and PlayStation in 1997, a collection that stayed in Japan, and released it worldwide in *Gradius Origins* on 7 August 2025.

## The NES game

Konami's Famicom conversion came out in Japan as *Salamander* in 1987. In North America it became *Life Force* in 1988. The first issue of *Nintendo Power*, in the summer of 1988, listed it among Konami's coming games, a "sci-fi action thriller" in which the player would "save the Milky Way", and the next issue gave it a six-page guide.

The cartridge has its own story. A planet-eating creature called Zelos has swallowed the worlds of Gradius and Latis, and two ships fly into its body to destroy its "heart and soul": the Vic Viper and, when a second player joins in, the RoadBritish Space Destroyer. Both players fly at once. *Computer Play*, an American magazine, was reminded of *Fantastic Voyage*, "that story about the miniaturized sub that travels through the human body".

Like the Japanese coin-op, the NES game uses the *Gradius* gauge. *Club Nintendo* explained that each pod left by destroyed aliens advances the available weapon "at the foot of the screen by one", and the A button selects it. *ACE* noted that "the method of tooling up is the same as in Gradius". The weapons are speed, missiles, the laser, the ripple laser, the force field and the Options, here limited to two.

There are six stages, three scrolling across and three scrolling upwards. *Club Nintendo* named them: the Cell Stage, the Volcanic Stage, the Prominence Stage with its "phoenix swarm", a second Cell Stage, the Temple Stage, based on the ancient city of Latis, and the Mechanical City Stage, with high-speed scrolling and Moai heads. Each ends with a guardian. Golem chases the ship around the screen "swinging his arms insanely" until its eye opens; Intruder spins four arms round its body; Cruiser Tetron spits fireballs and cools down only when the player fires a laser into its mouth. *Nintendo Power* wrote its guide with a straight face and a dictionary to hand: "Wembley's Fifth Galactic Dictionary defines the word dread as: 'the chilling sensation one feels upon meeting the Death Hands of Phenom.'"

The game also has Konami's familiar cheat. *Computer Play* hinted in December 1988 at "a secret that will give you more lives, which is good because you can only continue a game a few times". It was the [Konami Code](/vault/culture/konami-code), and in 1993 *Nintendo Power* explained that entering it on the title screen would "extend your ships in reserve to a total of 29".

### In Europe

*Life Force* reached Europe at the end of 1989, when *Club Nintendo* gave it a full review in its Christmas issue. British magazines liked it. *ACE* said in May 1990 that "Konami's Life Force pushes the NES to the limits" and that it "now takes up the mantle of best Nintendo shoot 'em up". *The Games Machine* warned that "Life Force is tough and gets no easier". When *Mean Machines* reviewed it in its first issue, in October 1990, at £29.99, the reviewer was "extremely pleased" that it "retains almost all of the major ingredients that made its arcade parent so enjoyable", and called it "a stunning shoot 'em up which is a must for your collection".
