*Red Alert* begins with a time machine. In 1924 Albert Einstein travels back to Germany and removes Adolf Hitler from history before he can come to power. Without Hitler there is no Second World War as we know it, and Stalin's Soviet Union fills the space, pushing west across Europe until the Allies have to fight it. Westwood Studios made this the prequel to *[Command & Conquer](/vault/games/command-and-conquer)*: the war that, by the end, sets up the world of Tiberium and the Global Defense Initiative. It sold on the reputation of the first game, and reviewers agreed it was the best of the real-time strategy games that had followed it, even while one of them called it an expansion in disguise.

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** [Westwood Studios](/vault/companies/westwood-studios). **Publisher:** [Virgin](/vault/companies/virgin-games) Interactive Entertainment.
- **Released:** November 1996 in North America and December 1996 in Europe, for MS-DOS and Windows 95; $54.99 in the United States, £34.99 in Britain.
- **Sides:** the Allies and the Soviet Union.
- **Players:** one, or up to eight over a network or the Internet, with a skirmish mode against up to seven computer opponents.
- **Music:** Frank Klepacki, including the theme "Hell March".
- **Other versions:** [PlayStation](/vault/systems/sony-playstation) (1997), and *Retaliation* (1998) for the PlayStation.
- **Source code:** published by [Electronic Arts](/vault/companies/electronic-arts) in 2020 (the remastered game) and 2025 (the original).

## A prequel, not a sequel

The fans who had spent a year with *Command & Conquer* were waiting for a sequel, and *Computer & Video Games* had to break the news: "Unfortunately, this isn't it." *Red Alert* was "the prequel to the original game, explaining how NOD and GDI were formed", with about 20 missions for each side. Westwood's real sequel, *Tiberian Sun*, did not come until 1999.

The story was told, as before, in filmed scenes between missions. Stalin himself briefs the Soviet player. *Computer Gaming World*'s Martin Cirulis thought his "frightening grin and gallows humor will haunt you in just about every cut-scene on the Russian side", and warned that players with "a working knowledge of history" might be uneasy at being told by Stalin what a fine fellow he was. Its technology came from what Cirulis called "the journal of non-reproducible results": Tesla's work built into giant coils that "fry Allied units at long range", and Einstein himself captured as a prize of war.

## What changed

The biggest change was on the screen. Under Windows 95 the battlefield was drawn in SVGA, at 640 × 400 according to *PC Zone*, instead of the first game's "chunky" VGA. Cirulis found that the higher resolution did more than look sharper: the player could see about twice as much of the battlefield, and the action felt slower, which answered those who "complained that C&C was only about mouse-clicking". The MS-DOS version stayed in the lower resolution, and he thought it "buggy and inadequate". Groups could now be moved together and made to keep their formation.

The two sides were further apart than GDI and Nod had been. The Soviets had attack dogs that could "tear a soldier to shreds in a second", submarines, ground-attack aircraft, V2 rockets and the Tesla coil, a tower that fires lightning. The Allies answered with gunboats, destroyers and cruisers, medics who heal nearby infantry, and a spy in a dinner jacket whom only the dogs could sniff out. Terry Coleman, *Computer Gaming World*'s strategy columnist, liked the commando Tanya best: "a not-so-closet pyromaniac who empties her revolvers into anything that moves". Cirulis thought the Soviets "very cool and innovative" and the Allies a set of "holdovers from C&C", as if they had been worked on last.

## Online from the start

Multiplayer was where *Red Alert* moved furthest. *PC Zone*'s reviewer installed it at home and within half an hour was playing, through Westwood's own Internet server, against a stranger in New York, who beat him. Up to eight could play over a network. The multiplayer games added tricks the campaign did not have, such as the Allies' fake buildings for fooling an opponent, radar jammers and ore thieves. A skirmish mode set the player against up to seven computer opponents, and a map editor on the CD let players make their own battlefields, which soon appeared on web sites for download. Early in 1997 the online games service Mplayer, where *Command & Conquer* was already one of the most played games, announced that it would add *Red Alert*.

## Reception

Coleman called it "a Mozart symphony to all the Salieri clones", which had followed *Command & Conquer* in a flood, and "sleek, sophisticated and very addictive". *PC Zone* led its reviews with it and called it a "time sponge": "You think you've been playing it for half an hour, but a quick glance at the clock reveals that it's now the year 1999." Cirulis was cooler. He found the missions "much better designed" than the first game's and "meshed more tightly to the storyline", but wrote that "RED ALERT could have been a real leap forward instead of a very cool expansion". He still called it "easily the best real-time strat game of the quarter".

Coleman thought the music "robust, adrenaline-pumping stuff". It was by Frank Klepacki, Westwood's in-house composer, and its best-known piece, the march "Hell March", is still there in the game's music table, as the track HELL226M, in the source code EA later published.

Before release *Edge* predicted that *Red Alert* would "probably sell more copies than any other PC game this year". It came close in Britain: over Christmas 1996, *Edge* reported, only *FIFA 97* "beat C&C: Red Alert and Tomb Raider to the top spot". Some of its rivals were unexpected. In March 1997 *Edge* passed on a claim in the *Washington Post* that Mattel's *Barbie Fashion Designer* had outsold it.

## Later history

Westwood kept the game going with two expansion discs of extra missions and units, *Counterstrike* and *The Aftermath*, in 1997, and the same year brought it to the PlayStation. A reworked PlayStation edition, *Retaliation*, followed in 1998; *PlayStation Power* described it as "not the sequel to Red Alert, but an update", for one or two players. *Red Alert* then grew into its own series, separate from the Tiberium games. *Red Alert 2* came out in 2000, when *Computer Gaming World* put it in its forecast of the Christmas season's best-selling PC games, followed by its expansion *Yuri's Revenge* in 2001, and, after Electronic Arts had closed Westwood in 2003, EA made *Red Alert 3* (2008). EA released the original *Red Alert* as freeware on 31 August 2008, and in June 2020 remastered it alongside the first game in the *Command & Conquer Remastered Collection*.

## Released source code

The source code has been published twice. In May 2020 Electronic Arts put the code of the *Remastered Collection*'s RedAlert.dll, together with the *Tiberian Dawn* code and the map editor, on GitHub for mod makers. On 27 February 2025 it published the source code of the original *Red Alert* in its own repository, alongside *Command & Conquer*, *Renegade* and *Generals*/*Zero Hour*. Both releases are under the GNU General Public License, version 3, "with additional terms applied", which grant no rights to the "Command & Conquer" name or other EA trademarks. The 1996 code "does not fully compile" as published: it was written for the Watcom C/C++ compiler and Borland's Turbo Assembler, and relies on DirectX 5 and on third-party network and sound libraries that are not included. EA describes the repositories as "for preservation purposes only".
