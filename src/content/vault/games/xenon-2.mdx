By the summer of 1989 the Bitmap Brothers were famous enough that their names sold the game, and *Xenon 2: Megablast* was sold on their names even though they had not written its code. They came up with the concept and oversaw everything "from graphics to guardians", *ST Format* explained, while a team called The Assembly Line did the programming. The result was a vertical shoot-'em-up with a shop, a shopkeeper who took off his Walkman to haggle, and a hip-hop record on the title screen. *ZZAP!* called it "quite simply the definitive Amiga shoot-'em-up".

## Fast facts

- **Design:** [The Bitmap Brothers](/vault/companies/bitmap-brothers) (Mike Montgomery, Steve Kelly and Eric Matthews).
- **Programming:** The Assembly Line. **Graphics:** Mark Coleman.
- **Music:** [David Whittaker](/vault/people/david-whittaker), arranging Bomb the Bass's "Megablast".
- **Publisher:** Image Works, [Mirrorsoft](/vault/companies/mirrorsoft)'s label; £24.99 on the Amiga and ST.
- **Released:** autumn 1989, reviewed in the October issues.
- **Later versions:** PC, Sega Master System (1991), Mega Drive (1992), Acorn Archimedes and others.

## Who made it

*Xenon 2* was the Bitmaps' third major game, after *Xenon*, published by [Melbourne House](/vault/companies/melbourne-house), and *Speedball*, their first game for Mirrorsoft's Image Works. *ST Format*'s profile in October 1989 set out who did what. The Bitmaps "came up with the game concept and oversaw the entire project from graphics to guardians", while The Assembly Line wrote the program. The code was written on an IBM machine and then moved across to the ST. Mark Coleman, who had drawn *Speedball*, did the graphics in *Degas Elite*, with extra animation routines written by Mike Montgomery. *The Games Machine* made the same point in its review: "Although not programmed by the Bros, a development team called The Assembly Line do a great job."

The game had changed in development. When *The One* visited in May 1989, Eric Matthews explained that the ship had originally been able to turn from a tank into a fighter, as in the first *Xenon*, but the idea "was dropped in favour of a more straightforward blast". "Everyone seems to be into destruction and extra weapons in a big way," he said. "We wanted to satisfy this demand."

## Five time bombs

The story is the usual excuse. A thousand years after the first *Xenon*, the Xenites have planted five time bombs at points in Earth's history. Each of the five levels is a different period, and the aliens evolve as you go. The first is a primeval sea of anemones, tadpoles and trilobites, ending at a giant nautilus shellfish. Then come insects, with a huge spider at the end; a level stuffed with fish and piranhas; dinosaurs; and finally a metallic future of robot craft. Every level after the first has two big guardians.

The ship scrolls slowly up the screen, but it can also move back down the level, which *The Games Machine* thought turned "an already highly playable shoot-'em-up into a classic". Another add-on let it dive to a lower layer beneath the action for a few seconds, while the aliens multiplied overhead. *Zero* was pleased that losing a life did not send you back to the start of the level, only to the last of several restart points in it.

## Crispin's Swop Shop

Destroyed aliens leave cash bubbles behind, and twice a level you spend it. The shopkeeper, Crispin, is a horned reptilian alien who turns off his Walkman as you enter; *The Games Machine* noted that he frowns and rubs his chin before making an offer, "but don't expect a good price!" He will buy back your add-ons for about half what they cost.

There was plenty to buy. *ZZAP!* counted 24 items and *ST Format* 29, priced from 200 to 6,000 credits: rear and side shots, lasers, mines, homing missiles, a flamethrower, drones and "Super Nashwan Power", which fitted every weapon for about ten seconds. The cheapest item was advice about what to buy next. Some add-ons also turned up in capsules left by destroyed aliens, but the heavy weapons had to be bought.

## "Megablast"

The music was a record. Bomb the Bass's "Megablast (Hip Hop on Precinct 13)" played over the title sequence, and David Whittaker arranged it for the game, working from samples of the recording, as *Computer and Video Games* reported. On the Amiga the boot disk played a sampled version, and a simplified arrangement continued in the game if you wanted it; otherwise you got the sound effects, in which every weapon had its own voice. *Zero* noted that Tim Simenon, who was Bomb the Bass, wrote his music with an Atari ST. The Bitmaps' dealings with Bomb the Bass's label, Rhythm King, later helped give them the idea for their own publishing label, [Renegade](/vault/companies/renegade).

When *ZZAP!*'s readers voted on Amiga music in July 1990, *Xenon 2* came top.

## Reception

The reviews were close to unanimous:

- *ZZAP!* (October 1989) gave the Amiga version a Gold Medal and called it "the definitive Amiga shoot-'em-up".
- *The Games Machine* (October 1989) gave the Amiga version 96% and called it "by far the best blaster of the year so far".
- *ACE* (October 1989) rated the Amiga version 910 and the ST version 905 out of 1,000, adding that the game "almost lives up to the hype".
- *ST Format* (October 1989) gave the ST version 90%, placing it "in the same elite class as Blood Money".
- *Zero*'s preview issue said it "IS as brilliant as you were expecting it to be. Maybe it's even better."

Not every reviewer thought it new. *ACE* found "elements of just about every shoot-'em-up you've ever played... from Space Invaders through to R-Type", and *Zero* thought the Bitmaps had learnt about playability "from the arcades — and in particular from [R-Type](/vault/games/r-type)". Most added that it did not matter. *Amiga Format* put a playable demo on its coverdisk that month, and in May 1991 *Amiga Power* ranked the game 39th in its All-Time Top 100.

## Later versions

A PC version followed; *PC Gamer* later called it "an average version". Image Works published a Master System version in 1991, and *Mean Machines* reviewed the Mega Drive version in February 1992. By late 1992 Virgin Games was advertising the Sega versions, after Mirrorsoft's collapse. Mirrorsoft's budget label, Mirror Image, had reissued the Amiga version at £9.99 by September 1991, and an Acorn Archimedes version was on sale by 1993. Reference works also list a CDTV version and a Game Boy version in 1992, Japanese versions for the PC-98 and X68000, and an Atari Jaguar version published in 2016 with new music.
