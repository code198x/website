A year before IBM announced its home computer, the PCjr, it gave Sierra On-Line a prototype and asked for a game that would show off the machine's colour graphics. Sierra spent 18 months and, by its own account, more than $700,000 on the answer. In *King's Quest* the player did not just read about a place and look at a picture of it. You walked a small knight, Sir Graham, around a kingdom drawn in 16 colours. He could swim, climb trees and step behind a rock so that his legs disappeared. *K-Power*, meeting the game at the Consumer Electronics Show in early 1984, called it "an amazing breakthrough in computer adventures because of its 3-D effects".

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** [Sierra On-Line](/vault/companies/sierra), Coarsegold, California.
- **Designer:** [Roberta Williams](/vault/people/roberta-williams).
- **First release:** 1984, on the IBM PCjr, marketed by IBM; Sierra sold its own versions for the IBM PC, Apple IIe and IIc and the Tandy 1000, at $49 each.
- **System:** Sierra's AGI (Adventure Game Interpreter) for the first three games; SCI (Sierra Creative Interpreter) from *King's Quest IV* in 1988.
- **Series:** eight games from 1984 to 1998, then an episodic *King's Quest* from 2015.

## Made for the PCjr

Sierra already had a connection with IBM, which had licensed its earlier colour adventure *The Wizard and the Princess* as *Adventure in Serenia*. According to *Wireframe*, IBM asked for an enhanced version for the PCjr, but Roberta Williams wanted to add animation, and a straightforward port turned into a new game.

In February 1985 *COMPUTE!*'s columnist Donald B. Trivette rang Sierra to find out how the game worked, and his article is the fullest period account of how it was built. Roberta Williams, who had already designed five adventure games, wrote a story out of familiar tales, the Billy Goats Gruff and Hansel and Gretel among them. Sir Graham (spelt "Grahame" in the article) must find three lost treasures for the king of Daventry, meeting on the way a troll under a bridge, a goat, a witch and a beanstalk. A team of six programmers and artists built it.

The kingdom is 80 screens, which Sierra called rooms: 48 outdoor scenes in a grid six scenes deep and eight wide that wraps round at the edges, and 32 interiors of caves and houses. Nobody stored the pictures themselves. Artists drew each scene on paper and traced it on a graphics tablet, which recorded the drawing as a list of instructions, and the game redrew the room line by line and filled in the colour each time the player walked in. The castle, the most detailed scene, took 2,400 bytes of instructions; the simplest took 470. A PCjr needed about four seconds to draw a room, and Trivette found it entertaining to watch.

Behind each picture sat an invisible second layer. Lines drawn with the same tablet marked where Graham could and could not walk, and every object in a room carried a priority from 1 to 15, low near the top of the screen and high near the bottom. As Graham moved, his own priority changed with his position, and the program compared the numbers to decide what to draw over what. That is how he could pass in front of a tree and then behind it. The program kept four save areas for animated objects, so a room could hold no more than four moving things at once, Graham included. Trivette noticed that the magic beans could only be planted in about half the rooms for this reason.

The rules of each room were written in a scripting language of Sierra's own, which *COMPUTE!* called the Game Adaptation Language and Sierra later called AGI, the Adventure Game Interpreter. The program looped through statements that worked, Trivette wrote, "sort of like a group of IF-THEN statements in BASIC"; one room had 180 lines of them, including the logic that moved a wandering goat from one screen to the next. The player still typed commands such as "take carrot" into a line at the bottom of the screen, while steering Graham with a joystick or the cursor keys.

## The PCjr fails, the Tandy 1000 arrives

The PCjr did not sell. John Williams, looking back in Sierra's tenth-anniversary magazine in 1990, called it "IBM's disastrous PCjr computer" and said Sierra "felt like the only company in history that had bet on IBM and lost". Sierra kept the rights to its PCjr software, and Tandy's answer to the PCjr, the Tandy 1000, could run it. With "better than 8000" Radio Shack and Tandy shops to sell through, *King's Quest* found a new market, and it helped pull Sierra through the lean years after the home computer crash (see [Sierra On-Line](/vault/companies/sierra)). Versions for the Amiga and the Atari ST followed, and Parker Brothers published one for Sega's [Master System](/vault/systems/sega-master-system).

## The series

Sales and fan letters persuaded Roberta Williams to write a sequel, and the games became a family saga.

### King's Quest IV and SCI

*King's Quest IV* was the game on which Sierra bet its new system. Sierra's engineers replaced AGI with SCI, the Sierra Creative Interpreter, a general-purpose object-oriented language in which the designers could write a game's behaviour themselves instead of waiting for it to be added to the engine. Sierra previewed the opening cartoon at the June 1988 Consumer Electronics Show in a private cinema, on a 386 PC with a Roland MT-32 playing an orchestral score, and John Williams remembered a woman in the audience crying as King Graham collapsed. The game was nowhere near finished. Al Lowe told *Wireframe* that in late August 1988 he was asked to look at it: "Roberta doesn't think it's going to be done in a month." His answer was that it would not be done in a year, and Sierra put every other game on hold while the whole company programmed *King's Quest IV* for 30 days. It shipped that year. For owners of older machines Sierra also sold an AGI version, which in John Williams's words "paled by comparison".

### Pointing instead of typing

From *King's Quest V* the player pointed and clicked instead of typing. According to *Wireframe*, the change came after Roberta Williams watched her own mother struggle to play her games. Ken Williams expected hate mail, but in Sierra's magazine in the spring of 1991 he reported a mostly positive reception, and in the same issue a parent described a disabled son finishing his first *King's Quest* with a modified mouse.

Sierra's games let the player die, and a wrong move could end *King's Quest* on the spot: *Wireframe* describes a knight whose single wrong step meant an untimely death. Lori Cole of Sierra defended death as a plot element at the Computer Game Developers' Conference in 1991, against Ron Gilbert of Lucasfilm Games; the [Sierra](/vault/companies/sierra) entry covers that debate.

## Later history

Ken and Roberta Williams sold Sierra to CUC International in 1996, and *King's Quest VIII: Mask of Eternity* was made under the new owners. Roberta Williams stayed to finish it, and later described its making as "an involuntary team effort". It was built on an engine from Sierra's Dynamix studio, which the team eventually took over and finished itself, and the game ended the Williamses' part in the series.

Activision, which inherited Sierra, announced a new *King's Quest* in August 2014, made by the small studio The Odd Gentlemen. It came out in episodes from 2015, both a sequel and a retelling of the old games that began with how Graham became king, and its art director, Evan Cagle, told *Wireframe* that the team's main concern was making sure "the Williamses felt we had done their property justice". The last episode appeared in 2016.
