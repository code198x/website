In *Dune II* the enemy does not wait for you. Each mission starts with a construction yard on a patch of rock in the desert of Arrakis and a little money. Somewhere beyond the black edge of the map a rival house has already dug in, and it is building, mining and training troops at the same moment you are. *[Computer Gaming World](/vault/magazines/computer-gaming-world)* called it "a real-time exercise in every sense of the word". Westwood Studios made it for Virgin Games at the end of 1992, and its pattern of harvesting a resource, building a base and producing an army became the one [real-time strategy](/vault/genres/rts-genre) followed: Blizzard's *[Warcraft](/vault/games/warcraft)* borrowed it in 1994, and Westwood's own *[Command & Conquer](/vault/games/command-and-conquer)* took it to a mass audience in 1995.

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** [Westwood Studios](/vault/companies/westwood-studios), Las Vegas. **Publisher:** [Virgin Games](/vault/companies/virgin-games).
- **Titles:** *Dune II: The Building of a Dynasty* in North America; *Dune II: Battle for Arrakis* in Europe.
- **Released:** MS-DOS, December 1992 ($59.99 in the United States); [Amiga](/vault/systems/commodore-amiga), 1993; [Mega Drive](/vault/systems/sega-mega-drive), 1994; [Acorn](/vault/systems/acorn-archimedes) RISC OS, from Eclipse, by 1995.
- **Credits (from Virgin's advertisements):** design by Aaron E. Powell and Joe Bostic; programming by Bostic and Scott K. Bowen; music and sound by Frank Klepacki and Dwight Okahara; produced by Brett Sperry.
- **Players:** one, against the computer.

## Not the first, but the pattern

*Dune II* was not the first strategy game played in real time. SSI's *Cytron Masters* (1982), Imagine's *Stonkers* (1983) for the Spectrum and Technosoft's *Herzog Zwei* (1989) for the Mega Drive all had armies that moved without waiting for turns, and American magazines were calling *[Populous](/vault/games/populous)* real-time strategy by 1990 (the [real-time strategy](/vault/genres/rts-genre) entry tells that story). What *Dune II* did was put the pieces together: a resource on the map, harvesters to collect it, buildings that unlock other buildings, factories that turn money into units, and an opponent doing all of it at once.

Westwood's people have named the games they were playing. Joe Bostic, the lead programmer, told *[Retro Gamer](/vault/magazines/retro-gamer)* in 2008 that "we were also playing *Herzog Zwei* on the Mega Drive at the time", along with *Populous*, *Military Madness* and *[Civilization](/vault/games/civilization)*, and "thought a game grown out of the combined features of these titles would be great fun to play". The first thing they built, he said, was real-time vehicle combat; base building and spice harvesting came after. The mouse was the advantage *Herzog Zwei* had lacked: it let the player give orders to individual units, and in Bostic's words "was critical in making the RTS genre possible".

## Mining Arrakis

The licence came from Frank Herbert's novel, by way of Virgin's first *Dune* game, a Cryo adventure built around the David Lynch film. The sequel kept the planet and dropped the story. The Emperor offers Arrakis to whichever house produces the most spice, with "no rules of engagement", and three houses answer: the Atreides, the Harkonnens and the Ordos, a house Westwood invented. *Computer Gaming World* noticed that the sandworms were "more moral, and likable, than many of the book's leading characters".

A mission begins on rock, the only ground that can be built on. The player lays concrete slabs and puts up windtraps for power, a refinery that comes with a harvester, silos to store what it earns, a barracks and a vehicle factory. The harvester drives out to the orange spice fields on the sand and trundles back full, and each load becomes credits. Later missions bring a radar outpost, a hi-tech factory for carryalls and ornithopters, repair pads, rocket turrets, a starport for buying units from off-world, and finally a palace with a house's own superweapon. Sandworms swallow anything that stays on the open sand too long.

Each house fights a little differently. Brett Sperry told *[The One](/vault/magazines/the-one)* that the Harkonnens were his favourite, "because they have the Death Hand Missile and the Devastator Tank"; the Atreides had the Sonic Tank and could call on the Fremen, and the Ordos had saboteurs and the Deviator. The missions ran in order and got steeply harder, and in the late ones the Emperor's Sardaukar joined the enemy.

## Clicking, not typing

Sperry wanted a game "easy to learn and difficult to master", he told *The One* in January 1993: "You don't have to read a long and boring manual to learn how to play. In fact, all of our focus groups picked up the game within the first five minutes without reading a manual at all." Everything was done with the mouse. Click a building and it showed a damage report and a menu of what it could make, with each item's cost; click a unit and give it a move or attack order; click on the radar map to send it anywhere. A mentat, the house's adviser, explained each mission and every building and unit. Units took orders one at a time; letting the player select several at once was one of the first things Blizzard changed when it made *Warcraft*.

## Reception

*Computer Gaming World* previewed a beta in December 1992. Chuck Moss found a game that "marries the development/financial management dynamic of *Sim City* to the tactical battle challenge of *The Perfect General*", and called it "a fast, tense, visual" game. The magazine's review in April 1993 praised the "highly intuitive" interface and the sound, from "the shrieks of dying infantry" to "one of the most original and enjoyable music scores ever composed for a computer game", and called the game "a real gem".

In Britain *PC Zone* reviewed it in its first issue, in April 1993, and reached for the same comparison: "a *Sim City* clone which is also a real-time shoot 'em-up", and "a thinking man's shoot 'em-up". When the Amiga version arrived, *[Amiga Format](/vault/magazines/amiga-format)*'s Andy Nuttall found it "dark, moody, atmospheric, addictive, brilliant". Bullfrog's *[Syndicate](/vault/games/syndicate)* was out the same year, and he put the two side by side: "If *Syndicate* could be described as *Populous 2* with guns, then *Dune 2* is *Sim City* with heavy artillery." His complaints were small: vehicles that would not find their own way round a building, and a pile of disk swaps for anyone with a single floppy drive.

## Mega Drive and Acorn

Westwood rebuilt the game for the Mega Drive, where Virgin published it in 1994 as *Dune II: Battle for Arrakis* in Europe and *Dune: The Battle for Arrakis* in North America, with new controls for the joypad. *Sega Visions* called it "a thinking warrior's game" where "economics are important". On the Acorn Archimedes, Eclipse published a RISC OS conversion; by September 1995 *[Acorn User](/vault/magazines/acorn-user)* was reporting "the successes of *Dune II*" and Eclipse's plans for a CD-ROM version.

## Warcraft and Command & Conquer

The closest early follower came from Blizzard. Patrick Wyatt, who started Blizzard's *[Warcraft: Orcs & Humans](/vault/games/warcraft)* in 1993, has written that the team "were inspired to create *Warcraft* after playing (and replaying and replaying) a game called *Dune 2*", and that before Blizzard had artists on the project he used screen captures of *Dune II*'s art as placeholders. *Computer Gaming World*'s reviewer found the resemblance plain in 1995. Westwood staff have said so too: Adam Isgreen told *Retro Gamer* in 2008 that the similarities were "pretty... blatant", while Bostic took them as "the sincerest form of flattery". The [Warcraft](/vault/games/warcraft) entry has the detail.

*Dune II* also gave its name to Westwood's next game before that game existed. Sperry told *The One* in January 1993 that *Dune 2* ran on "Westwood's Command and Conquer engine which took four man years to create", and that another game on it was "tentatively called *Command & Conquer: Fortress of Stone*". It became *[Command & Conquer](/vault/games/command-and-conquer)*, set on Earth and fought over Tiberium, in 1995. *[Edge](/vault/magazines/edge)* thought it "an only slightly improved version" of *Dune 2*: "Substitute spice for Tiberium and you have *Command & Conquer*." Looking back in 1997, Sperry told *Edge* that *Command & Conquer* came from "a perceived gap in the market — that started with *Dune 2*, actually".

## Later history

Westwood went back to Arrakis twice. *Dune 2000* (1998) for Windows was a remake of *Dune II* with filmed briefings, and *Emperor: Battle for Dune* followed in 2001. After [Electronic Arts](/vault/companies/electronic-arts) closed Westwood in 2003, players kept the original going themselves: fan projects such as OpenDUNE and Dune Legacy rebuilt the game to run on modern systems.
