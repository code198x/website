*Defender of the Crown* opens like a film, with credits over a harpsichord tune, and for many people who saw it in 1986 that was the point. [Cinemaware](/vault/companies/cinemaware)'s first game put castles, jousting tournaments and torch-lit sword fights on the [Commodore Amiga](/vault/systems/commodore-amiga) in hand-drawn pictures that no earlier home computer game had matched. Underneath was a simple strategy game, a cut-down version of the board game *Risk*. *Computer Gaming World*'s reviewer saw both sides in January 1987. Judged strictly as a strategy game, he wrote, it "would be mundane". But "you will use it as a showcase program to demonstrate the power of the Amiga to your friends."

## Fast facts

- **Publisher:** Master Designer Software, under its Cinemaware label; distributed by [Mindscape](/vault/companies/mindscape) in America and [Mirrorsoft](/vault/companies/mirrorsoft) in Britain.
- **Released:** Christmas 1986, for the Amiga; $49.95, on two disks, needing 512K.
- **Team:** designer Kellyn Beeck; producer John Cutter; art director [Jim Sachs](/vault/people/jim-sachs); programmer R.J. Mical; music by Jim Cuomo.
- **Setting:** England after the death of Richard I, borrowed from Walter Scott's *Ivanhoe*, with Robin Hood as an ally.
- **Later versions:** Commodore 64, Atari ST, PC, Amstrad CPC, NES, CD-ROM, and remakes into the 2000s.

## Saxons, Normans and Robin Hood

The king is dead and the crown is missing. The player picks one of four Saxon lords, each with different strengths in leadership, jousting and swordplay, and sets out to unite England and drive out three Norman rivals. Each turn is a month. The lord collects taxes from his lands, buys soldiers, knights and catapults, and chooses one main action: hold a tournament, raid an enemy castle for gold, or march into a neighbouring territory. Robin of Locksley, waiting in Sherwood Forest, can be called on for help three times in a game.

The action scenes are where the pictures move. A tournament puts the player behind the lance, aiming at an oncoming knight's shield. A siege is fought with a catapult lobbing boulders at the castle wall. A raid, or the rescue of a kidnapped Saxon lady, becomes a sword fight. Rescue her and, as Jimmy Maher describes it, "gratitude turns to love", in a romantic fireside scene with no bearing on winning the war. Maher traces the game's characters, including Cedric of Rotherwood, Brian de Bois-Guilbert and Wilfred of Ivanhoe, to Scott's novel, and its look to decades of Hollywood swashbucklers.

*AmigaWorld*'s Bob Ryan liked the strategy most and the action least. After two or three goes at the catapult, he found, "you will be a catapult expert and bored by future breach attempts". He still called the game "a giant leap forward in computer game design".

## Made in a hurry

Bob Jacob had founded Cinemaware early in 1986 to make games with what he called a "mass market sensibility": "no typing, get you right into the game, no manual". Kellyn Beeck designed *Defender of the Crown*, and Jim Sachs, whom Jacob recruited as art director, led the artists, who drew in *Deluxe Paint*. Sachs remembered the three of them flying to the Amiga's makers in Los Gatos to talk over Beeck's ideas. "The Amiga was vital to the game, because I would not have been interested in doing it on a lesser machine." He and Beeck researched castles and costumes, and argued: real castle walls "would have been plastered smooth and painted, but most people these days expect them to be bare stone." He worked mostly in the Amiga's 320 by 200 pixel, 32-colour mode, and he would have liked a first-person sword fight. "There were many compromises due to deadlines."

The deadline nearly sank the game. "With about three months until our deadline, we scrapped all our code and hired legendary programmer RJ Mical to rewrite the game," the producer, John Cutter, told *Retro Gamer*. Beeck had designed it to run in real time; Cutter switched it to turns. "We shipped on time, but I don't know if my decision ultimately helped or hurt the project." Sachs called the work "pure hell. Several months of 20-hour days, with no days off." Maher records that the rush left a bug that doubled the player's army if the home castle was left undefended, and that neither Sachs nor Mical worked for Cinemaware again.

It sold anyway: 20,000 copies in its first six weeks, Maher reports, when about 150,000 Amigas were in use. The artist Jeff Hilbers explained Jacob's plan. Amiga sales "could never get much bigger than 30 or 40 thousand", so the company would build its name there, "then create a degraded version on the C64 that would really pull the money in."

## The Commodore 64 and other versions

Mirrorsoft released the Commodore 64 version in Britain in 1987, on disk only at £14.95; a reader asking *Zzap!64* for a cartridge was told the game needed about 300K. *Zzap!64* made it a Sizzler in August 1987. "No doubt many of you will have seen screen pictures of this game on a certain 16-bit machine, and thought, 'what on earth would a 64 version turn out like?'" one reviewer wrote, before praising "the finest static graphics to grace a 64 game". Maher notes that some of the ideas cut from the Amiga original went into the conversions, which "looked uglier but played better".

Versions followed for the Atari ST and the PC, and in 1988 *Amstrad Action* reported that Ubi Soft was converting it to the Amstrad CPC for Mirrorsoft. In 1989 *ACE* reported a CD-ROM edition for PCs from Mirrorsoft and Cinemaware, with a narrator and a recorded soundtrack. Konami published an NES version; Germany's *Video Games* reviewed it under Konami's Palcom label in January 1991 and gave it 52%, finding it heavy on strategy and suited to beginners.

## Later history

Sachs came back to the game alone. For Commodore's CD32 he made *Defender of the Crown II*, a "Director's Cut" with narration in five languages, new scenes and new music, in which the player raises a ransom for King Richard. "Commodore shipped a few copies just before shutting down operations," he said. "It was heartbreaking for me."

When the Cinemaware name was revived in 2000, a remastered *Defender of the Crown* came out for the Game Boy Advance, followed in 2003 by *Robin Hood: Defender of the Crown* for the PC, Xbox and PlayStation 2, and later by an iOS version. The rights passed to [Starbreeze](/vault/companies/starbreeze) in 2016 and to the publisher Nordcurrent in 2024; the [Cinemaware](/vault/companies/cinemaware) entry follows that story.
