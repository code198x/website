The villain of *Wizardry* is an evil wizard called Werdna, who rules a maze ten levels deep. Asked in 1983 where Werdna came from, the game's designer, Andrew Greenberg, had a short answer: "That's me; it's Andrew spelled backwards." *Wizardry* was a student project from Cornell University that became one of the best-selling programs of the early 1980s. It gave the player a whole party of adventurers to build and lose, and a dungeon that players had to map for themselves, square by square. In America it was one of the games that defined the computer role-playing game. In Japan it became something closer to a national pastime, and Japanese companies were still making *Wizardry* games long after its American publisher had stopped.

## Fast facts

- **Full title:** *Wizardry: Proving Grounds of the Mad Overlord*.
- **Designers:** Andrew Greenberg and Robert Woodhead, of Cornell University.
- **Publisher:** Sir-Tech Software of Ogdensburg, New York.
- **First release:** September 1981, for the 48K [Apple II](/vault/systems/apple-ii), at $49.95.
- **Language:** Apple Pascal, about 14,000 lines.
- **Series:** eight main games, from *Wizardry* (1981) to *Wizardry 8* (2001), and many more made in Japan.
- **Relations:** the [Western RPG](/vault/genres/western-rpg) of which it was an early landmark, and the [JRPG](/vault/genres/jrpg), which grew partly out of it.

## A bored student and a PLATO terminal

Greenberg told *Family Computing* in 1983 that he started *Wizardry* as an undergraduate at Cornell, during the reading period before exams, when he was so bored that a friend said, "Why don't you put Dungeons and Dragons on your computer?" He tried, then decided that Dungeons & Dragons was "unsuited for a computer" and began to experiment with something less rigid. Soon his room was full of friends play-testing the prototype, "day and night", for months.

Cornell was a PLATO site, part of the networked teaching system on which some of the first computer role-playing games had been written, and *COMPUTE!* described Greenberg as the manager of its PLATO facility. Woodhead later said that there were "dungeon games on PLATO in the same vein as Wizardry". The historian Jimmy Maher points to *Oubliette*, a PLATO game that had already used a first-person view of the dungeon, while finding the contents of *Wizardry*'s dungeon "completely original". *Wizardry*'s party of player-made characters is its own.

## Sir-Tech

Woodhead reached the game by way of a novelty business. *Computer Gaming World*'s 1992 history of Sir-Tech tells how his mother, Janice, ran the business with Fred Sirotek, Jr., and asked her son, a Cornell computer science student, to write a program that would show whether a rail shipment of sand was worth sending. Sirotek bought a $7,000 Apple for the job. Woodhead then wrote a mailing-list program, and took it and the Apple to the Trenton Computer Show, driven there by Sirotek's son Norman, who agreed to go because Atlantic City was nearby. Seeing the crowd round Woodhead's stand, Norman saw a business. The Siroteks started Sir-Tech, which began with Woodhead's space game *Galactic Attack*.

Then Woodhead showed the Siroteks the role-playing game written by his fellow student Greenberg. The two polished it together under the title *Dungeons of Despair*. According to *Family Computing*, Greenberg designed and programmed the prototype and Woodhead wrote the program code of the released game; *COMPUTE!* also credited Woodhead with "most of the actual coding". The first version was written in BASIC, which proved too slow, so the game was rewritten in Pascal, *Computer Gaming World*'s reviewer explained in 1982, and held back until a run-time system was available that let it run on Apples without the Pascal language system. Greenberg said Pascal was the only way to fit the game, about 14,000 lines of code, into a 48K computer: assembly language would have needed "five or six times as many code lines".

## Into the maze

*Wizardry* came out in September 1981, in a plastic bag with a photocopied manual, and cost $49.95. Players began in a castle town. At the training grounds they made characters: humans, elves, dwarves, gnomes or hobbits, of good, neutral or evil alignment, as fighters, mages, priests or thieves. A character who earned enough experience could change to one of the elite classes, bishop, lord, samurai or ninja, at the cost of all that experience and some years of age. Up to six characters formed a party in the tavern, bought weapons at Boltac's Trading Post and went down the stairs.

Below the castle lay the maze, ten levels deep, each a grid of about 20 squares by 20, shown as corridors drawn in outline in a small window in the corner of the screen. There was no map on screen. "Mapping is essential to survival in the game," wrote Mark Marlow in his *Computer Gaming World* review, and "one of the most challenging tasks is to complete an accurate map of all ten levels", because the designers had put tricks in to make mapping hard. Spells had names such as MILWA and LOMILWA, for light, and DUMAPIC, which told a lost party how far it was from the stairs; combined with MALOR, the teleport spell, it let an experienced party move around the maze in relative safety. Monsters were shown as a single picture. "We're not telling people a story," Greenberg said. "They're telling their own story."

Players grew attached to their characters. Greenberg said that "many players send in disks to Sir-tech and ask the company to retrieve dead characters", which Pascal made easy to do.

## A hit

By 30 June 1982, nine months after release, *Wizardry* had sold 24,000 copies, according to a *Computer Gaming World* list compiled from publishers' figures; *Ultima* had sold 20,000. Sir-Tech's own history, told to the same magazine in 1992, put the first year's sales at 100,000. Either way it kept selling: in 1983 *Family Computing* called it "one of the best-selling computer programs of any kind". In 1992 *Computer Gaming World* found Sir-Tech selling "millions of dollars" of games a year, with a past "totally dependent on the Wizardry series".

The first three games were called scenarios. Characters who finished one could be carried into the next: *Knight of Diamonds* (1982) and *Legacy of Llylgamyn* (1983), which Greenberg called "a major breakthrough" because it showed several menus at once, like the windows on Apple's Lisa.

## The later series

*Wizardry IV: The Return of Werdna* (1987), designed by Roe Adams, turned the series upside down. The player was Werdna, waking on a stone bier without his amulet, and had to fight up and out of the dungeon against more than 500 real *Wizardry* characters, taken from the disks players had sent to Sir-Tech. It was an "expert" game, and it let players save inside the dungeon for the first time. *Computer Gaming World* reported that year that the series was now being written on a Japanese MS-DOS computer, so that new games could come out on every format at once, "even in Japan".

*Wizardry V: Heart of the Maelstrom* followed in 1988, *Wizardry VI: Bane of the Cosmic Forge* in 1990 and David Bradley's *Wizardry VII: Crusaders of the Dark Savant* in 1992. Sir-Tech shipped 37,000 copies of *Crusaders* over the first weekend that it had finished boxes. The last American game, *Wizardry 8*, spent years in development. No major publisher would take it, and it reached the shops at the end of 2001 as an exclusive of the retailer Electronics Boutique, made by Sir-Tech Canada. *Computer Gaming World*'s reviewer said it "emerges from the ashes of what once was Sir-tech Canada", and the magazine made it its role-playing game of the year, recalling a time when *Wizardry*, *Ultima* and *Might and Magic* had been "PC gaming's grand triumvirate".

## Wizardry in Japan

In Japan the series found a bigger audience than at home. Roe Adams visited Japan in 1985 and reported in *Computer Gaming World* that "Both Wizardry and Ultima have huge followings in Japan". Japanese computer magazines covered [Richard Garriott](/vault/people/richard-garriott), as Lord British, the way American tabloids covered television stars, and when Woodhead introduced himself in a computer shop, people ran outside to shout that he was there. "He was practically mobbed by autograph seekers," Adams wrote.

A version for Nintendo's Famicom was, in *Nintendo Power*'s words in 1992, "a megahit in Japan", and a Super Famicom version was promised. A version for the [NES](/vault/systems/nintendo-entertainment-system) reached America in 1990 through the publisher Nexoft. Japanese designers learned from *Wizardry* when they built their own role-playing games. *Edge* wrote that *[Dragon Quest](/vault/games/dragon-quest)* blended "the quasi-medieval worlds and stark D&D mechanics of Ultima, Wizardry" and [Henk Rogers](/vault/people/henk-rogers)'s *The Black Onyx*, and one of the artists who made the first *[Final Fantasy](/vault/games/final-fantasy)* told *Retro Gamer* that *Dragon Quest* had combined *[Ultima](/vault/games/ultima)* and *Wizardry* with manga culture.

Japanese companies went on making their own *Wizardry* games, including *Wizardry Nemesis* and the *Wizardry Llylgamyn Saga* collection for the Sega Saturn. The taste lasted: in 2010 Takeshi Kajii, the Sony producer who proposed *[Demon's Souls](/vault/games/demons-souls)* to From Software, told *Edge*, "I've always loved the dark fantasy genre, from Wizardry right through to King's Field."

## After Sir-Tech

The name now belongs to a Japanese company. In October 2020 Drecom announced that it had acquired the copyrights and trademarks of the *Wizardry* series. It licensed the name to Digital Eclipse, which rebuilt *Proving Grounds of the Mad Overlord* on top of the original Apple II code, with new graphics, and released it as an early-access game in September 2023. The full release, for PC and consoles, followed in May 2024.

Andrew Greenberg died in August 2024, aged 67. After leaving games at the end of the 1980s he had become a lawyer specialising in intellectual property.
