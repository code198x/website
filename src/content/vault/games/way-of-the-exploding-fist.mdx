In the summer of 1985 a *ZZAP!64* writer visiting [Melbourne House](/vault/companies/melbourne-house) heard "unearthly screams" and found the staff crowded round a computer. They were playing *The Way of the Exploding Fist*, a karate game based, he was told, on the arcade game *Karate Champ*. After a trip to the local arcade to compare the two, he decided the home version was even better.

Plenty of people agreed. *Fist* was number one in the Commodore 64 charts that September, scored over 90% in *ZZAP!64* and *CRASH*, and was voted Game of the Year at the 1985 Golden Joysticks. It was written a long way from its British fans, in Melbourne, Australia, by [Beam Software](/vault/companies/beam-software), and *CRASH* later said it "founded the entire beat 'em up genre".

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** [Beam Software](/vault/companies/beam-software), Melbourne.
- **Publisher:** [Melbourne House](/vault/companies/melbourne-house).
- **Released:** 1985, on the Commodore 64 first (£9.95), then the Spectrum (£8.95).
- **Platforms:** [Commodore 64](/vault/systems/commodore-64), [ZX Spectrum](/vault/systems/sinclair-zx-spectrum), [Amstrad CPC](/vault/systems/amstrad-cpc), [BBC Micro](/vault/systems/bbc-micro) and Acorn Electron.
- **Credits (*CRASH*):** Greg (later Gregg) Barnett, Greg Holland, Stephan Taylor, Damian Watharow and William Teng. Barnett designed it.
- **Reviews:** *ZZAP!64* 93%; *CRASH* 92%.
- **Award:** Game of the Year, *C&VG* Golden Joystick Awards for 1985.
- **Sequels:** *Fist II: The Legend Continues* (1986) and *Exploding Fist +* (1988).

## The fight

One player, or two, fight a karate contest in front of oriental backdrops, watched by a judge whom *CRASH* described as "the little bald headed bloke with the Mexican moustache". Blows that impress him earn half a point, shown as a yin or a yang symbol, and a really well executed move earns a full yin-yang. Beat an opponent twice and you move up a rank. Against the computer you start by fighting a much-battered Novice and climb through eleven levels to the "exalted rank of 10th Dan"; lose a bout and you are sent back to the start.

The controls packed the moves onto a joystick. With the fire button up, the eight directions walk, jump, crouch, block, punch and somersault; with it held down, they become kicks, from the flying kick to the sweep and the roundhouse. Melbourne House advertised "18 different manoeuvres". *CRASH* advised learning the few that worked against low-ranked opponents first, because some were fast and safe and others, like the slow roundhouse, left you open. The graphics were large: Melbourne House told *ZZAP!64* that over 600 sprite images went into the animation. There were digitised grunts and cracks, and a thud when a fighter hit the floor.

## Reviews and charts

*ZZAP!64* reviewed the Commodore 64 version in August 1985 and gave it 93%. "As a Karate Champ fanatic I've been waiting for a decent 64 version for a long time," one reviewer wrote, and this one had "state-of-the-art animation and brilliant gameplay". It went to number one in both *Commodore User*'s general chart and its Commodore 64 chart in September.

The Spectrum version, at £8.95, arrived in October, and *CRASH* gave it 92%. It lacked the colour of the Commodore and Amstrad versions, but the reviewers found the speed and gameplay "nearly identical", thought the monochrome fighters as effective as "the Commodore's blocky sprites", and noticed that the computer opponent was more vicious. "It's here at last!" one began. "This is by far and away the best sports combat simulation available yet." *CRASH* also heard at a trade show that autumn that the Spectrum version had actually been finished first, although the Commodore 64 version came out first. On the Spectrum, too, *Your Sinclair* counted it among that winter's number ones on the Gallup chart. The game also came out for the BBC Micro and Acorn Electron, and *Acorn User* printed high scores for both in 1987.

At the Golden Joystick Awards for 1985, held on a Thames riverboat in 1986, *C&VG*'s readers made *Fist* Game of the Year and Melbourne House Software House of the Year, ending two years in which [Ultimate](/vault/companies/ultimate) had taken the top award. Alfred Milgrom, who owned both Beam and Melbourne House, later told *Edge* that *Fist* sold 500,000 copies across Europe.

## Rivals

*Fist* started a rush of karate games. [System 3](/vault/companies/system-3)'s [*International Karate*](/vault/games/international-karate) followed at the end of 1985, and reviewers judged it almost entirely against *Fist*. *CRASH* still preferred *Fist*; *Sinclair User* found "so little to choose between the two programs" that it came down to style, and the Commodore 64 version of *International Karate* was, in *ZZAP!64*'s view, "a damn sight better". Greg Barnett was not impressed by the rival's soundtrack, telling *ZZAP!64* in October 1986 that its music sounded like [Rob Hubbard](/vault/people/rob-hubbard)'s *Thing on a Spring* "slowed down".

## The sequels

*Fist II: The Legend Continues* (1986) was a different kind of game: an arcade adventure in which the hero crosses the scrolling lands of an evil warlord, fighting his soldiers and searching for eight scrolls. *CRASH* gave it 58% in March 1987, while noting that the original had "founded the entire beat 'em up genre, and imitations have poured forth ever since".

*Exploding Fist +* came out in late 1988 on the [Firebird](/vault/companies/firebird) label. It went back to straight fighting: *Your Sinclair*'s preview described opponents coming at the player in pairs, and a friend could join in to even the odds. *ZZAP!64* gave it 68%.

*Amstrad Action* was still saying in 1994 that "every one-on-one beat-em-up ever follows a direct lineage from" the original.
