*Contra* only ever had one arcade sequel, and it arrived quickly. In July 1988 *[ACE](/vault/magazines/ace-magazine)* noticed that the follow-up to Konami's *Gryzor* had been "showing up in West End arcades over the last few weeks", and that "even here, the machines are called Super Contra". *Super Contra* kept the two commandos and the alien invasion, and added stages seen from overhead. Two years later its NES version, shortened to *Super C*, gave American players the same mix with new level layouts. *[Nintendo Power](/vault/magazines/nintendo-power)* warned that it "will challenge even the top Power Players". For extra lives it had a code of its own, not the famous Konami Code.

## Fast facts

- **Developer and publisher:** [Konami](/vault/companies/konami).
- **Arcade:** *Super Contra*, 1988.
- **NES:** *Super C*, North America, spring 1990; in Europe, *Probotector II: Return of the Evil Forces*.
- **Other versions:** IBM PC and Amiga.
- **Players:** one or two at once.
- **Extra lives:** right, left, down, up, A, B, Start on the title screen gives ten lives.

## The arcade game

*Retro Gamer* describes *Super Contra* as "both the first and last arcade sequel in the series". It leaned harder on the films that had shaped the first game. An introduction featured "bad impressions" of Sylvester Stallone and Arnold Schwarzenegger, and the last stage borrowed from *Alien*: bipedal creatures in the style of the film's xenomorph, hatching eggs and "Facehugger-style enemies". It was the first *Contra* game with overhead sections. It also introduced Jagger Froid, the snake-armed final boss who came back in *Contra III: The Alien Wars*, *Contra 4* and *Contra ReBirth*.

The overhead stages brought the series' first smart bombs, called "shells". In Stuart Campbell's account for *Retro Gamer*, players started with none, and could find and use them only in the overhead stages. *Retro Gamer* thought the sequel had "better visuals and more varied enemies" than *[Contra](/vault/games/contra)*, but less interesting level design, because the sloping ground it introduced was overused and made the side-on stages feel like "straight left-to-right processions".

## Super C on the NES

*Nintendo Power* announced the NES version in its January/February 1990 issue, under the headline "a look into the future of NES Game Paks". "Contrary to speculation, there will be a NES sequel to everyone's favorite combat action game from Konami," it wrote, promising "the two player simultaneous play control that made its predecessor so popular". The next issue listed *Super C* among the games due in March, and previewed its stages and weapons. The Laser gave "both high powered and continuous shooting", a "new and improved Rapid Fire" helped players reach the later stages, the Spread made shots "fan out", and a "protective capsule" kept the player alive for a time.

The full review followed in May/June 1990. Its advice kept coming back to the Spread gun, called the Spreader: "using Spreader is almost always the key to defeating the toughest enemies." The final stage, it warned, began with "heat-seeking alien mines, explosive bubbles and swift monsters", and "Konami has spared no expense to challenge even the hottest Power Player". The same issue explained that *Super C* had arrived too late for the magazine's awards for 1989 and would have to wait a year.

As with the first game, the NES version used the arcade's settings but not its maps. *Retro Gamer* notes that its levels have "mostly different actual level layouts", and that the smart bomb was left out.

### Ten lives, not thirty

On the NES, the Konami Code (up, up, down, down, left, right, left, right, B, A, Start) gave *Contra* players 30 lives. *Super C* had its own sequence, which *Nintendo Power* printed in July/August 1990: on the title screen, press right, left, down, up, A, B and Start, and "your game will begin with ten fighters in reserve". For a two-player game, players selected the two-player option first and then entered the code. The same page gave a second secret: hold A and B and press Start on the title screen for a "Sound Mode" that played the game's music and effects. In January 1993 the magazine's Classified Information section set the two codes side by side: for *Super C*, "a new code was created which allows for ten fighters in reserve". The [Konami Code](/vault/culture/konami-code) entry follows the older code's history.

## Probotector II and other versions

As with the first game, European NES owners got *Super C* with robots in place of the human soldiers, as *Probotector II: Return of the Evil Forces*. *Total!* reviewed it in February 1993, at £45 and "out now", with jokes about the hero's "titanium torso" hitting the ground. It found the game hard but fair: "The controls are easy to learn and really responsive, the collision-detection is spot-on and there are no unavoidable deaths." Its verdict was "Probotector II is a classy game".

Home computers were part of the plan too. In October 1989 *Commodore Magazine* reported that *Super Contra* was "slated for release (on both the 64 and Amiga) during the first quarter of 1990". The Amiga version appeared in American mail-order price lists through 1990, and *Retro Gamer* lists conversions for the NES, PC and Amiga.

The Game Boy's *Operation C* (1991) was, in *Retro Gamer*'s words, "loosely based on Super Contra": the same settings again, with layouts even further from the NES game's. Later re-releases kept *Super C* in circulation. *Contra 4* on the Nintendo DS (2007) included the NES versions of *Contra* and *Super C* as bonus content, and by 2009 *Retro Gamer* could point readers to *Super Contra* on Xbox Live Arcade.
