*Mega Man 2* is the sequel that, in *Retro Gamer*'s words, "elevated Capcom's Astro Boy-style hero to classic status". The first *[Mega Man](/vault/games/mega-man)* had been, in one American magazine's words, "the sleeper hit of '88"; its follow-up sold 1.51 million copies and is still, by [Capcom](/vault/companies/capcom)'s own count, one of its million-sellers. It was made in a hurry. Its planner, Akira Kitamura, said the team had about three months, debugging included, and no time to polish anything: "Once something was finished, we'd check that it worked, and that was that." Players did not notice the rush. Britain's *[Mean Machines](/vault/magazines/mean-machines)* gave it 95% and told readers it "simply MUST be purchased as soon as possible".

## Fast facts

- **Developer and publisher:** [Capcom](/vault/companies/capcom).
- **Released:** December 1988 in Japan as *Rockman 2: Dr. Wily no Nazo* ("The Mystery of Dr. Wily"); June 1989 in North America; early 1991 in Britain, at £34.99.
- **Director and planner:** Akira Kitamura, who had directed the first game and left after this one.
- **Producer:** [Tokuro Fujiwara](/vault/people/tokuro-fujiwara).
- **Artist:** [Keiji Inafune](/vault/people/keiji-inafune).
- **Music:** Takashi Tateishi.
- **Sales:** 1.51 million, according to Capcom.

## Three months, and their own time

The first game had come from a small, young team led by Kitamura, and the sequel came from much the same people. [Keiji Inafune](/vault/people/keiji-inafune), who drew for both games, later described the second as a labour of love done around other work: "So we, of our own accord, got together, spent our own time, we worked really, really hard, you know, just 20-hour days to complete this, because we were making something we wanted to make." It was, he said, the best time of his working life at Capcom. *Wireframe* told the story the same way from the producer's side: a "double-or-quits sequel" that even Tokuro Fujiwara "wasn't sure about making at first", where "all the unpaid overtime soon proved worthwhile". Kitamura's memory, from a 2011 interview with the illustrator Hitoshi Ariga, is of the deadline: "In MM2 especially, we truly had no time. We hardly spent any time fine-tuning and polishing the bosses." The normal round of playtesting, trying every weapon in every place, did not happen. "If we hadn't done it like that, we never would have made the deadline."

Some of the design came from the audience. Kitamura wanted the new Robot Masters to help sell a game that, he said, had little "shelf appeal", so Capcom ran a contest in Japan for children to design bosses, and he put Dr. Wily front and centre on the flyer. The subtitle, *The Mystery of Dr. Wily*, was added for the same reason, to make the villain known.

Fujiwara, the arcade designer behind *[Ghosts 'n Goblins](/vault/games/ghosts-n-goblins)*, had moved across to Capcom's console side, reluctantly at first, and overseeing *Mega Man 2* was his first job there. *Wireframe* dates his most inventive work from that move, calls *Mega Man 2* "a 1.5 million-selling smash", and says he kept working on the series until the mid-1990s.

## Eight Robot Masters

The structure is the first game's, enlarged. Dr. Wily is back, and this time eight robots guard the way to his fortress: Metal Man, Air Man, Bubble Man, Quick Man, Crash Man, Flash Man, Heat Man and Wood Man. The player picks the order. Beating each one gives Mega Man its weapon: the Metal Blade, Air Shooter, Bubble Lead, Quick Boomerang, Crash Bombs, Time Stopper, Atomic Fire and Leaf Shield. *Retro Gamer* pointed out that the "two extra robotic bosses" set the number for the sequels that followed. Once all eight are down, Mega Man goes into Wily's castle, a run of further stages that includes a rematch with all eight.

Each stage is built around its master. Air Man's is in the sky, where *[Nintendo Power](/vault/magazines/nintendo-power)* warned of the Lightning Lord and his chariot rising out of the clouds. Heat Man's has disappearing platforms, and Quick Man's has laser beams that *[Club Nintendo](/vault/magazines/club-nintendo)* called "lethal". *Mean Machines* thought the game used "every dirty trick ever seen in any platform game".

The weapons are the point. Kitamura said that for the sequel his team designed the weapons first, deciding how each one moved on screen, and only then matched them to bosses, so that a player watching a boss closely might guess which weapon hurt it. His example was the Quick Boomerang against Metal Man: it is the only weapon that keeps firing while the button is held, and Metal Man reacts to each press of the button, so hold it down and he does nothing. Metal Man's own Metal Blade is even more effective against him: a couple of hits finish him. "Metal Man is a cinch," *Nintendo Power* told readers, recommending "a taste of his own medicine". That was deliberate. Asked by Ariga about the two-hit kill, Kitamura said it was written into the planning documents as "a 'hidden trick'", because a game without secrets struggled to get into the magazines. *Club Nintendo* called the Metal Blade "the most versatile weapon that Mega Man can collect".

## Passwords, tanks and a second difficulty

The first game had no way to save progress, which *Retro Gamer* counted among the reasons it was the harder of the two. *Mega Man 2* gives the player a password that returns him to where he left off, marked as positions on a grid of lettered and numbered squares; *Club Nintendo* thought the system made writing passwords down "quite fun". It also added Energy Tanks, which store energy to be used from the pause screen, and three devices from Mega Man's creator, Dr. Light, handed over after the Air Man, Heat Man and Flash Man stages: a jet sled, a platform that climbs walls and a platform that floats upwards.

The Western cartridges begin with a choice of Normal or Difficult, which *Mean Machines* listed as two skill levels. According to IGN, as cited by Wikipedia, Difficult is the game as released in Japan, which had no choice; Normal was added for the export versions and makes Mega Man's weapons do more damage.

## Music

Takashi Tateishi wrote the music. His first attempts did not please Kitamura, who remembered them as "extremely cutesy sounding, like something from *Bubble Bobble* or *Fantasy Zone*". Kitamura told him to stop concentrating on melody and to let rhythm carry the stages, to match the speed of the running and jumping. The results included the stage themes for Crash Man, Heat Man and Quick Man, whose "intense music", Kitamura said, adds to the tension, and the theme of Wily's first castle stage. Kitamura called that stage "the real heart of MM2", the first place where the player controls a fully powered Mega Man. Reviewers noticed the music: *Mean Machines* gave the sound 92% for its "funky tunes", and *Club Nintendo* praised the "rocking soundtracks".

## Reception

The American press was expecting it. *Electronic Gaming Monthly*, in its first issue in January 1989, called *Mega Man 2* a follow-up to "the sleeper hit of '88" and Capcom's "SPECIAL ATTRACTION for May", and *Nintendo Power* gave it a strategy feature in its July/August 1989 issue. In Britain, where the game arrived at the start of 1991, *Mean Machines* scored it 95%, with 96% for both graphics and playability, and said it had "probably the greatest variety of backdrops and sprites yet seen in a NES game". *Club Nintendo* went further: "'Mega Man 2' is faultless."

Later writers were a little cooler. *Retro Gamer*, looking back in 2008, called the game "notoriously arduous" and noted that some players had found working out which weapon beat which boss "far too much trial and error", but still said it "is widely regarded as the zenith" of the series. Its 2013 list of Capcom's best games said it did "nothing that remarkable from a design perspective over the first game", but polished it "wonderfully".

## Legacy

*Mega Man 2* sold 1.51 million copies, according to Capcom's list of its million-sellers. *Wireframe* gave a figure of 1.5 million. Kitamura left after completing it, and the series carried on under Fujiwara as producer, with Inafune as artist, through four more NES games. When Capcom revived the original series with *Mega Man 9* in 2009, deliberately in 8-bit style, *Wireframe* wrote that its graphics, sound and tough play all took "direct inspiration from Mega Man 2".
