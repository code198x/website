"Edge will admit it had its doubts," began the magazine's review in January 2003. Nintendo had handed one of its most revered series to an outside studio "only a few had heard of", and that studio, Retro Studios of Austin, Texas, was making it as a first-person game. When [Shigeru Miyamoto](/vault/people/shigeru-miyamoto) held back the playable demo at the E3 show in 2001 because of worries about the controls, "alarm bells rang". *Metroid Prime* then earned nine out of ten, and four months later *Edge* named it game of the year: the finest game the magazine had played in twelve months, and "one of the finest 2D-to-3D transitions yet seen".

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** [Retro Studios](/vault/companies/retro-studios), Austin, Texas. **Publisher:** [Nintendo](/vault/companies/nintendo).
- **Released:** November 2002 in North America, at $50; February 2003 in Japan; 21 March 2003 in Britain.
- **Key people:** Mark Pacini (lead designer) and Todd Keller (lead artist) at Retro; Kensuke Tanabe and Shigeru Miyamoto at Nintendo.
- **Setting:** the planet Tallon IV.
- **Visors:** combat, scan, thermal and X-ray.
- **Sequels:** *Metroid Prime 2: Echoes* (2004), *Metroid Prime 3: Corruption* (2007), *Metroid Prime 4: Beyond* (2025).

## A studio in Texas

Retro Studios was set up in 1998 with Nintendo's backing, and partly staffed with people from Iguana, the Austin studio behind *Turok*. Iguana's founder, Jeff Spangenberg, had sold it to Acclaim and used the money to start the new company. It began several GameCube projects at once, among them an American football game, a vehicle combat game and an action-adventure. *Retro Gamer*'s account of the development says Miyamoto was not impressed by most of what he was shown on a visit, but liked one team's action-adventure prototype with a female lead, and Retro was given *[Metroid](/vault/games/metroid)*.

The idea of seeing through Samus Aran's eyes came from Miyamoto. "Mr Miyamoto came to us with the idea of playing a Metroid game from the first-person perspective," Pacini recalled. "He felt that the best way to play a 3D game where the character had a gun was from a first-person viewpoint. At first, we were not seeing his vision and we knew the fans would have the same initial reaction." Early screens show Samus in third person in the Chozo Ruins. In 2002 Nintendo bought a majority of Retro's shares, Spangenberg left, the other projects were cancelled and many staff were laid off; the rest of the team worked on to finish the game.

## A first-person adventure

Nintendo called it a first-person adventure, not a shooter. "To us," Pacini said, that "meant that the core gameplay was not about shooting enemies but rather the immersive experience of being Samus and exploring the environment." Their model was the Super NES game: "Our main inspiration was [Super Metroid](/vault/games/super-metroid). That's all you need."

Tanabe, who oversaw the game for Nintendo, asked Retro for "a visor system ... in order to make it clearly new and differentiated from first-person shooter games". The scan visor turned the world into something to read. Samus could scan almost anything, from creatures to the logs left by space pirates, and each scan was stored. *Edge* found that scanning a new room before doing anything else "soon becomes second nature". The thermal visor showed enemies in the dark. In a fight the player could lock on to an enemy and circle it instead of aiming freely, and when Samus rolled into her Morph Ball the camera pulled back to third person for puzzles, half-pipes and magnetic rails. The planet was one continuous place. "The player was able to play the entire game without a break in the action," Pacini said, because there were no loading screens.

Everything was arranged the way the 2D games had been. Samus begins with few abilities, finds doors and ledges she cannot yet pass, and comes back later with the upgrade that opens them. *Edge* wrote that "deconstructing the layout and deciphering clues left in the design by the developer to find routes to a new room is an absolute joy", and praised a rotating 3D map. It had complaints too. Strafing by holding the left shoulder button, and looking up and down by holding the right, felt awkward at first. The grapple beam arrived too late in the adventure, crash bugs got through, and making some scanning compulsory struck the magazine as "unnecessarily punitive" for completists.

Owners of the Game Boy Advance *Metroid Fusion*, released the same month in America, could link the two with a cable. That unlocked Samus's Fusion suit and a copy of the original NES *Metroid*.

## Reception

*Edge*'s review found that the series had been recreated so faithfully that "within minutes of starting the game everything feels exactly the way you'd expect it to". In its awards in May 2003 the magazine named *Metroid Prime* game of the year, ahead of *[Neverwinter Nights](/vault/games/neverwinter-nights)*, and gave it its audio award, for a soundtrack and sound effects that worked together to "envelop the player". It placed Retro Studios third among developers of the year, and argued that whatever Nintendo's involvement, "the majority of the work (and by definition significant development decisions) remained the responsibility of the Austin-based team".

## Later history

Retro made two sequels with Tanabe as Nintendo's producer: *Metroid Prime 2: Echoes* for the GameCube in 2004, directed by Pacini, and *Metroid Prime 3: Corruption* for the Wii in 2007. The three were collected for the Wii, with pointer controls, in 2009. Retro went on to make *Donkey Kong Country Returns* (2010), and when *Edge* interviewed Tanabe and Retro's president, Michael Kelbaugh, in 2011, they described how Retro had "gradually come to really think like Nintendo". In February 2023 Nintendo released *Metroid Prime Remastered* for the Switch, rebuilt by Retro, and *Metroid Prime 4: Beyond* followed in December 2025.
