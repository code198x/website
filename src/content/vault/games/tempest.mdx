*Tempest* began as a bad dream. When [Dave Theurer](/vault/people/dave-theurer) finished *[Missile Command](/vault/games/missile-command)* for [Atari](/vault/companies/atari), he set out to make a "first-person *[Space Invaders](/vault/games/space-invaders)*", and it did not work. Gene Lipkin, whom Theurer calls "the head guy at Atari at the time", played it and said "ho-hum". Theurer went back to a recurring dream from his childhood, of monsters creeping up out of a deep, dark pit in the ground, and his supervisor, Steve Calfee, told him to build it. The result, released in October 1981, put the player on the rim of a wireframe tube drawn in coloured light, with things climbing up it. *Electronic Games* called it "not only the most visually striking coin-op around, but one of the most challenging as well."

## Fast facts

- **Maker:** [Atari](/vault/companies/atari), coin-op division.
- **Design and program:** [Dave Theurer](/vault/people/dave-theurer); project leader Morgan Hoff, hardware engineer Doug Snyder, technician Mary Pepper.
- **Released:** October 1981. The copyright registration gives May 1981 as the date the game was first shown outside Atari, most likely on field test.
- **Display:** colour [vector](/vault/techniques/vector-graphics), Atari's first; *Electronic Games* called it "the first full-color quadrascan coin-op".
- **Controls:** a [spinner](/vault/hardware/spinner) knob and two buttons, fire and Superzapper.
- **Levels:** 16 tube shapes and 99 levels.
- **Sales:** about 30,000 machines, according to *The Atari Book*.
- **Home versions:** Electric Dreams' official conversions for the Amstrad CPC and ZX Spectrum, 1986–87.

## From Space Invaders to a tube

Theurer's first version moved rows of aliens towards the player in 3D. He told *Retro Gamer* that it had "enough reference points" to look interesting but not to play: players could not judge where an enemy was, so they could not aim. The fix was to glue everything to a surface. The enemies stayed stuck to the walls of a tube and the player's ship stayed on its rim, so both sides could only move along lines the eye could follow. Every shape was drawn first on squared graph paper, "because there was no appropriate design tool at the time for this type of graphics".

One early version made people ill. "The first version had the surface moving and the player's object stationary," Theurer said. "Personally, I got nauseated after working on it for a period of time. Some others had a similar reaction." It was dropped long before the game went out on test.

The name took several tries. The team called it *Aliens* at first, and the source code still uses that name: its modules are ALEXEC, the "aliens executive", ALDISP, ALSOUN and so on. The prototype cabinet said *Vortex*, until players joked about what that sounded like. Theurer lobbied for *Tempest* when the staff voted on a list of names.

## Colour on a vector screen

Until 1981 vector games drew in white, and makers laid coloured plastic over the screen to fake colour. Atari's colour vector display was new hardware, still being developed alongside the game. "It was colour. It was 3D. It was fast. It was new!" Theurer remembered. It was also fragile. Owen Rubin, who had expected the first colour vector board for his own *Space Duel*, recalled that the prototype "would constantly blow out the deflection amps" and, at first, drew more slowly than the black-and-white version. Theurer's warning was worse: "if you parked the beam off the screen for too long, it would burn a hole in the phosphor coating".

The [vector graphics](/vault/techniques/vector-graphics) entry explains how the display worked and where *Tempest* sits among Atari's QuadraScan games.

## Playing it

The ship, which *Video Games* called "horseshoe-shaped", runs around the rim of a tube that stretches into the distance. Some tubes are closed, like a circle or a heart; others are open, like a V or a flat line. *Electronic Games* counted "flat-edged (with 15 corridors) and rounded (possessing 16 sectors)". Enemies appear at the far end and climb towards the player, who spins the knob to reach the right lane and fires down it.

The period guides give the enemies' order of arrival:

| Enemy | First level | What it does |
|-------|-------------|--------------|
| Flippers | 1 | Flip from lane to lane; one that reaches the rim can carry the ship away |
| Tankers | 3 | Split into two flippers when shot |
| Spikers | 3 | Leave green spikes in the lanes, which can impale the ship between levels |
| Fuseballs | 11 | Zip up and down the lanes; *Video Games* warned, "Don't let it climb onto the rim" |
| Pulsars | 17 | Electrify their lane |

From level 33 some tankers carry fuseballs instead of flippers, and from level 40 some carry pulsars. From level 65, *The Atari Book* says, the tubes turn invisible.

The Superzapper is the emergency button. The first use on each level "obliterates all alien life forms on the screen", *Electronic Games* explained; a second use destroys only one enemy, the most dangerous. *Video Games Player* set its readers a puzzle on exactly that: a fuseball is climbing, the first zap is spent, and the answer is that the second one "will single out the fuseball and vaporize it".

At the end of a level the ship dives down the tube to the next one, which is why the spikes matter. Theurer's option to start on a higher level, which paid a bonus for the choice, was his idea: "We wanted to maximise the fun time/total play time ratio."

The spinner was part of the design once the tube arrived. "The controller has a fabulous feel when you spin it," Theurer said. "Just the right amount of resistance and just the right amount of angular momentum when you stop applying the force."

## The booby trap

Just before production Theurer moved the Atari logo on the start screen because it looked lopsided. He had forgotten his own protection against pirates who replaced "Atari" with their own name: the game would then overwrite a random location in memory a few minutes later. Moving the logo set it off. When the score fell in a certain range, the address it chose was the coin counter, and some players found themselves with 40 free credits. "It took us two months to figure out the cause!"

## Home versions

Atari's own home versions stalled. A version for the Atari 5200 was finished far enough to appear, boxed, in the film *Cloak & Dagger*, but it stayed unreleased until a prototype surfaced in 1998.

The first official home versions came from Electric Dreams. *Computer Gamer* reviewed the Amstrad CPC version in December 1986, noting that it had been written for the Amstrad first "because it is the slowest of the big three machines". The ZX Spectrum version, programmed by David Pridmore, followed early in 1987. *CRASH* gave it 62%, finding it "a good try at a game not really suited to this Spectrum"; *Your Sinclair* called it "true to the original, in all its spindly detail".

## Tempest 2000 and after

[Jeff Minter](/vault/people/jeff-minter) had loved *Tempest* since he found it in a Piccadilly Circus arcade. In 1994 he wrote *[Tempest 2000](/vault/games/tempest-2000)* for Atari's Jaguar. *Digital Press* expected other magazines to rave "over the techno music", and its reviewer, who had written the Jaguar off, was won over: "The smartest thing Atari could do right now is pack this game in with every Jaguar." Minter came back to the tube several times, as his own entry tells; the latest, *Tempest 4000*, made by his company [Llamasoft](/vault/companies/llamasoft) and published by Atari, came out for PlayStation 4, Xbox One and PC on 17 July 2018.

## Source code

The original source code for the arcade game is public. The historicalsource archive published it on GitHub in October 2021, with no licence stated. The programs were written in 6502 assembly language with Atari's MAC65 assembler, and the files carry Theurer's name as programmer and dates from August 1980 to December 1981.
