The boxer facing you is the thing to watch. A change of pose or a familiar attack can give you an opening, provided you recognise it and respond in time. *Punch-Out!!* makes that process of learning an opponent the centre of its boxing.

[Nintendo](/vault/companies/nintendo)'s 1984 arcade game places the player behind a see-through, mesh-like fighter. The opponent remains visible through him. The cabinet uses two screens: portraits and statistics above, the fight below.

## The arcade ring

In Nintendo's later *Iwata Asks* interview, Genyo Takeda and Shigeru Miyamoto recalled being asked to make a game that used two televisions, partly to use existing stock. They settled on boxing and stacked the displays vertically. This was a design decision behind a particular cabinet, rather than evidence that boxing inherently required two screens.

Bill Kunkel's November 1984 *Electronic Games* review described separate left and right punch buttons and a larger button for a knockout punch. He praised the presentation and visible opponent, but found the repeated circuit less interesting after the player had mastered it. His review records both the initial appeal and that reservation.

The arcade sequel, *Super Punch-Out!!*, introduced ducking. Hamster's licensed catalogue dates it to 1984, the same year as the original, rather than the following year given in some retrospective accounts.

## Little Mac's career

The 1987 NES adaptation, *Mike Tyson's Punch-Out!!*, made the player's fighter Little Mac, accompanied by trainer Doc Louis. Mario referees, and Mike Tyson waits at the end of the career. The smaller, solid player character replaces the arcade game's transparent figure.

Progress runs through the Minor, Major and World circuits. Passwords awarded at promotions let the player resume from a later circuit. Between bouts, learning how to handle a particular opponent matters more than simply throwing punches quickly.

Mac can aim high or low, dodge and block. His hearts track his ability to keep punching, while stars provide special uppercuts. An exhausted Mac must avoid attacks until he can recover. These resources give successful defence a role in the next offensive opportunity.

The game was prominent in *Nintendo Power*'s first issue in July/August 1988: first in the players' choices and second in the combined chart. Those rankings document that audience's enthusiasm; they are not worldwide sales figures.

Nintendo subsequently replaced Tyson with Mr. Dream in a revised NES edition. *Club Nintendo*'s 1991 British review explicitly compared the two versions and identified the changed champion. A separate gold-cartridge prize edition also preceded normal retail sale, as Nintendo designer Makoto Wada recalled in *Iwata Asks*.

## Later rounds

*Super Punch-Out!!* reached the Super Nintendo in 1994, with returning fighters and new opponents. This is a different game from the identically named arcade sequel. Nintendo's development interview discusses its later Japanese release in 1998, which should not be mistaken for the first release anywhere.

The Wii's *Punch-Out!!* followed in 2009, developed with Next Level Games. Producer Kensuke Tanabe said the NES version supplied its starting point. Returning opponents become harder on a second encounter, extending the same process: notice a pattern, learn a response, then adjust when the boxer changes it.
