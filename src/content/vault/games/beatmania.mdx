In 1998 *Edge* set its readers a quiz: what was the most popular arcade game that year for Konami, one of the world's biggest arcade makers? The clue was that it ran on "ten-year-old technology", and the answer, "few westerners would" know, was *Beatmania*, a game in which the player is a club DJ. The cabinet had five keys and a record turntable. Notes fell down the screen in time with a dance track, and the player hit the matching key, or scratched the record, as each one arrived. Crowds gathered to watch. Konami named a whole family of music games after it, Bemani, and in Japan it is still running.

## Fast facts

- **Developer and publisher:** [Konami](/vault/companies/konami).
- **Arcade release:** Japan, December 1997, after a showing at the JAMMA trade show that autumn.
- **Controls:** five keys and a turntable for each player; the cabinet takes two players.
- **Home versions:** PlayStation (Japan, 1998, with a turntable controller); a Game Boy version in 1999; a European PlayStation edition in 2000.
- **Successors:** yearly "mixes", then *Beatmania IIDX* (1999), with seven keys; the original series ended with *Beatmania The Final* in 2002.
- **Its name lives on in:** [Bemani](/vault/companies/bemani), Konami's label for its music games, from *[Dance Dance Revolution](/vault/games/dance-dance-revolution)* to *Guitar Freaks*.

## A DJ in the arcade

Konami showed *Beatmania* at the JAMMA arcade show in Tokyo in the autumn of 1997, where *Edge* noticed "the first game based on - get this - DJ mixing". It went into Japanese arcades that December. The man in charge of its music, Hiroshi Takyasu, manager of Konami's sound development department, told *Edge* he had not expected much: "I didn't think Beat-Mania would become as popular as it is now. It is much more than I expected."

The idea was close to Sony's *[PaRappa the Rapper](/vault/games/parappa-the-rapper)*, which had been a hit on the PlayStation in Japan the year before: match the prompts on screen in time with the music. *Beatmania* gave it an instrument. The prompts scroll down five lanes towards a line at the bottom of the screen, like "digital sheet music", and each lane belongs to a key. Every so often a note calls for the turntable, and between the set notes the player is encouraged into short "freestyle" scratching. "Needless to say," *Edge* wrote, "early attempts can get quite messy." Takyasu's team wanted the music played in clubs, and the first game's tracks had titles like "Overdozer" and "Love So Groovy".

## Players show off

Takyasu thought he knew why it caught on: "players can make a good-looking performance in front of other people looking on. Players can show off. This is very important for Beat-Mania." And the audience mattered more than the pictures: "The players themselves aren't watching the graphics - the gallery is watching." Koji Okamoto, general manager of two of Konami's research and development departments, agreed: "Graphics are important, but we do not believe that they are a crucial factor to a good game." In Tokyo's Shibuya district, *Edge* reported, "large crowds gather around experienced players", and some used the game more to show off their DJ technique than to play it.

Konami kept the game fresh with new songs. *Beatmania 2nd Mix* followed in 1998, and *3rd Mix* added a battle mode in which two players compete for the higher score. Britain saw *3rd Mix* in its arcades in early 1999. *Arcade* magazine's reviewer found "crowds of people watching the coin-op DJs torture the turntables" at his local arcade and called it "definitely a chart topper".

Its success changed Konami's arcade business. By February 1999 music games were the leading genre at Japan's AOU show, "inspired by the success of Konami's Beat-Mania series", and Konami had a name for them: Bemani, from *Beatmania*. *Edge* later reported that the company had coined the word and copyrighted it, to claim the genre as its own. Konami's Games & Music Division was renamed the Bemani division. Its next games put other instruments in the arcade: *Pop'n Music* (1998), with big buttons for younger players; *Dance Dance Revolution* (1998), played with the feet; and *Guitar Freaks* and *Drummania* (1999), which could be linked for a two-player band. In December 2000 *Newsweek* reported that Konami had sold 25,000 *Beatmania* machines in three years, in an industry where selling 1,000 counted as a success.

## At home

A PlayStation version of *2nd Mix* came out in Japan in the autumn of 1998, with 27 tracks, 19 from the arcade and eight new ones, a training mode and a "DJ demo" mode for recording a performance. ASCII made a turntable controller for it, sold separately at ¥5,800. *Edge* reported that it "sold like hot cakes". Append discs of new songs followed, and a Game Boy version, *Beatmania GB*, was fourth in Japan's all-format sales chart in the spring of 1999. *Edge* counted more than 40 home versions by 2003, "from PSone to WonderSwan".

The West was harder. At the ATEI trade show in London in January 1998, *Edge* reported, the cabinet with its "twin record deck" "failed to put many delegates in a spin". Konami's European PlayStation edition, released in Britain in 2000 with its own controller, added tracks from British acts such as Moloko. *Edge* looked back in 2004: "*Beatmania* did get a UK release on PSone, but players either found it too hard, too expensive or just felt stupid bashing away at a plastic turntable." Konami tried its next music game in Britain with more caution, and it was *Dance Dance Revolution*, as *Dancing Stage*, that became the hit.

## IIDX and after

At the AOU show in February 1999 Konami showed *Beatmania IIDX*, in a deluxe cabinet with a large plasma screen, extra speakers and "a bass vibration shaker". It gave each player seven keys, two more than the original, beside the turntable. The five-key series ended in 2002 with *Beatmania The Final*, but IIDX carried on. *Edge* counted 39 distinct *Beatmania* cabinets in the 12 years to 2010, and its critics came from the West too: Alex Rigopulos of [Harmonix](/vault/companies/harmonix), the studio that went on to make *[Guitar Hero](/vault/games/guitar-hero)* and *[Rock Band](/vault/games/rock-band)*, told *Edge* that the Bemani games were "sorely deficient in a number of key respects", but that seeing them succeed in Japan showed what music games could do. *Edge*'s own verdict was that "the scrolling note charts, coloured prompts and peripherals that define modern rhythm-gaming are all Konami creations".

The IIDX series is still going. Konami Amusement put *Beatmania IIDX 33 Sparkle Shower* into Japanese arcades on 17 September 2025.
