In August 1982 *[Computer & Video Games](/vault/magazines/computer-and-video-games)* described a new arcade game in which "the deep blue fortress of Zaxxon floats in space at the frontier of a third dimension in arcade games". Sega's *Zaxxon* was a scrolling shooter, much like *[Scramble](/vault/games/scramble)*, but it looked down on its fortress from above one corner. Walls had height, so the player's ship had height too, and a pilot who misjudged it flew straight into the brickwork. Looking back in 1990, *[ACE](/vault/magazines/ace-magazine)* called it "the first of the 'gasp look at those graphics' games".

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** [Sega](/vault/companies/sega); American cabinets of the time carried the Sega/Gremlin name.
- **Released:** 1982 in the arcades (*ACE* later dated the launch to March 1982).
- **View:** a diagonal, three-quarter view from above, drawn with no perspective (see [isometric projection](/vault/techniques/isometric-projection)).
- **Home versions:** [ColecoVision](/vault/systems/colecovision), Atari 2600 and Intellivision (all by Coleco); Atari 8-bit and Apple II (Datasoft); Commodore 64 (Synapse); ZX Spectrum (US Gold).
- ***CRASH*:** 58% for the Spectrum version, issue 14, March 1985.
- **Follow-ups:** *Super Zaxxon*, *Zaxxon 3-D* for the Sega Master System, and *Zaxxon's Motherbase 2000* for the 32X.

## Flying by the shadow

On a flat screen, height is hard to show. *Zaxxon* gave the player two clues. An altimeter at the left of the playfield showed the ship's height, and the ship cast a shadow on the ground below it. *Electronic Fun with Computers & Games* counted the shadow among the ways a flat screen could give "the illusion of 3-D". *C&VG* added that the ship itself looked larger or smaller with height.

It mattered because the fortress was built to catch the careless. The ship had to climb over walls and slip through "the tiny gaps between the walls and the electronic barrier", as *C&VG* put it, but the fuel tanks that kept it flying sat low on the ground, and the enemy planes could only be hit at the right altitude. The controls were set like an aircraft's: pushing the joystick forward dived, and pulling it back climbed. *Video Games* warned its readers in October 1982 to "remember up is down and vice versa", and to expect "a lot of rough landings into walls. (Yes, Virginia, there are bricks in space!)"

A mission had three parts. The ship flew over the first fortress, shooting gun emplacements, missiles and fuel tanks and strafing planes on their runways. It then crossed open space in a dogfight with the enemy fleet, and reached a second, better-defended fortress. At the end waited the robot Zaxxon and its homing missile, which needed six hits to destroy. Beat it, and the mission started again, harder.

Not everyone was convinced by the design. *Electronic Games* thought the only problem with the game was "the difficulty of piloting the player's ship", and *ACE* in 1990 thought "its gameplay was pretty limited – but it looked fantastic".

## "The game that couldn't be brought home"

That was what people called *Zaxxon*, Charles Ardai wrote in *Electronic Fun* in December 1983, before it was brought home in many versions.

[Coleco](/vault/systems/colecovision) made the console versions. The ColecoVision cartridge was one of the machine's first games in 1982, and in September 1983 *Video Games Player* named it Coleco's best-selling cartridge. Coleco also promised versions for the Atari 2600 and the Intellivision, which appeared later. The 2600 had no hope of the arcade picture: looking back in 2000, *Classic Gamer Magazine* found that it "didn't even duplicate the diagonally scrolling graphics that made the arcade version so unique", which left "just another vertical shooter".

The home computers came closer. Datasoft converted the game to the Atari 8-bit computers and the Apple II. Ardai reviewed the Apple version and thought it "certainly the best version of this arcade classic that I've ever seen", down to "the bricks in the walls, your bubble shaped cockpit, even the artwork on the floor of the fortress". Synapse Software adapted it for the Commodore 64, and in July 1984 *Ahoy!* listed it at number 13 in a chart of the best-selling entertainment software.

## On the Spectrum

The official Spectrum version came late. Several Spectrum games had already borrowed the idea: *CRASH* named *Zaxxan* by Starzone and *Havoc* by Dynavision as earlier attempts, and called *Havoc* "a Zaxxon game" when it reviewed it in February 1985. The next month it reviewed US Gold's official *Zaxxon*, at £7.95, and was disappointed. To keep colour clash down, the Spectrum game left out the fortress floor and back wall, so that "everything seems to hang in empty blackness", and the reviewers found the controls sluggish. One suggested the old trick for judging height: fire at a wall until the shots pass over it. The verdict was "a grave disappointment", and the overall score was 58%.

## Sequels

Sega followed the game with *Super Zaxxon*, which HesWare sold for the Commodore 64 with "tighter passageways", a tunnel and "a fire-breathing dragon that makes Zaxxon's robot look like a wimp". *ACE* thought Sega was still "failing to innovate in the game design department" with it. *Zaxxon 3-D* for the Master System used Sega's 3-D glasses. *[Computer Gaming World](/vault/magazines/computer-gaming-world)* called it "the most amazing experience in depth-of-field gaming ever produced for the mass market" in 1988. *Zaxxon's Motherbase 2000* came to the 32X in the mid-1990s. The fanzine *Digital Press* thought it "missed the mark".

## Was it the first?

*Zaxxon* is often described as the first isometric arcade game. The magazine *Wireframe* said so in 2019: "The first isometric game to hit arcades was Sega's pseudo-3D shooter, Zaxxon", adding that *[Q\*bert](/vault/games/qbert)* came out the same year. The period press was more careful. *Video Games* called it "another of Sega's clever pseudo-3-D designs", after Sega's racing game *Turbo*, and *C&VG* placed it at "the frontier" of a third dimension rather than claiming it had crossed first. What the sources agree on is that it made the diagonal view famous. When Sandy White showed his *[Ant Attack](/vault/games/ant-attack)* to *[Sinclair User](/vault/magazines/sinclair-user)* in 1983, the 3D game he compared it with was *Zaxxon*.
