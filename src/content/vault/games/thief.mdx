"A game where the enemies are to be avoided and where the only bloodshed is usually that of your character — can Looking Glass be serious?" That was *Edge*'s opening question when it reviewed *Thief: The Dark Project* in January 1999. In a year of first-person shooters, [Looking Glass Studios](/vault/companies/looking-glass) had made a first-person game in which fighting was usually a mistake. The player is Garrett, a professional burglar in a gloomy city of magic and 19th-century machinery, and his tools are shadows, silence and patience. *Edge* decided the studio was serious: the game "manages to create a whole new spin on an overly familiar genre".

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** [Looking Glass Studios](/vault/companies/looking-glass), Massachusetts.
- **Publisher:** [Eidos](/vault/companies/eidos) Interactive.
- **Released:** December 1998, for Windows PCs.
- **Price:** £40 according to *Edge*, £44.99 according to *PC Zone*; $49.99 in the United States.
- **Project director:** Greg LoPiccolo.
- **Engine:** Looking Glass's own, later known as the Dark engine, which *System Shock 2* also used.
- **Sequels:** *Thief Gold* (1999), *Thief II: The Metal Age* (2000), *Thief: Deadly Shadows* (2004) and the reboot *Thief* (2014).

## From Quake rival to burglary game

When *Edge* first visited in early 1997, *The Dark Project* looked like one more game in the wake of *[Quake](/vault/games/quake)*: a first-person game with polygon enemies, a medieval setting and "volumetric rendering, with ray-cast lighting". The project director, Greg LoPiccolo, told the magazine that the comparison ended there. "In essence you're a thief in this undefined medieval age, sort of medieval meets 'Brazil' meets 'City of Lost Children'," he said, with "some electricity, some magic, and some 19th century machinery kind of stuff". Already, he said, "much of the actual gameplay involves using shadows effectively. You're constantly sneaking around and making decisions: who to kill, who to sneak past, and who to trick."

"Essentially we're building a type of simulator," LoPiccolo said, "where object interactions are correct and physics are tied in correctly". Looking Glass called its system for giving objects real-world properties Act-React. "Things that should burn will burn," the lead designer at the time, Jeff Yaus, explained, "and then it's up to the player to decide to burn things, whether or not we've anticipated it." Looking Glass had made *Ultima Underworld* and *[System Shock](/vault/games/system-shock)*, and in 1998 *Maximum PC* grouped *Thief* with Ion Storm's *[Deus Ex](/vault/games/deus-ex)* as two games that would carry *System Shock*'s ideas forward. The magazine pointed out that the people behind them, Doug Church and [Warren Spector](/vault/people/warren-spector), had been "two of the central talents involved in *System Shock*".

## Light and sound

The game turns darkness and noise into things the player can read. A gem at the bottom of the screen shows how visible Garrett is. *Edge* listed what affected it: the light in the area, how close he stands to a wall, how fast he is moving, and even whether he has a weapon drawn. Surfaces matter to the ear as well as the eye. Walking on metal gratings or opening a door can give Garrett away, and a moss arrow can lay a silent carpet over a noisy floor.

The guards are built to listen. They shout into the dark if they hear something, investigate, and call for help if they find an intruder. Bodies left in the open raise the alarm, so the player learns to carry unconscious guards into dark corners. *PC Zone*'s Paul Presley found the tension of crouching in a dark corner as a guard walks past, or dropping a plate on a stone floor and hearing "someone in the room next door say 'Did you hear something?'", "almost unbearable". The same sounds work for the player: footsteps, whistling and conversations tell Garrett where the guards are.

Garrett's equipment is mostly for avoiding a fight. His blackjack knocks a guard out from behind. His bow fires broadhead arrows that kill, but also water arrows that put out torches, moss arrows that carpet a noisy floor, rope arrows to climb to a ledge, noisemaker arrows to draw a guard away, and gas arrows that knock guards out. Flash bombs, mines and a sword complete the kit. The difficulty levels change the job rather than adding enemies: *Computer Gaming World* found that where a mission on normal asked for a magic sword and 1,000 gold, on expert it asked for 2,000 gold and no killing.

## Reviews

*PC Zone* gave *Thief* 90 per cent in January 1999. Presley's verdict was "very, very enjoyable, if somewhat clichéd": he praised its "excellent movement and audio engines" and said the ordinary burglaries were "some of the most well-designed and absorbingly playable levels ever encountered in a first-person game". He disliked how often the story turned to zombies and other undead, which reduced parts of it to "standard hack 'n' slash", and wished for a multiplayer mode.

*Edge* thought no other developer could evoke "such a sense of place" and credited *Thief* with stealth elements that "outdo those of Metal Gear Solid", Konami's stealth game of the same year. It noted a "slightly dated 3D engine" and a limited number of enemy types. *Computer Gaming World*'s review, by Lance A. Larka, was headlined "*Thief* Slips Into the Shadows for One of the Freshest Experiences in Gaming". He praised the sound and the variable difficulty, but found some areas so dark that he had to turn the brightness up to see anything.

## Sequels

Looking Glass reused the engine for *System Shock 2* (1999), and in 1999 Eidos released *Thief Gold*, the original game with "three treacherous new missions". *Thief II: The Metal Age* followed in 2000. Garrett's city has a new, corrupt sheriff, with a power called the Mechanists behind him, and *PC Zone* noted with relief that the zombies were largely gone; it gave the sequel 90 per cent.

*Thief II* was Looking Glass's last game. The studio closed in 2000, and its designer Randy Smith wrote that there was "a low chance that Thief 3 will ever see the light of day". It did. Smith and Terri Brosius had begun designing it early in 2000, and when the project moved to [Ion Storm](/vault/companies/ion-storm)'s Austin studio, Smith became its project director and Brosius its lead writer. *Thief: Deadly Shadows* came out in 2004 for PC and Xbox. It added a third-person view and a city to explore between missions, and *Edge* gave it seven out of ten. *Computer Gaming World*'s Johnny Liu admired its lighting and third-person camera, but thought it had been "rushed out the castle door". Eidos closed Ion Storm Austin early in 2005.

After Square Enix bought Eidos, its Canadian studio Eidos Montreal made a new *Thief*, released in February 2014 for PC and the PlayStation and Xbox consoles. *Edge*'s preview presented it as a chance to introduce fans of *[Dishonored](/vault/games/dishonored)* to "the genre's originator". Its reviews were mixed. In 2022 the *Thief* name passed, with Eidos Montreal, to the Swedish group Embracer.
