A droid steps into view, a trooper takes a careful aimed shot, and the bullets skitter off its armour. That was how *Amstrad Action* opened its review of *Laser Squad* in 1989, and it catches what the game is about. Every soldier has a small budget of action points, and everything costs some: turning round, opening a door, picking up a clip, taking a snap shot or an aimed one. Spend them all on moving and your man cannot fire back when something comes round the corner. Keep some in hand and he fires first.

[Julian Gollop](/vault/people/julian-gollop) wrote it for the ZX Spectrum in 1988 and sold it by mail order through his own new label, Target Games. It was the third version of an idea he had been working on since *Rebelstar Raiders* in 1984, and the magazines that reviewed it gave it between 84% and 97%. Five years later a demo of its sequel became *[UFO: Enemy Unknown](/vault/games/x-com-ufo-defense)*, the game known in America as *X-COM*.

## Fast facts

- **Design and programming:** [Julian Gollop](/vault/people/julian-gollop).
- **Publisher:** Target Games, Harlow, Essex, on the Spectrum in 1988; Blade Software, of St Ives in Cambridgeshire, for the Commodore 64, Amstrad CPC and Amiga versions of 1989. Krisalis Software published a PC version in 1992.
- **Price:** £9.95 on Spectrum cassette; £9.95 cassette and £14.95 disk on the Commodore 64 and Amstrad; £19.95 on the Amiga.
- **Scenarios:** three in the box (*The Assassins*, *Moonbase Assault*, *Rescue from the Mines*), with two more in each expansion kit.
- **Players:** one against the computer, or two taking turns at the same machine.

## A game of points

Before a mission starts you arm the squad from a budget of credits. *ZZAP!64* counted 200 of them, four grades of armour from "light and cheap through to expensive dreadnought thickness", and a shop of automatic and sniper rifles, light and heavy lasers, explosives, grenades and daggers. The tables of damage, accuracy and weight were detailed enough for *CRASH*'s reviewer to call them "complex enough to be bewildering". Heavy kit burdens a trooper and cuts his action points, so the budget and the load both shape the fight.

Then the squad deploys at the edge of a scrolling map, which Target's advertising gave as 80 by 50 squares, and play goes turn and turn about. Each trooper faces one of eight directions and sees only what is in front of him, a 90° field of view by *ZZAP!64*'s account. The enemy moves out of sight. A soldier left with enough points on opportunity fire will shoot at anything that crosses his line of sight during the other side's turn, which is how a long corridor becomes, in *ZZAP!64*'s words, "a kill zone if a sniper is waiting". Fire comes in three kinds, auto, snap and aimed, each dearer and more accurate than the last. A game ends when one side reaches 100 victory points, won by killing enemies and hitting the mission's targets.

The scenery is part of the game. Furniture, gas canisters and walls can all be shot. *CRASH*'s reviewer blew up a canister blocking a corridor in *Moonbase Assault* and took his own trooper with it, because he was "standing too close". The same reviewer found the armour tables less generous than they looked: "even level four armour offers scant resistance to the enemy lasers, with troopers almost always perishing after a second hit".

## The scenarios

The first mission sends the squad into the house of Sterner Regnix, head of the Marsec Corporation, to kill him. *Moonbase Assault* is a raid on the Omni Corporation's lunar databank, and *Rescue from the Mines* means breaking three prisoners out of their cells in a mine complex and getting them to the lift shaft.

Target promised more from the start. Its advertisement in *Sinclair User* said each expansion kit would hold two "fully documented scenarios" for £3.95 by mail order, and *CRASH*'s reviewer was sceptical: in his experience expansion kits were "almost invariably" wishful thinking by the software house. Target delivered. Expansion Kit One added *The Cyber Hordes*, the defence of a rebel station against war droids, and *Paradise Valley*, a retreat from it under alien attack. *C&VG* expected it to be on sale by December 1988. Blade put both on its disk versions. Expansion Kit Two followed in 1990 at £4 on cassette, with *The Stardrive* and a two-sided battle, *Laser Platoon*, which *Amstrad Action* called "a real bloodbath".

*C&VG*'s Tony Dillon was the most taken with it: "Aside from the rather naff packaging, *Laser Squad* is one of the best games I have ever played on any computer. One-player it's great, two-player it's unbeatable." *Your Sinclair*'s reviewer, who admitted he had "never been interested in strategy wargaming before", called it "mega fab" and put it "on a par with Elite for thinking warmongers". *CRASH* thought it took *Rebelstar* "much further without losing any of its playability".

The Commodore review was cooler. *ZZAP!64* liked the tension of hidden movement but thought that "in the long term the game may not be as strong or complex as your average SSI wargame", naming the American publisher of serious computer wargames. *Amstrad Action* made the CPC version its Mastergame. Its reviewer, Trenton Webb, liked the two-player game most, where each side directs its forces while the other looks away "and a truly intriguing battle of wits ensues". In the same issue Gollop said he had got as much out of the Amstrad as anyone could, adding a scanner and extra detail in its four-colour mode.

## Target Games and Blade Software

*CRASH* announced Target Games in June 1988 as a company Gollop had founded that March. *Amstrad Action*, interviewing him in 1989, named its founders as Julian, Nick and Peter Gollop and quoted their aim of "producing high quality strategy games designed to go beyond the middle mark". Gollop later told *Retro Gamer* that he had started Target with a partner who then left, and that the name had been the partner's choice.

Target sold the Spectrum game itself, by mail order from Harlow. The other versions went through Blade Software, which *ZZAP!64* described in 1989 as a new company, "an offshoot of a deal involving programming team Teque and The Software Business Ltd". Blade's advertising that autumn listed the game for the Amiga and Atari ST at £19.95 alongside the 8-bit versions. *Amiga Format* gave the Amiga version 93%, and *The Games Machine* called it "the best tactical strategy game on the Amiga at the moment". The later PC version came from Krisalis Software of Rotherham, which *Computer Gaming World* reviewed in February 1993 with five missions.

## From Laser Squad to X-COM

*Laser Squad* came out of *Rebelstar*, which Firebird had sold for £1.99 in 1986 and *CRASH* had given 93%. *Rebelstar* in turn rebuilt *Rebelstar Raiders* (1984). The action points and opportunity fire were already there; *Laser Squad* added the shop, the detailed maps and the scenarios that could be loaded as add-ons.

After *Lords of Chaos* (1990) Julian and Nick Gollop, now trading as Mythos Games, went back to squads. "The next thing we made was a demo for *Laser Squad 2* on the Atari ST, which ultimately became *UFO: Enemy Unknown*," Gollop told *Retro Gamer*. MicroProse published it in 1994, and *PC Gamer* noted that it had begun life as the sequel to *Laser Squad*. Its soldiers spend time units instead of action points, on walking, kneeling, turning and shooting, and a soldier who saves enough still fires during the enemy's turn. Gollop returned to the name in 2002 with *Laser Squad Nemesis*, a play-by-email game sold over the internet by his company Codo Technologies.
