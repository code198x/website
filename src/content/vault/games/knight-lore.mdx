On Friday 9 November 1984 the first copies of *Knight Lore* reached the *CRASH* offices in Ludlow. Ultimate had said almost nothing about it beyond a rare press release promising a new three-dimensional technique called Filmation, and that was enough to set readers writing to the magazines in numbers. When the game arrived it did something nobody had seen on a [ZX Spectrum](/vault/systems/sinclair-zx-spectrum): each room of a castle was drawn as a solid-looking scene in forced perspective, and the hero could walk behind a pillar, disappear from view, climb onto a block and jump across a gap, all without the flicker that usually came with moving graphics on the machine.

## A curse and forty days

*Knight Lore* brings back Sabreman, the pith-helmeted explorer of *[Sabre Wulf](/vault/games/sabre-wulf)*, now under a curse. By day he is himself; at night he turns into a werewulf, Ultimate's spelling. To lift the curse he must find the old wizard Melkhior somewhere in the castle and bring the ingredients for a cure to his cauldron. He has forty days and forty nights; a sun and moon at the bottom of the screen show the time passing.

Each room is a puzzle. *Sinclair Programs* listed what waited in them: "skittering ghosts, some taciturn soldiers, others balls of fire, huge spiked blocks, vanishing stairways, obstinate flames." Blocks slide on their own, sink when you land on them, or vanish to reveal spikes. The change from man to beast happens whenever night falls, which, the same review pointed out, "is lethal if it occurs in the middle of some delicate manoeuvre". As usual with Ultimate the instructions, written in rhyme, explained very little. *CRASH*'s players learned that the cauldron would only say what it wanted next in daytime, and that approaching it as a werewulf was fatal.

## One colour per room

The trick reviewers dwelt on was how *Knight Lore* avoided the Spectrum's attribute clash, the smearing of colour that happens when two coloured objects share an 8×8 character square. Every room uses a single colour, and Sabreman takes on that colour as he enters. *CRASH*'s reviewer noted that "each screen has its own colour", so the game is not short of colour, and that the moving characters use masking, "so that the moving characters do not flicker at all when they pass in front of other objects". *Sinclair User* described the result as "a crepuscular world of claustrophobic menace" built from careful shading. The [Filmation engine](/vault/techniques/filmation-engine) entry explains how the drawing works.

## What the magazines said

The reviews arrived in the new year. *Sinclair Programs* gave *Knight Lore* 99% in January 1985, above its 98% for *Underwurlde*, and wrote that it was "a game by which all future maze games, and most other games, will be judged". *CRASH* gave it 94%; one reviewer thought it "probably the best game yet produced for the Spectrum". *Sinclair User* gave it 9 out of 10. At the *Computer & Video Games* [Golden Joystick awards](/vault/culture/golden-joystick-awards) in spring 1985 it was voted Game of the Year, ahead of Activision's *Ghostbusters*.

Versions followed for the Amstrad CPC in 1985 and the BBC Micro, where *Acorn User* called it "the most challenging, inventive and addictive game of its class", and for MSX.

## Finished early, released late

In 1988 the Stamper brothers told *CRASH* that "*Knight Lore* was finished before *Sabre Wulf*". The market, Tim Stamper said, was not ready for it: "if we released *Knight Lore* and *Alien 8*, which was already half-finished, we wouldn't have sold *Sabre Wulf*." So Ultimate released *Sabre Wulf* first, in the summer of 1984, and held *Knight Lore* back. "They could have had *Knight Lore* possibly the year earlier. We just had to sit on it because everyone else was so far behind."

## A new genre

*Alien 8* reused the technique in 1985, and *Nightshade* and *Gunfright* followed. Other companies built isometric games of their own, and by 1988 *CRASH* could say that *Knight Lore* "brought about a new genre, the forced-perspective (or isometric) 3-D arcade adventure", passing on one reader's claim that it was "the second-most-cloned piece of software after the word processor WordStar". Chris Stamper put it more simply when explaining *Gunfright*: "Everyone was copying our *Knight Lore* concept, so we thought we'd do one as well!" Jon Ritman was one of the programmers who took it up. He had just started on Ocean's *[Batman](/vault/games/batman-1986)* (1986) when, as *CRASH* told it, "he'd seen *Knight Lore* and decided that this was the sort of game he wanted to write next"; his next isometric game was *[Head Over Heels](/vault/games/head-over-heels)* (1987).
