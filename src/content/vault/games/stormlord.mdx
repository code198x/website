## Overview
*Stormlord* is [Raffaele Cecco](/vault/people/raffaele-cecco)'s follow-up to
*Cybernoid* for [Hewson](/vault/companies/hewson-consultants): a scrolling
platform game about freeing captured fairies, with the dense, exact pixel work
that made his name.

It is also the best-documented game in this Vault, because *CRASH* ran
**Cecco's Log** — a development diary, published under dated entries while the
game was being written.

## How it was received
The press disagreed about it more than about any other Hewson title here.

| Magazine | Score | Date |
|---|---|---|
| *Your Sinclair* | **93%** | June 1989 |
| *CRASH* | **91%** | May 1989 |
| *C&VG* | 87% | June 1989 |
| *The Games Machine* | 86% | May 1989 |
| *Sinclair User* | **71%** | May 1989 |
| *ACE* | **574/1000** | June 1989 |

Twenty-two points between *Your Sinclair* and *Sinclair User* in the same month,
and *ACE*'s 574 is far below the 871–931 it gave *R-Type* and *Driller*. *Your
Sinclair* returned to it at 92% in August 1990.

*Stormlord II: Deliverance* followed in 1990 — *Your Sinclair* 91%, *CRASH* 85%.

## Cecco's Log
The diary is the reason this entry exists in the detail it does. Written in the
first person with mock-*Star Trek* stardates, it ran across a year of *CRASH*
and records the decisions as they were taken.

**The scrolling, May–June 1988.** The earliest entries are about making it move.
He is "using every nifty little trick I can think of" to get the scrolling fast
enough, and by 18 June is adding a **parallax effect** — a pattern of dots
giving an illusion of depth behind the playfield. A visitor to the studio that
August is recorded as duly impressed by "the super-smooth scrolling plus the
fabulous colourful and lavish graphics".

**Why he was pleased to be on it at all**, 3 October 1988:

> How wonderful it is to once again be tucking into my pride and joy —
> *Stormlord*. To be quite honest, I was getting rather jaded bashing away at
> *Cybernoid II*-type code for so many months. Programming the same game can
> become very routine and boring after a while, regardless of how innovative or
> tremendous you thought it was at the beginning.

**A design decision, 4 October.** He resolves that *Stormlord*'s information
panel should be unlike those in *Exolon* and *Cybernoid*, representing what the
player needs **graphically rather than numerically** — prettier, and less boring
than his old panels. In the same entry he notes a change in the industry with
visible regret:

> These days, regretfully, it is becoming the norm for graphics to be drawn by
> an artist leaving the programmer's time free to concentrate on coding.

**A mechanic, 11 October.** Discussing with Nick Jones how the character should
fire, he settles on holding the key to build energy and releasing it to throw —
more energy meaning a faster, longer throw. His own note on why: it "introduces
a nifty element of strategy that I will utilize when planning out the maps". The
control scheme is chosen before the level design, and shapes it.

**Refactoring, 14 October.** He spends a day re-coding the sprite handler:

> I could adopt the old adage of 'if it works then leave it' but feel that the
> new modifications (that make the routine more flexible) will pay dividends as
> far as programming time goes in the long run.

**And the rest of it.** The 'life force' and 'fairy level' indicators working on
12 October. A visit on the 13th to "good friend and fellow programmer" David
Perry, who wrote *Savage*, to admire a VW Scirocco that Perry — 6ft 8in — has to
drive with the sunroof open. The customary last-minute rush on the 18th, with
the Spectrum choosing that moment to misbehave. And the meeting itself on the
19th with Hewson's software manager Paul Chamberlain, where he and Jones were
"slightly praised for our work (but as usual we had to make our own coffee)".

## Why the diary matters
[Andrew Braybrook](/vault/people/andrew-braybrook) kept development diaries in
*Zzap!64* and they are rightly famous. Cecco doing the same in *CRASH* means
**both** of [Hewson](/vault/companies/hewson-consultants)'s flagship authors
published their working notes while working — which is a remarkable amount of
primary documentation for an industry usually reconstructed from interviews
decades later.

For anyone learning this craft, the diary is more useful than the game: it shows
a professional choosing a control scheme before a map, rewriting a working
routine because flexibility will repay the time, and being bored by his own
successful engine.
