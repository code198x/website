"Consider this," says the gypsy at the start of *Ultima IV*, and puts a moral dilemma to the player: a host who agrees to a treaty but insults your country at dinner. Do you bear the slurs, or demand an apology? Seven questions later the game has decided who you are. *Computer Gaming World*'s reviewer, Scorpia, spotted what was missing: "There is no central evil to defeat here; no Mondain, no Minax, no Exodus." For three games [Richard Garriott](/vault/people/richard-garriott) had sent players to kill a wizard. In 1985 he asked them to become a better person instead. The series that made that turn had begun six years earlier, in a computer shop in Houston, and it went on to put Britannia online.

## Fast facts

- **Creator:** [Richard Garriott](/vault/people/richard-garriott), who signed his games "Lord British".
- **Publishers:** California Pacific (*Ultima*, 1981), Sierra On-Line (*Ultima II*, 1982), then Garriott's own [Origin Systems](/vault/companies/origin-systems) from *Ultima III* (1983).
- **Main series:** nine numbered games, *Ultima* (1981) to *Ultima IX: Ascension* (1999).
- **Spin-offs:** the *Worlds of Ultima* games (1990–91), *Ultima Underworld* (1992) and *Ultima Online* (1997).

## From Akalabeth to Ultima

Garriott told *Computer Gaming World* in 1986 how it started. He learned Dungeons & Dragons in 1977, wrote 28 small role-playing games at school on a Teletype that ran on paper tape, and after leaving school worked in a ComputerLand store, where he met an Apple II ("Wow! Graphics!"). The result was *Akalabeth*, with "perspective-view dungeon graphics". The store owner talked him into selling it himself; he spent $200 on zip-lock bags, and when a copy reached the publisher California Pacific "there were tickets waiting for me at the airport". He told the German magazine *PC Player* in 1999 that California Pacific sold 30,000 copies of *Akalabeth* at five dollars each to him, and that he first called his next game *Ultimatum* before shortening the name to *Ultima* for copyright reasons.

California Pacific published *Ultima* in 1981, then ran into financial trouble. Other publishers wanted the sequel, but Garriott wanted "a cloth map enclosed in a nice box, rather than just the ziplock with a disk in it". Sierra On-Line agreed and published *Ultima II* in 1982. He did not feel Sierra was "author friendly", so in 1983 he, his brother Robert and Chuck Bueche started Origin Systems, and *Ultima III: Exodus* was its first product. Each game took longer to write: *Akalabeth* three months, *Ultima* nine, *Ultima II* and *III* a year each and *Ultima IV* two years. Garriott rewrote every Ultima from scratch.

## The Quest of the Avatar

*Ultima IV* (1985) grew from two things, Garriott said. He had seen a television programme about the Avatar of Eastern religions, "a perfect being", and he had started getting fan mail from players who read things into his games. In the earlier Ultimas a champion of good often had to steal from shops to survive; he wanted a game where that was not the answer. "It is intended that it be you, the player, traveling in the lands of Britannia," he said, and the character-building questions were there so the player would feel "responsible for that character's deeds".

He built the virtues from three principles, Truth, Love and Courage, which he noticed in *The Wizard of Oz* (the Scarecrow, the Tin Man and the Lion), and combined them into eight: Compassion, Valour, Honour, Justice, Humility, Sacrifice, Spirituality and Honesty. Working out which virtue came from which principles was left for the player. The world was 16 times the size of the earlier games', 256 by 256 tiles, and its dungeons held 11-by-11 rooms, each designed by hand. The bridge trolls, Garriott admitted, went in a week before release, "when I finally found a spot to put in the ten lines of code". *Computer Gaming World* gave *Ultima IV* its Adventure Game of the Year award.

## A bigger Britannia

*Ultima V: Warriors of Destiny* followed in 1988, and *Ultima VI: The False Prophet* in 1990 threw out almost everything players knew. Scorpia wrote that after five games "of established style and pattern, this one came as a shock": the alphabet-long list of commands was gone, the world and its towns were drawn at one scale, fights happened on the map instead of a separate combat screen, and "just about any object in the game can be taken, moved, or manipulated, whether it's a pair of pliers or a grandfather clock". It took her several days to get used to. The new interface worked with a mouse or the keyboard. The British magazine *ACE* called *Ultima VI* "yet another quantum leap in playability".

*Ultima VII: The Black Gate* (1992) finished the move to a mouse-driven game with no typing and set the story two hundred years on, in a Britannia infiltrated by a friendly-seeming cult, the Fellowship. *Computer Gaming World*'s Charles Ardai admitted he had been warned off it by people who had not played it. "Well, they understood wrong," he wrote, and compared it to "a really good fantasy novel". The series reached a lower point with *Ultima VIII: Pagan* (1994), which exiled the Avatar to another world and asked him to jump and run. *PC Player* reported that Lord British apologised to fans in an open letter.

The last numbered game, *Ultima IX: Ascension* (1999), was more than five years in development and arrived in a state *Computer Gaming World* could not forgive: "Another ULTIMA, another huge disappointment." Its reviewer praised the most detailed world yet seen in a role-playing game but found it choppy even on the fastest machines, and the magazine withdrew it from contention for its role-playing game of the year because "the boxed release was so buggy".

## Underworld and Online

Two spin-offs pointed ahead. *Ultima Underworld: The Stygian Abyss* (1992), developed for Origin by Blue Sky Productions, put the player in a first-person, freely moving 3D dungeon. *ACE* called it "the closest thing I've seen to Virtual Reality … on a home computer".

*Ultima Online* went into public beta in the United States in 1997 and drew 50,000 would-be testers. *Edge* described a Britannia where non-player characters had their own "living and feeding patterns": kill the sheep grazing near a dragon's lair and the dragon comes into town for food. Early in the beta a player killed Lord British himself. After launch, players who were unhappy with lag, bugs and player-killing staged a drunken protest at Lord British's castle, and Garriott wrote an open letter asking for patience; Origin said it had made more than 3,000 fixes. By early 1999 Origin put its paying players at more than 100,000. In 2004 *Computer Gaming World* called *Ultima Online* "the first successful MMORPG ever released" (see [the history of the MMORPG](/vault/genres/mmorpg-history)).

## On other machines

The early games spread from the Apple II to the Commodore 64, IBM PC, Amiga and others. On consoles, in January 1989 *Electronic Gaming Monthly* noted that *Ultima* had become the third NES cartridge with a battery to keep a game in progress, after *[The Legend of Zelda](/vault/games/legend-of-zelda)* and its sequel. In Britain the series was sold through [MicroProse](/vault/companies/microprose) in the late 1980s and then [Mindscape](/vault/companies/mindscape); the [Origin Systems](/vault/companies/origin-systems) entry has the detail.

## Influence

*Ultima* and *[Wizardry](/vault/games/wizardry)* were the American role-playing games that Japanese designers learned from: a *Final Fantasy* artist told *Retro Gamer* that *Dragon Quest* had combined the two with manga culture (see [JRPG](/vault/genres/jrpg) and [Western RPG](/vault/genres/western-rpg)). John Carmack remembered using an Apple II sector editor to give himself gold in *Ultima III*. When *Computer Gaming World* opened its Hall of Fame in 1988, *Ultima III* and *IV* were among the first games in it; by 2004 *Ultima VI* and *Ultima Underworld* had joined them.

## Later history

The single-player series ended with *Ascension*. Garriott left Origin in 2000 and set up Destination Games, which *Computer Gaming World* reported in 2001 had joined the Korean online publisher NCsoft; the magazine's headline began "ULTIMA is dead". EA closed Origin in 2004 (see [Electronic Arts](/vault/companies/electronic-arts)). *Ultima Online* outlived the studio that made it. After Origin it was run by EA, then Mythic Entertainment, and from 2014 by Broadsword, and it is still running. Garriott's own later role-playing game, *Shroud of the Avatar*, from his company Portalarium, was released in 2018.
