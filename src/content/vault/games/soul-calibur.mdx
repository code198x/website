*Soul Calibur* is a fighting game in which everyone carries a weapon: a katana, a sword and shield, a staff, a whip-like blade that unrolls across the arena. Namco released it in Japanese arcades in 1998, and a year later rebuilt it for Sega's new Dreamcast in about seven months. The home version looked better than the arcade machine it came from. "The next generation has finally arrived," *Edge* wrote in October 1999. In Japan, *Weekly Famitsu* gave it 40 out of 40, a perfect score that only one game had received before.

## Fast facts

- **Developer and publisher:** [Namco](/vault/companies/namco).
- **Producer:** Hiroaki Yotoriyama. **Senior project director (Dreamcast):** Teruaki Konishi. **Lead programmer (Dreamcast):** Shinobu Nimura.
- **Arcade:** Namco System 12 board; in Japanese arcades from 30 July 1998.
- **Dreamcast:** Japan, 5 August 1999 (¥5,800); North America, 9 September 1999, at the console's launch; UK, 26 November 1999 (£39.99).
- **Predecessor:** *Soul Edge* (1995), released in Britain as *Soul Blade*.
- **Reviews:** *Weekly Famitsu* 40/40; *Edge* nine out of ten; *Dreamcast Magazine* (UK) 94%.

## From Soul Edge to eight-way run

*Soul Edge* was Namco's first weapon-fighting game, announced in 1995 for the System 11 arcade board and converted to the PlayStation in 1996. It introduced characters who would stay with the series, such as Siegfried and Voldo, but its fighters could only sidestep to avoid an attack, and purists, *Edge* recalled, called it "a poor man's *Tekken*".

The sequel's answer was eight-way movement: a fighter could run in any direction around the arena, not just towards and away from the opponent. Yotoriyama told *Retro Gamer* that it came about almost by accident. Another Namco team had a prototype "which involved running around a field", and his team borrowed its movement system to see what would happen. It suited the vertical and horizontal sword swings already in *Soul Edge*, and the game was rebuilt around it. Namco's two fighting-game teams worked side by side and shared technology; *DC-UK* was told that the *Tekken* developers were good at *Soul Calibur*, "but they can't beat us".

The arcade game took 60 to 70 people about 14 months. It ran on Namco's System 12 board, and by July 1998 it was number two in *Computer and Video Games*'s arcade chart.

## Seven months on the Dreamcast

The Dreamcast version was not a straight port. Konishi and Nimura told *DC-UK* that it took "about seven months from studying the hardware to the final version", and because the Dreamcast let characters move more precisely, "we had to rework all of the animation". Some motion capture was recorded again. Yotoriyama remembered how different the two machines were, down to "colouring methods, the accuracy of internal calculations, translucency display methods". A team of 40 to 50 did the work, and the deadline was set partly so the game could ship with the American Dreamcast.

The result added a great deal to the arcade game: Team Battle and Survival modes, a practice mode, a new character in the pirate Cervantes, extra costumes, and a Mission Battle mode in which players met set challenges, such as fighting on a narrow ledge or against invisible weapons, to earn points to spend in an art gallery. *Edge* found the mission mode "hard to stop". The final boss, Inferno, changed fighting style after each bout, taking up the weapons of the other characters.

## Reception

*Edge* reviewed the Japanese import in October 1999 and gave it nine out of ten, calling it "a work of art – beautiful, fascinating, enthralling and downright thrilling", and concluding that "just as it did when 32bit arrived, Namco has once more defined a new videogame era". Passers-by in the magazine's office, it wrote, had been "repeatedly brought to a slack-jawed standstill" by the animation. In Japan the game went straight to number one in the all-formats chart, and its 40 out of 40 in *Weekly Famitsu* made it the second game to receive a perfect score from the magazine. *DC-UK* called it "the greatest, most beautiful fighting game in the world" before the British release, and *Dreamcast Magazine* gave the PAL version 94%.

It was one of the games that sold the console in America. Sega's press release for the US launch on 9 September 1999 listed it among the launch titles, and Namco's team told *DC-UK* that the American version followed straight after the Japanese one. *Retro Gamer* later put sales at more than a million copies worldwide.

## Later history

Some of what the team wanted to do had to wait: Yotoriyama said ideas such as weapon upgrades went into *Soul Calibur II* to *IV*. The series has continued since, through *Soulcalibur VI* in 2018. The Dreamcast game itself was re-released for the Xbox 360 on Xbox Live Arcade in July 2008, with high-definition graphics but without online play, and on iOS in 2012.
