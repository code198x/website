After three years of Olympic events, [Epyx](/vault/companies/epyx) went to the beach. *California Games* kept the familiar menu, the choice of up to eight players and the world records of its predecessors, but its six events were surfing, half-pipe skateboarding, BMX, roller skating, the foot bag and the frisbee, and each player competed for a sponsor instead of a country. On the Commodore 64 it won *ZZAP!64*'s Gold Medal. On the Spectrum, *CRASH* called it "the poorest Epyx release yet". Two years later it was the game in the box with Atari's new handheld, the [Lynx](/vault/systems/atari-lynx).

## Fast facts

- **Developer and publisher:** [Epyx](/vault/companies/epyx). Project manager Matt Householder.
- **Released:** 1987, for the [Commodore 64](/vault/systems/commodore-64).
- **UK publisher:** [U.S. Gold](/vault/companies/us-gold).
- **Events:** half-pipe skateboarding, foot bag, surfing, roller skating, BMX and flying disk.
- **Sponsors:** nine, including Casio and Kawasaki.
- **Place in the series:** fifth, after *[Summer Games](/vault/games/summer-games)*, *Summer Games II*, *[Winter Games](/vault/games/winter-games)* and *World Games*.

## From "Rad Sports" to California

Householder said the idea came from him and his wife, Candi. Living near San Francisco, they were used to seeing cyclists and skateboarders doing stunts in Golden Gate Park and surfers at Santa Cruz, and one weekend she suggested that Epyx make a game about them. On 25 March 1986 he wrote a proposal for "Rad Sports": two skateboarding events, two BMX events, surfing, windsurfing, hang gliding and parachuting. It also suggested promoting the companies that made the equipment, an idea Epyx's marketing department took up.

It took almost a year to settle the design. Some of the team worried that nobody would know what "Rad" meant, and Bob Botch, Epyx's vice-president of marketing, who had already named *World Games*, suggested *California Games*. Householder explained the choice: when people want to have fun, "California and the west coast come to mind". The name helped fix the events. Programming began in January 1987 and finished in early July.

Two of the events had to be renamed. Paul Vernon, one of Epyx's artists and a Hacky Sack enthusiast, suggested that game, but the name was a trademark, so it became the "foot bag"; the Frisbee became the "flying disk". Chuck Somerville had been riding half-pipes since the early 1970s. Both acted as models for the artists, and Epyx held a "California Games clinic", disguised as a company cookout, where staff in California costumes skated, threw frisbees and kicked a foot bag while a BMX rider and a skateboarder performed for the cameras. When someone discovered that the skateboarder could fly off the top of the screen, the team liked the bug so much that they left it in.

## The events

| Event | What you do |
|---|---|
| Half-pipe | Pump up and down the ramp and time aerial turns, hand plants and kick turns, within 1 minute 15 seconds or four wipe-outs |
| Foot bag | Keep the bag in the air for 75 seconds with head, knees and feet, with extra points for stunts |
| Surfing | Ride the wave for a minute and a half, staying near the curl, while beach bums hold up score cards |
| Roller skating | Skate the length of a seafront boardwalk, avoiding cracks, grass and balls |
| BMX | Race a desert course with jumps and bumps, scoring for stunts; three easy falls or one serious one end the run |
| Flying disk | Throw a frisbee to a catcher in a lakeside park, scoring for the kind of catch |

## Reception

*ZZAP!64* gave the Commodore 64 version a Gold Medal in September 1987. "If you gather up all the superlatives from previous Epyx reviews and add them together," one reviewer wrote, "you just might go half-way towards describing *California Games*." He picked the surfing as his favourite: "fun to play, relaxing to watch and flawless in its implementation".

The Spectrum version, published by U.S. Gold at £8.99, got a very different welcome. *CRASH* gave it 36% in February 1988 and called it "the poorest Epyx release yet". One reviewer wondered whether "the Spectrum simply isn't up to a game of this complexity, or because the programmers have skimped on this version"; another complained that each event had to be loaded separately "and when you get there it just doesn't seem to have been worth it". The disappointment lingered: reviewing *Out Run* in the same issue, a *CRASH* reviewer asked, "Why are US Gold doing this to me? First *California Games* turns out to be a massive disappointment..."

## Other versions

By November 1988 British dealers were selling it for the [Atari 2600](/vault/systems/atari-2600). *ACE* reviewed the [Sega Master System](/vault/systems/sega-master-system) cartridge in June 1989, at £24.95, and thought it "smarter than all the other computer versions with the possible exception of the Amiga", though it had lost the original's multi-player option. [Milton Bradley](/vault/companies/milton-bradley) announced a version for the [Nintendo Entertainment System](/vault/systems/nintendo-entertainment-system) the same year.

The most important was the [Atari Lynx](/vault/systems/atari-lynx) version. Epyx designed the handheld that Atari launched in 1989, and *ACE* reported from its first showing that the machine would come with *California Games*. That Christmas British mail-order dealers were selling the Lynx at £199.90 with the game. Not everyone was won over: reviewing *Tetris* for the Game Boy that November, *ACE* granted that the Lynx had "software like *California Games*", but said it would trade all of it for *Tetris*.

A sequel, *California Games II*, moved to new events: hang-gliding, snowboarding, jet surfing and skateboarding down a drain. When the *Australian Commodore and Amiga Review* reviewed the Amiga version in November 1992, it found the formula "a little tired".
