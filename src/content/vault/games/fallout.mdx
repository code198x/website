When *Fallout* came out in the autumn of 1997, the computer role-playing game was widely thought to be finished. *Computer Gaming World*'s reviewer, Jeff Green, called it "the payoff for long-suffering RPG fans who have seen the genre diluted in recent times by an endless stream of half-baked, buggy, uninspired duds", and said the people it would appeal to were "all role-playing fans, especially those who thought the genre was dead". Interplay's game replaced swords and elves with a wrecked, radioactive California dressed in the look of 1950s atomic-age America, and it let one character solve its problems by talking, stealing, sneaking or shooting. Along with *Diablo* and *Baldur's Gate*, it is one of the games credited with bringing the genre back (see [Western RPG](/vault/genres/western-rpg)).

## Fast facts

- **Developer and publisher:** [Interplay](/vault/companies/interplay), Irvine, California.
- **Released:** October 1997, for DOS and Windows 95; a Macintosh version was advertised alongside.
- **Designers:** [Tim Cain](/vault/people/tim-cain), Chris Taylor, Chris Jones and Leonard Boyarsky, as credited in *Computer Gaming World*'s review. Cain was the lead programmer and producer.
- **Setting:** southern California in 2161, 84 years after a nuclear war in 2077.
- **Price:** $54.95.

## Out of the vault

The game opens with a film. In 2077, with the world's oil and uranium running out, a war wipes out most of civilisation in two hours. The story picks up about 80 years later, in the year 2161, with the player as a lifelong resident of Vault 13, an underground shelter housing hundreds of people. The chip that controls the vault's water-purification system has failed, and the overseer sends you out to find another. You have 150 days before the water runs out.

That quest is timed, but the rest of the game is not. Outside the vault the player meets raiders, gangsters, a religious cult and mutants, and slowly uncovers a larger plot that Green described as "a clever, creepy amalgamation of Mad Max, Dawn of the Dead, and The X-Files". *GamePro* praised its flexibility: you could find people to help build a new water chip, steal one from another vault, or ignore the quest altogether: "Just be ready for the consequences." Green played it twice, once as a hero and once as a killer, and "it felt as if I had played two different games".

The look was part of the appeal. The world is shown from above at an angle, and *Computer Gaming World* admired a design that "evokes the kitsch 1950s' Cold War Americana", down to a spiral-bound manual styled as a "Vault Dweller's Survival Guide", complete with recipes. *GamePro* singled out the "eerily realistic facial animations" of the characters the player talks to.

## From GURPS to SPECIAL

*Fallout* began as a game built on GURPS, the Generic Universal Role Playing System published by Steve Jackson Games, and early advertisements called it *Fallout: A GURPS Post-Nuclear Adventure*. Well into development, as *Computer Gaming World* reported, "creative differences" between the two companies led Interplay to drop GURPS and write its own rules.

The replacement was SPECIAL, named after the seven statistics every character has: Strength, Perception, Endurance, Charisma, Intelligence, Agility and Luck. A player spends points on those, picks three "tag skills" to specialise in, such as first aid or lockpicking, and can take up to two optional traits, each with a good and a bad side. *Computer Gaming World* called the result "actually pretty good". Fights are turn-based: each turn a character has a number of action points, set by the statistics, to spend on moving, shooting or reloading, and any left over add to armour class until the next turn. Choices also change how the world treats the player, through a reputation that marked Green's character as a "champion" with good karma.

The review had complaints. The inventory was a single long list, the quest log missed quests, the characters who joined the player's party could not be controlled directly and got in the way, and fights with rocket launchers at close range dragged on for too many turns. None of that changed the verdict.

## Making it

According to *Edge*, the game took from 1994 to 1997, survived two near-cancellations, and its designers argued with marketing and executives over everything from the setting to the name. Brian Fargo, Interplay's founder, finally took a build home for a weekend and told Cain: "You should call this Fallout." Advertisements before release called it the "unofficial sequel" to *[Wasteland](/vault/games/wasteland)*, the post-nuclear role-playing game Fargo had made in 1988.

Cain left Interplay in early 1998 while work was starting on the sequel. Interplay insisted to *PC Zone* that the split was amicable.

## Later history

*Fallout 2* followed a year later, in 1998, under Interplay's role-playing label, Black Isle Studios. The squad-based *Fallout Tactics: Brotherhood of Steel* came out in 2001. Black Isle's own third game, code-named Van Buren, was abandoned when the studio was dissolved amid Interplay's financial troubles, and in 2004 Bethesda Softworks, the makers of *[Morrowind](/vault/games/morrowind)*, won the right to make *Fallout 3*. *Hyper* reported that the former Black Isle team was "absolutely crushed"; fans worried the series would become "Morrowind with guns", and Bethesda's Todd Howard promised that *Fallout 3* would keep the gallows humour of the first two games.

Bethesda bought the series outright in 2007 and released *Fallout 3* in 2008. Obsidian Entertainment, which employed former Black Isle staff, made *[Fallout: New Vegas](/vault/games/fallout-new-vegas)* for Bethesda in 2010. *Fallout 4* followed in 2015 and the online *Fallout 76* in 2018, a launch *Wireframe* covered for its bugs. In April 2024 Amazon began a television series set in the same world.
