Early in *Hitman: Codename 47* a police chief sits down to dinner in a Hong Kong restaurant, and the way to kill him comes from *The Godfather*. The player hides a pistol in the toilet, excuses himself from the table and comes out of the lavatory shooting. *Computer Gaming World* called it "a well-designed moment of recognition" that other designers should learn from. [IO Interactive](/vault/companies/io-interactive)'s first game, released for the PC in late 2000, made the kill a puzzle: each mission is a place full of people on their own routines, and the player works out how to get close to a target through disguise, timing and patience rather than firepower. Twenty years and eight main games later, the series still works that way.

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** [IO Interactive](/vault/companies/io-interactive), Copenhagen.
- **First game:** *Hitman: Codename 47* (2000), for Windows PCs, published by [Eidos](/vault/companies/eidos) Interactive.
- **Engine:** IO's own, Glacier.
- **Protagonist:** Agent 47, a bald assassin with a bar code tattooed on the back of his head.
- **Music:** [Jesper Kyd](/vault/people/jesper-kyd) scored the first four games.
- **Main series:** *Codename 47* (2000), *Silent Assassin* (2002), *Contracts* (2004), *Blood Money* (2006), *Absolution* (2012), then *Hitman* (2016), *Hitman 2* (2018) and *Hitman 3* (2021).

## Codename 47

The game opens with 47 waking up strapped to a table and being told he is an assassin. After a short training level he is sent after mob bosses and triad leaders in Hong Kong, Colombia, Budapest and Rotterdam. *Edge* later reported that it was a PC game only because PlayStation technology "wasn't able to support IO's ideas at the time". The character arrived almost fully formed: IO's managing director, Janos Flösser, showed *Edge* early sketches of 47 with the bald head, bar code and red tie.

*PC Zone*'s Paul Presley gave it an award for excellence. "Disguises play an important role," he wrote, "and there is nothing quite as classy as initiating a gunfight, switching clothes, then strolling casually away while bodyguards and henchmen run around like headless chickens." He also found it "perhaps the first game in history to actually make me feel a bit uneasy about my actions", describing a guard garrotted from behind, "his limbs flailing in a macabre dance of death", before 47 drags the body out of sight.

Others were harder on it. Every mission had to be finished without saving. *Computer Gaming World* called the missing in-mission save "an oversight that nobody designing an action game should make", and mocked guards who ignored a six-foot bald man with a bar code on his head as long as he wore their uniform. *Edge* found "an original premise... let down by poor level design and clumsy controls". *PC Gamer* gave it 54 per cent. When a reader wrote in to defend the game in December 2001, the reviewer replied that *Hitman* "doesn't require stealth - it requires powers of premonition worthy of a professional psychic". Flösser admitted in 2004 that "we took a beating on the control issue".

## Bodies that fall

The deaths that unsettled Presley came from a physics system written for the game. Thomas Jakobsen, who wrote it at IO, explained it in a paper, "Advanced Character Physics", which he presented at the Game Developers Conference in 2001. The physics in *Codename 47* moved cloth and plants and made "dead human bodies fall in unique ways depending on where they were hit, fully interacting with the environment". Each corpse was a stick figure: points joined by fixed-length constraints, with limits on how joints could bend and extra constraints to stop the knees passing through each other. To knock a body back, the code moved the struck point, such as the shoulder, and let the simulation work out the rest. Jakobsen wrote that the methods were "integrated in IO's in-house game engine Glacier".

This is the technique later called [ragdoll physics](/vault/techniques/ragdoll-physics). *Hitman* shipped it two years before *Unreal Tournament 2003*, the game *Edge* named in 2005 as the first attempt at ragdoll characters. Players noticed at the time. A *PC Gamer* reader wrote in 2001 that "the skeletal animation and physics are the best yet seen in any game of its type" and credited "the superb Glacier engine".

## Silent Assassin to Blood Money

*Hitman 2: Silent Assassin* (2002) moved to consoles as well as the PC, fixed the controls, and in Flösser's words sold "over three million copies" by 2004. For its score IO hired the Budapest Symphony Orchestra. Its end-of-mission ranking gave the series its best-known challenge. Steven Poole wrote in *Edge* that the game treats killing everyone as a valid way to play, but "holds out a carrot for those who wish to refine their skills and withhold their violence sufficiently to receive the Silent Assassin rating", and the player can wander between "the twin poles of kill-everything-that-moves and take-down-only-the-target". The game also caused offence: a level set in a Sikh temple drew complaints from the Sikh Coalition, and in November 2002 Eidos and IO agreed to remove the material from the game's website and to change the GameCube version and, where possible, existing copies.

*Hitman: Contracts* (2004) framed its missions as the flashbacks of a badly wounded 47, and *Hyper* complained that most of them were "lifted from the first Hitman game". It won Kyd a BAFTA for its music. *Hitman: Blood Money* (2006) added the ideas later games kept. *Edge* noted that "'accident' kills are now a full-fleshed feature, allowing the environment to be trap-rigged", and that 47 could now be caught on CCTV. Cash from missions could be spent to lower 47's "notoriety", a reputation system *Edge* found "seemingly confusing and futile", and each mission ended with a breakdown of noise, violence and notoriety. "For those who play the Hitman games with an eye for macabre slapstick and a maniac's mischief," *Edge* concluded, "Blood Money is the series' highlight."

## Absolution and the World of Assassination

Six years passed before the next game. *Hitman: Absolution* (2012), built on IO's new Glacier 2 engine, gave 47 a cover system and an "Instinct" mode, and its director Tore Blystad told *Edge* that the team wanted "the player to feel like the ultimate assassin and not just have the fantasy on the box cover". Kyd did not score it.

IO then went back to large open levels. *Hitman* (2016) came out in episodes, one location at a time; *Wireframe* judged that "an episodic approach to Hitman worked in its favour". By *Hitman 2* (2018) IO had bought itself out of Square Enix, and Warner Bros. published the game. IO published *Hitman 3* (2021) itself. *Wireframe* called the trilogy "the most consistently creative stealth-puzzle hybrid series of the decade". In January 2023 IO renamed *Hitman 3* as *Hitman: World of Assassination*, a single game that gives access to the locations of all three.
