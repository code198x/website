At the end of 2003 Patrice Désilets had just finished *Prince of Persia: The Sands of Time* as its creative director, and Ubisoft wanted him to make the next one. He could not get excited about it. "The problem is that a prince isn't an action figure," he told *Edge*. "A prince is someone who's waiting to become king." He remembered a book about secret societies, and his favourite chapter, on Hassan-i-Sabbah, an 11th-century missionary who founded the order later known as the Assassins. What if the player were an assassin, not a prince?

For a year the project was still called *Prince of Persia: Assassin*, with an assassin escorting a computer-controlled prince through Jerusalem. "I was asked to do a Prince of Persia game and I didn't," Désilets said. The prince was gradually squeezed out, and in November 2007 Ubisoft Montreal released *Assassin's Creed*.

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** Ubisoft Montreal. **Publisher:** [Ubisoft](/vault/companies/ubisoft).
- **Creative director:** Patrice Désilets. **Producer:** Jade Raymond.
- **Released:** November 2007 for the Xbox 360 and PlayStation 3; a PC version followed in 2008.
- **Setting:** the Third Crusade in 1191, in Acre, Damascus and Jerusalem, with a frame story in the present day.
- **Music:** [Jesper Kyd](/vault/people/jesper-kyd).
- **Sales:** more than ten million copies, *Edge* reported in 2012.

## An assassin, not a murderer

"I didn't want to make a game where you were a murderer," Désilets told *Edge* in 2006. "I wanted to make you an assassin." The hero, Altaïr, belongs to the Order of Assassins, which in the game sides with neither the Crusaders nor the Saracens; his nine targets are, in *Edge*'s words, "simply those who profit from and perpetuate the conflict". He must find each one by scouting the city from its highest towers, then pickpocketing, eavesdropping and interrogating, and finally strike and escape across the rooftops.

Désilets had wanted to be an actor before he studied film, and he wanted players to perform the part. "You control him like a puppet," he explained when the game was first shown, with each face button controlling a different part of Altaïr's body. To him the running and climbing had a precise model: "I wanted to replace the car of GTA with a third-person action character. The pleasure of driving a car in Liberty City should be the same as the main character in Assassin's Creed."

The cities were built around that idea. Anything that stuck out more than two inches could be climbed, *Edge* reported in 2006, and the lead level designer, David Chateauneuf, made sure that "almost everything was climbable". The game's huge cathedral in Acre is "100 per cent climbable". A leap of faith from a high perch ends in a bale of hay, a moment Désilets fought for: "The fantasy of hiding in a hay bale, like in the movies, was so great that it overcame people's disbelief."

## Crowds and the Animus

The new consoles made crowds possible. On the PlayStation 2, Désilets said, *Sands of Time* could not draw more than six or seven characters at once, with its 32MB of memory. *Assassin's Creed* filled its streets with a crowd of pot-bearers, scholars, beggars and drunks that could hide Altaïr, by blending in among a group of scholars, or give him away. The team, which started with about 20 people, most of them *Sands of Time* veterans, had grown to around 120 by the end of three years' development, and it built a new engine on the way.

The game's biggest secret was its frame story. Altaïr is a memory. In the present day a bartender called Desmond Miles has been kidnapped and is reliving his ancestor's life through the Animus, a machine that reads "genetic memories". Désilets took the idea from a documentary about DNA. "I am a justification freak," he said; the Animus explained the menus, the on-screen display and why a dead player could start again.

## Reception

*Edge* called Altaïr "a triumph of animation in videogames" and the three cities "beautifully realised", but gave the game seven out of ten. The missions repeated themselves: "most missions are simple to complete", and the crowds and architecture were rarely used to make the assassinations harder or cleverer. "It's as if Ubisoft Montreal has failed to find a game worthy of its stunningly realised main character and sumptuous setting," the review concluded, though "for all its failings Assassin's Creed deserves to be played". Looking back in 2012, *Edge* described a backlash on release, with the repetitive mission structure and the science-fiction framing among the complaints. The game still sold more than ten million copies. "For an 80 Metacritic game, that's a lot," Désilets said.

## The series

*Assassin's Creed* became Ubisoft's biggest series. *Assassin's Creed II* (2009) moved to Italy, to Florence and Venice, with a new hero, Ezio Auditore da Firenze. Désilets called the first game "an amazing toy" and the second "the real game with rules and missions", but he preferred the first: "It's pure." Later games travelled across history; *Wireframe* called *Assassin's Creed Odyssey* (2018), set in Ancient Greece, the high point of the series. In July 2026 Ubisoft said the series had sold more than 250 million games since 2007.
