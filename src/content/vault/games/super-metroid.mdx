*Edge* could not quite decide what to make of *Super Metroid*. Reviewing a Japanese import in June 1994, it noted that at 24 megabits the cartridge "may be one of the biggest ever on the SNES", then complained that a determined player would finish it in about twelve hours, which "makes you wonder where all those megabits went to". It gave the game eight out of ten. *Super Play*'s reviewer that month had no doubts at all. "I didn't think they made games like Metroid any more," he wrote, and the magazine's verdict was that "Super Metroid is absolutely marvellous and you should own it." Over the following decade the second opinion won. By 2005 *Retro Gamer* could say that *Super Metroid* was "still hailed as one of the greatest games of all time", and the design it refined had become the model for a whole genre.

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** [Nintendo R&D1](/vault/companies/nintendo-rd1) with [Intelligent Systems](/vault/companies/intelligent-systems).
- **Director:** [Yoshio Sakamoto](/vault/people/yoshio-sakamoto). Producer: Makoto Kanoh. Main designer: Tomomi Yamane.
- **Released:** Japan, 19 March 1994; North America, April 1994; Europe, July 1994.
- **Cartridge:** 24 megabits, with a battery to keep three saved games.
- **Series:** the third *Metroid* game, after *[Metroid](/vault/games/metroid)* (1986) and *[Metroid II: Return of Samus](/vault/games/metroid-ii)* (1991).

## Making it

The idea came from the producer. Makoto Kanoh told Sakamoto that *Metroid* was very popular in North America and encouraged him to make a new one with the kind of graphics the Super Famicom could now produce. Sakamoto told *Retro Gamer* in 2011 that the game design and concept had been settled before *Metroid II* was made for the Game Boy. What the team wanted from the new machine was "greater power of expression", a world that felt "closer to a reality". *Metroid II* also gave them a starting point for the story: it ended with a baby Metroid hatching in front of Samus, and *Super Metroid* picks up from there.

The work was shared between two companies. The Intelligent Systems staff on the project were all programmers, and Sakamoto remembered no rivalry between them and the Nintendo team. The new hardware brought its own lessons. Sakamoto said that the Super Famicom was thought difficult to develop for, and that how the team divided up its video memory changed "wildly" what the game could show. Tomomi Yamane, whom Sakamoto regarded as the main designer, took a particular interest in the hardware and worked with the Intelligent Systems programmers on what images could be displayed. The [Yoshio Sakamoto](/vault/people/yoshio-sakamoto) entry tells the rest of the story, from the overrunning schedule to the nap room.

## Planet Zebes

The game opens on the Ceres space station, where Samus Aran has left the last Metroid for scientists to study. *Retro Gamer*'s John Szczepaniak described the title screen as the first scene of the game: a dark room panned across in close-up, then revealed to be full of dead bodies. Samus answers a distress call, finds the baby Metroid gone and fights Ridley, one of her old enemies, in a battle she cannot win. Ridley escapes with the Metroid, and Samus has 60 seconds to leave before the station explodes, while, as *Edge* put it, the screen "starts swaying in a Mode 7 kinda way". She follows him to the planet Zebes.

Zebes is one large, connected map. Samus starts with a basic beam weapon, and most of the planet is out of reach until she finds the equipment to get there. *Edge* first thought the play "very pedestrian", then found that the paths "channel you towards weapons and other helpful items which then allow you access to other parts of the game". Its reviewer liked the Morphing Ball, which curls Samus up to roll through small gaps; the grappling beam, called the "Electric Rope" in the Japanese version, for swinging "Spider-Man-style" across a lagoon; and the speed boots, which leave a smear of colour behind Samus as she runs. A map on the pause screen shows the rooms already visited, and computer terminals download plans of the areas not yet explored. *Super Play* found that each new ability sent the player's mind back to places they had passed: a room seen early on, now worth exploring again.

The game rarely explains itself. Sakamoto told *Retro Gamer* that the team "really didn't want to explain things to the player using too many words". Szczepaniak pointed to one example. Two of the planet's creatures, the small Etecoons and the bird-like Dachora, show Samus how to wall-jump and how to use the Speed Booster, just by doing it in front of her. He also noticed that Samus is drawn correctly whichever way she faces, with her gun on the same arm, something he said few other 2D games bothered with.

## Reviews

*Super Play*'s June 1994 import review compared the game with the film *Aliens*, praised a "Carmina Burana-esque" soundtrack in the penultimate area, and suggested readers not read the manual so as to get the most from a first play. The reviewer admitted being on a second play-through already, determined this time to find more than 72 per cent of the hidden missiles and rockets. *Nintendo Power* rated it 4.7 out of 5 for graphics and sound, 3.8 for play control, 4.6 for challenge and 4.6 for theme and fun. *Edge* was the cool voice: the graphics "couldn't be described as mindblowing", some levels looked "downright dreary", and the orchestral tunes had "a tendency to drone after a while". But it still thought *Super Metroid* offered what the Super NES had lacked lately, "an engrossing environment in which the player can lose himself".

How many copies it sold depends on the source. *Retro Gamer* gave an estimate of 2 million worldwide in 2010. Figures from the Japanese industry body CESA, as reported secondhand, put shipments at 1.42 million by late 2003.

## The ending

Mother Brain, the villain of the first game, returns as the final enemy, and so does the baby Metroid, now grown huge. It does not survive the fight. Sakamoto later singled out the moment in a talk at the Game Developers Conference in 2010. He had taken control away from the player to show a dramatic scene, which he believed was unusual for an action game at the time. The scene worked. In October 1994 a *Nintendo Power* reader wrote in to ask why the hatchling had to die, when players could save the Etecoons and the Dachora: "I was crying as soon as the Metroid died. Were you guys trying to play a drama? If so, you did a good job."

## Speed and sequence breaking

The game keeps a record of how long each saved game has been played and what share of the items Samus has collected. *Super Play* noticed straight away that the times gave friends something to compete over. Szczepaniak wrote in 2005 that the design had "a degree of non-linearity that begs to be manipulated": players practised "sequence breaking", reaching areas before the game expected, and took on the bosses with as little equipment as possible, with 20 per cent of the items or fewer counted as good. The scorekeeper [Twin Galaxies](/vault/events/twin-galaxies) was by then recording [speed runs](/vault/communities/speedrunning) on consoles. The table *Retro Gamer* published in 2004 listed Brad Jackson's 51 minutes as the fastest completion of *Super Metroid*, on a real console, "regardless of items acquisition percentage".

## Later releases

Nintendo has released the game again many times: on the Wii's Virtual Console from 2007, then the Wii U and New Nintendo 3DS, on the Super NES Classic Mini in 2017, and on its Nintendo Switch Online service from 2019. The *Metroid* series itself went on to *Metroid Fusion* (2002), *Metroid: Zero Mission* (2004) and *Metroid Dread* (2021) in 2D, and to *[Metroid Prime](/vault/games/metroid-prime)* (2002) in 3D.

## The Metroidvania template

When Konami's *[Castlevania: Symphony of the Night](/vault/games/symphony-of-the-night)* arrived in Europe in 1997, *Edge* found its closest relative in *Super Metroid*, "to which this latest saga pays almost religious homage", citing "the gradual opening out of the map" and "the uncompromising puzzles". Fans later joined the two names into "Metroidvania", the name now used for action games set on one connected map opened up by new abilities. The [Metroidvania](/vault/genres/metroidvania) entry follows the genre and the word.
