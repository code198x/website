When *Computer Gaming World* reviewed *Prince of Persia* in December 1989, Charles Ardai spent most of his space on how the hero moved. "Errol", as he nicknamed the nameless prince, "runs, he creeps, he leaps and ducks, he hangs by his fingertips — and he looks like a real person doing it." The reason was simple and laborious. Jordan Mechner had videotaped his younger brother David running, jumping and climbing, traced the movements frame by frame and turned them into sprites on an Apple II. Ardai concluded that "Prince of Persia is the Star Wars of its field."

The game took Mechner four years, from 1985 to 1989, written in 6502 assembly language on the [Apple II](/vault/systems/apple-ii) and published by [Brøderbund](/vault/companies/broderbund). It sold slowly at first, then was converted to almost every computer and console of the next few years. Its prince returned in sequels, a 3D reboot by [Ubisoft](/vault/companies/ubisoft) and a Hollywood film. Mechner kept a journal the whole time and later published it. In 2012 he recovered the game's source code from disks his father had come across while spring cleaning, and put it online.

## Fast facts

- **Designer and programmer:** [Jordan Mechner](/vault/people/jordan-mechner), whose first game was *[Karateka](/vault/games/karateka)* (1984).
- **Publisher:** [Brøderbund](/vault/companies/broderbund), 1989, for the Apple II at $34.95. In Britain the Amiga and Atari ST versions came out at £24.99 in early 1991, the Amiga one through [Domark](/vault/companies/domark).
- **Goal:** escape the Sultan's dungeons and reach the princess within 60 minutes of real time.
- **Animation:** [rotoscoped](/vault/techniques/rotoscoping) from videotape of David Mechner.
- **Source code:** 6502 assembly language, recovered in 2012 and published on GitHub.

## Tracing a prince from videotape

Mechner had used rotoscoping before, on *Karateka*, for which he filmed his karate teacher and his father on Super 8 film. For *Prince of Persia* he switched to a VHS camcorder, which let him see a take straight away and record it again if it was wrong. He described the process to *Wireframe* in 2020. David, dressed in white, ran, jumped and climbed in front of the camera. Mechner played the tape back on a VHS machine with a clean freeze-frame and photographed the television screen with a 35mm camera, one picture for every third frame of video. When the prints came back from the photo lab, he painted each one with a black marker and white paint so that the figure stood out as a silhouette, and taped eight to twelve of them onto a sheet. A digitiser card, which he said came from a company in England, turned each sheet into an Apple II picture, and he cleaned the frames up pixel by pixel.

He wanted the weight to show. Animation drawn without a filmed reference had always looked wrong to him: "You could see that they had no weight and they never quite looked right." His aim was "a character who felt fluid and lifelike so that players would feel suspense, knowing if they moved too far, they could fall into spikes and that it would really hurt."

Reviewers noticed the details. *Amiga Format* pointed out in 1991 that when the prince stops running, "as he comes to an abrupt halt his torso will be thrown back", and that when he climbs onto a ledge "you really push your elbows out to pull yourself up".

## An hour in the palace

The story came from films. Mechner told *Retro Gamer* that the first ten minutes of *Raiders of the Lost Ark* were an inspiration, and told *Wireframe* of his love for the 1940 fantasy *The Thief of Bagdad*. The Sultan is away at war. His Grand Vizier, Jaffar, has seized power and given the Sultan's daughter an hour to marry him or die, and the player, her imprisoned lover, has that hour to get from the dungeons to the top of the palace. The time limit does not reset. As Ardai put it, "An hour is an hour is an hour."

Each level is built from flick screens of platforms and drops, with loose floor tiles that give way, beds of spikes, blades that slice across doorways, and gates opened by pressure plates, some of them only for a few seconds. Guards with swords wait along the way, and the prince has to find a sword of his own before he can fight them. Mechner also took ideas from *[Lode Runner](/vault/games/lode-runner)*, a puzzle-platform game he admired but in which "you didn't feel a sense of jeopardy".

He did not give the player lives. "I felt limiting lives would be too mean to the player, but that infinite lives would lose the tension," he told *Wireframe*. Instead the prince has a strength meter, topped up by potions scattered through the levels. Not every potion helps: *The One* warned that they "should be drunk or avoided". When the prince dies, a keypress brings him back as often as needed, but the hour keeps running.

The strangest moment comes on the fourth level, where a mirror blocks the way. The game's source code shows that a running jump through the mirror creates the prince's shadow, a double that turns up again on later levels. On the final level, the code's comments note, "Shadow steals sword", and the prince and his shadow have to be reunited.

Mechner built the levels with his own editor. He had meant to include it on the disk, as *Lode Runner* had done. That did not happen, but it let him rebuild almost everything at the end: "Most of the final twelve levels that everybody knows were built or rebuilt in the last couple of months before the game shipped."

## Slow sales, then everywhere

The reviews were enthusiastic. Ardai wrote that "Playing Prince of Persia will simply spoil you for any game whose designers couldn't be bothered to put this much effort into their product." Sales were not. Mechner told *Wireframe* that the Apple II version sold only about 7,000 copies in its first eight months or so. As word spread and the game was converted to other machines, he said, sales "increased hugely". *The One* gave the Atari ST version 93% in January 1991, when the PC version was already on sale at £29.99. In March 1991 *Amiga Format* called the Amiga version "maddeningly irritating and very, very addictive" and "an instant classic".

*Retro Gamer*'s list of versions shows how far the game travelled: the Apple II, Macintosh, PC, Amiga, Atari ST, Amstrad CPC, ZX Spectrum and SAM Coupé among computers, and the NES, SNES, Mega Drive, Master System, Game Gear, Mega-CD, Game Boy, Game Boy Color and TurboDuo among consoles. By 1999 *PC Zone* put the game's sales at "over two million copies".

## Its influence

Other developers took notice. In 1991 *The One* reported that Paul Cuisset, artistic director of the French company [Delphine Software](/vault/companies/delphine-software), had been so excited by *Prince of Persia* that he developed a new graphics routine for Delphine's *Cruise for a Corpse*. *Edge* later described Cuisset's *[Flashback](/vault/games/flashback)* as "strongly influenced by Another World and Prince of Persia". [Eric Chahi](/vault/people/eric-chahi) said that Mechner's *Karateka* had strongly influenced the visual style of his *[Another World](/vault/games/another-world)*, which also used rotoscoping. When *[Tomb Raider](/vault/games/tomb-raider)* came out, Cyrille Fontaine of Kalisto called it a "3D Prince of Persia".

## Sequels, a reboot and a film

Mechner was creative director of *Prince of Persia 2: The Shadow and the Flame* (1993), which had a team of programmer, sound designer and artists, and a 75-minute time limit. He consulted only briefly on *Prince of Persia 3D* (1999), published under The Learning Company's Red Orb label. *PC Zone*'s review of it was damning: "what was revolutionary in the Eighties is Nineties' bread and butter".

In the early 2000s Ubisoft approached Mechner about rebooting the series. *Prince of Persia: The Sands of Time* (2003), a 3D game on which he was credited as game designer, writer and creative consultant, revived it. It was followed by *Warrior Within* and *The Two Thrones* (2005). Mechner pitched a film to the producer Jerry Bruckheimer and wrote its first screenplay. *Prince of Persia: The Sands of Time*, directed by Mike Newell with Jake Gyllenhaal as the prince, came out in 2010 and, according to *Wireframe*, took more than $336 million. The series returned to side-on platforming with *Prince of Persia: The Lost Crown*, from Ubisoft Montpellier, released on 18 January 2024.

## The journals and the source code

Mechner kept a journal throughout development. "I've kept a journal since I was in college," he told *Wireframe*, "and I find it very helpful to work through ideas." In 1985 he wondered in it whether there would "even be a computer games market two years from now", and whether he should be writing screenplays instead. He published the journals from 1985 to 1993 as *The Making of Prince of Persia*, first as a self-published e-book and later as an annotated, illustrated edition from Stripe Press. In 2023 he told the story again, with his family's history, in a graphic novel, *Replay*.

The source code was nearly lost. The disks holding it had been missing for more than a decade. Then, in 2012, Mechner's father found a box of odds and ends while spring cleaning and sent it to his son. Among them were three 3.5-inch floppy disks with the Apple II source. Jason Scott of the [Internet Archive](/vault/communities/internet-archive) and the late Apple II collector Tony Diaz read the 22-year-old disks, a job that, Mechner wrote, "took most of a long day and night". In April 2012 Mechner posted the code on GitHub. He also made available a technical document he had put together in October 1989 for the teams converting the game to other machines.

The repository holds the game's 6502 source, its disk routines and its copy protection. It carries no open-source licence. Mechner's statement in it reads: "As the author and copyright holder of this source code, I personally have no problem with anyone studying it, modifying it, attempting to run it, etc. Please understand that this does NOT constitute a grant of rights of any kind in Prince of Persia, which is an ongoing Ubisoft game franchise." For other games whose code has been published or recovered, see [game preservation](/vault/culture/game-preservation).
