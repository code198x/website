"Over the past few years the games market has been inundated with Doom clones," *Edge* wrote in August 1996, "some good, some average, some terrible but none matching the frenzied violence and perfectly constructed locations of the bona fide original. Until Quake." [id Software](/vault/companies/id-software)'s follow-up to [Doom](/vault/games/doom) went out as a free shareware episode that June, and it changed two things at once.

The first was the picture. *Doom* had drawn a flat floor plan with heights added, and could never put one room above another. *Quake* built its castles, sewers and bases from real three-dimensional polygons, so levels could stack catwalks above floors above flooded tunnels, and you could look up and down. Its monsters were polygon models too, of 200 to 500 polygons each, John Carmack told *Computer Gaming World*, where *Doom*'s had been flat pictures. The second was the network. Any owner of *Quake* could start a server, and anyone who knew its internet address could join.

## Fast facts

- **Developer and publisher:** [id Software](/vault/companies/id-software), Mesquite, Texas. GT Interactive distributed the boxed version.
- **Released:** shareware episode on 22 June 1996; the full game followed that summer, at $50.
- **Team:** programming by [John Carmack](/vault/people/john-carmack) and Michael Abrash; levels by [John Romero](/vault/people/john-romero), American McGee, Sandy Petersen and Tim Willits; art by Adrian Carmack.
- **Sound:** Trent Reznor of Nine Inch Nails, who made the sound effects and the ambient soundtrack.
- **Multiplayer:** IPX networks, modem, and the internet over TCP/IP; up to 8 players on a player's own machine or 16 on a dedicated server.
- **Programming language:** QuakeC, for changing the game's rules.
- **Source code:** released under the GNU General Public License on 21 December 1999.

## A visit to Mesquite

*Computer Gaming World*'s Vic Long flew to id's offices in Mesquite, near Dallas, shortly before the game was finished. Players had already had a taste: *Qtest*, a test release of a few DeathMatch levels, had gone out earlier in the year. Long found a team arguing about design. They had removed the automatic aiming that *Doom* used. "We settled on removing auto-aiming because doing so rewards skill," Carmack said. Sandy Petersen's advice was shorter: "You need to aim." There would be no automap either, because the designers thought it spoiled the suspense.

Each designer had a style. McGee built the metal levels, Willits the medieval ones full of traps, Petersen the fantasy levels and Romero the military bases. The dynamic lighting, which lit up corridors as rockets flew down them, Carmack had coded "in an hour as a bet". Boxes of nail-gun ammunition carried the Nine Inch Nails logo. There was barely a story, and id's Jay Wilbur did not mind: "A bad game with a great story is a bad game. A great game with a bad story is still a great game." In November 1996 the magazine put *Quake* first in its list of the worst back stories in PC games, with one line of explanation: "There is no story for Quake."

Carmack was already looking past it. "Quake will be id's last pure planar polygon-based engine," he said.

## Playing over the internet

*Doom* had linked four players on one network. *Quake* was built as a client and server from the start. In September 1996 *Computer Gaming World* explained how to run a listen server on your own machine for up to 8 players, or a dedicated server for 16, and pointed out what was new: "you don't need an online service to run Quake over the Internet. You can enter the IP number of a server where Quake is running and connect directly."

Dial-up modems made that laggy. In December 1996 id released *QuakeWorld*, a free version rewritten for internet play, and players found games through QSpy, a server list that showed each server's ping time. *Computer and Video Games* called *Quake* "the first to have such an impact on world-wide internet gaming", and its April 1997 PC chart put *QuakeWorld* at number one. For the method that made movement feel smooth over a slow line, see [client-side prediction](/vault/techniques/client-side-prediction).

3D graphics cards changed how it looked. In early 1997 id released *GLQuake*, a version written for the OpenGL graphics interface with the 3Dfx Voodoo chipset as its reference card. *Computer Gaming World* ran it at 640×480 and was struck by the translucent water.

## QuakeC and the mods

*Quake* kept its game rules outside the engine, in a language called QuakeC that players could edit and recompile. "With QUAKE C, there's nothing you can't do," American McGee told *Computer Gaming World* in 1996. Players added grappling hooks, vehicles and new game types. Robin Walker and John Cook in Australia remade a *Doom* map called Fortress as *Team Fortress*, released in 1996, which split players into classes; 500,000 people downloaded it, and Walker and Cook later moved from Australia to join Valve. The [modding](/vault/communities/modding) and [total conversions](/vault/communities/total-conversions) entries follow these projects further.

The physics produced tricks of their own. Firing a rocket at your own feet threw you upwards, the "rocket jump", and strafing into a wall while running "can increase your speed by up to 40 per cent", Anthony Bailey of the *Quake done Quick* team told *PC Zone* in 1998. Their recorded runs through the whole game are a landmark in the history of [speedrunning](/vault/communities/speedrunning).

## Reception

*Computer Gaming World* made *Quake* its action game of 1996, "even though it isn't the most innovative or original effort of the past year". Its gameplay was *Doom*'s, the magazine said: kill the monsters and find the key. What set it apart was the true 3D world, a dark atmosphere and "the best multiplayer options". The same magazine's list of the 150 best games of all time, in November 1996, placed it 36th: "The ultimate implementation of the deathmatch, Quake also creates the spookiest atmosphere ever presented in an action game."

## The engine goes out

id licensed the engine to other studios. Raven Software used it, with some *Quake II* technology, for *Hexen II*; Ritual Entertainment, founded as Hipnotic Interactive, made the *Scourge of Armagon* mission pack and then began *Sin* on it, before moving that game to the *Quake II* engine; and Valve's first game, [Half-Life](/vault/games/half-life), "uses the enhanced Quake engine", *Computer Gaming World* reported from E3 in 1997. Valve's reworked version became its GoldSrc engine.

## The series

*Quake* was the last game Romero made at id. He left in 1996 and started Ion Storm with Tom Hall. The sequels were different games under the same name: *Quake II* (1997); *Quake III Arena* (1999), which concentrated on online multiplayer; *Quake 4* (2005); *Enemy Territory: Quake Wars* (2007); the free-to-play *Quake Live* (2010); and *Quake Champions*, fully released in 2022. An enhanced edition of the original, developed by Nightdive Studios, came out in August 2021. Players still meet every August at [QuakeCon](/vault/events/quakecon), which began in a Texas hotel two months after the shareware release.

## Released source code

On 21 December 1999 id released the source code of *Quake* under the GNU General Public License, version 2 or later: the complete code of *WinQuake*, *GLQuake*, *QuakeWorld* and *GLQuakeWorld*. The licence let anyone "do anything you want with the code, including sell your new version", provided they published their own source. id publishes it today in its `id-Software/Quake` repository on GitHub. The release covered the programs, not the game's levels, models and sounds.
