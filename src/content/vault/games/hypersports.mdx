"Here we go again with button bashing to wake the dead," *Personal Computer Games* sighed in October 1984, when Konami's *Hyper Sports* reached Britain's arcades. A year earlier Konami's *[Track & Field](/vault/games/track-and-field)* had players hammering two run buttons to race a sprinter down a track. *Hyper Sports* kept the hammering and added events that punished it: an arrow released a fraction of a degree off, or a triple jump launched at the wrong angle, ended the game as surely as a slow swim. In 1985 it became one of the first big hits for the revived [Imagine](/vault/companies/imagine-software) label, and on home computers the buttons became a joystick, shaken from side to side as hard as the player dared.

## Fast facts

- **Arcade:** Konami, 1984; seven events, up to four players.
- **Home versions:** Imagine, 1985–86, for the ZX Spectrum, Commodore 64, Amstrad CPC and BBC Micro; the Spectrum and Commodore 64 versions have six of the seven events.
- **Spectrum:** 48K, £7.95; programmed by [Jonathan 'Joffa' Smith](/vault/people/jonathan-smith); 92% in *CRASH*.
- **Commodore 64:** £8.95, music by [Martin Galway](/vault/people/martin-galway); 80% in *ZZAP!64*.
- **Charts:** number two in the Gallup Spectrum chart in summer 1985; number one in *CRASH*'s readers' chart in November 1985.

## The arcade game

The coin-op had seven events, and a player had to reach a qualifying mark in each to go on to the next. It opened with the 100 metres freestyle, to be swum in under two minutes: hammering a run button made the swimmer go, and pressing the jump button when prompted let him breathe. Skeet shooting came next, with aiming boxes that tracked the clay pigeons automatically and a button for each side. Then came the long horse, archery, the triple jump, weightlifting and, last and hardest, the pole vault. *Personal Computer Games* found the sports "a little more varied than in Track and Field but the skills and aims are the same", and promised it was "bound to raise a sweat".

Looking back in 1995, *Edge* thought *Hyper Sports* still the best of the genre. Swimming and weightlifting were "simple all-out wagglers", but archery and the triple jump "demanded pixel-perfect timing and carefully controlled button presses and releases". Playing it again eleven years on, *Edge* wrote, showed "not how basic the game was but how naive the description 'joystick basher' is".

## Imagine's comeback

Liverpool's Imagine Software had collapsed in July 1984, and [Ocean](/vault/companies/ocean-software) bought its name. Ocean used the label for arcade conversions from Konami, and *Hyper Sports* followed *World Series Baseball* as one of the first. *CRASH*'s reviewers welcomed it as "the real follow-up to *Daley Thompson's Decathlon*", Ocean's own multi-event game of 1984, and as proof "that Imagine are well on their way back to the top".

The Spectrum version had six of the seven events; like the Commodore 64 version, it left out the pole vault. *CRASH* found it remarkably close to the coin-op, "even down to the bird which flies across the screen when you get a maximum on the skeet shooting", and its swimming instructions were blunt: "Smash the keyboard (or your joystick) to bits to get speed." Weightlifting was "a pound-your-Spectrum-keyboard-through-the-floor screen". But one reviewer pointed out that the game "isn't just a test of brute strength like its predecessor, but involves timing and skill too", and another warned that it was "really frustrating having to go back to the start if the odd arrow is a couple of points of a degree out". It scored 92%. In the September 1985 tips pages, a reader advised waiting until the vaulter's legs were almost vertical, then pressing fire "and waggle the joystick like mad"; for the weightlifting he recommended "a really good joystick (like a Kempston)".

*CRASH*'s review did not name a programmer. Jonathan Smith, who went on to write *Mikie* and *Green Beret* for Imagine, is remembered by his Ocean colleague Paul Hughes as the man who "programmed and provided the graphics" for *Hyper Sports*.

## The other versions

*ZZAP!64* reviewed the Commodore 64 version in September 1985. Its reviewer described the swimmer being driven "by hurling your joystick from side to side" and the vaulter's somersaults by an instruction to "hurl the joystick around the room". It gave the game 80% overall and 94% for "hookability", with the note "Will your joystick stand it?" Martin Galway's music for it included his version of Vangelis's *Chariots of Fire*, which he wrote from memory before Ocean had the rights.

Ocean's history records two more conversions. Simon Cobb wrote the archery, triple jump and swimming for the Amstrad CPC version; he had never seen anyone do a triple jump, and implemented it "as just three jumps", not a hop, a step and a jump. Richard Kay converted it to the BBC Micro, "a fantastic machine", he said, before leaving Ocean in 1985. The Amstrad version did badly: *AMTIX!* gave it 30%, complaining of "poor sprites, feeble animation".

## In the charts

*Hyper Sports* sold. In the Gallup Spectrum chart that *Sinclair User* printed, it was number two for the months ending 19 July and 23 August 1985, and still fifth in mid-September. *CRASH*'s Hotline chart, compiled from readers' votes, shows how long it stayed popular. It entered at 16 in September 1985 and was voted to number one in November. It was second for the next two months, fifth in February 1986, and eighth from March to June. It was still in the readers' top thirty at the end of the year.
