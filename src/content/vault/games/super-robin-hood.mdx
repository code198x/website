In September 1986 Philip and Andrew Oliver drove to London for the Personal Computer World Show with a handwritten design for a game they had not started. They were living with their parents in Wiltshire and taking a year out instead of going to university. They had noticed that *Ghostbusters* sold on its name, but they could not afford a licence, so they chose a hero nobody owned: Robin Hood. At a tiny stand they met two brothers their own age, David and Richard Darling, who were starting a budget label called [Code Masters](/vault/companies/codemasters). The twins asked what Code Masters would pay for it, and David Darling said £10,000. "Hopefully, the shock didn't show too much in our expressions!" Philip said later. The game became *Super Robin Hood*, the twins' first for Code Masters, and the start of a partnership that produced [Dizzy](/vault/games/dizzy).

## Fast facts

- **Authors:** [Philip and Andrew Oliver](/vault/people/oliver-twins), on the Amstrad CPC.
- **Publisher:** [Code Masters](/vault/companies/codemasters), £1.99 on cassette.
- **Released:** late 1986 on the Amstrad CPC; Spectrum and Commodore 64 versions in 1987.
- **Spectrum version:** converted by Mark Baldock, with a title tune by [David Whittaker](/vault/people/david-whittaker).
- **Reviews:** a Rave in *[Amstrad Action](/vault/magazines/amstrad-action)*; 69% in *[AMTIX!](/vault/magazines/amtix)*; 52% in *[CRASH](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine)*.
- **Later:** on Code Masters' *Quattro Adventure* compilation (1990); remade by the twins for the NES; reworked as *Robin Hood: Legend Quest* (1993).

## A month on one Amstrad

The twins' account of writing it is told in Chris Wilkins and Roger Kean's *The Story of the Oliver Twins*. They had promised the game in a month, in time for Christmas, and they had a single Amstrad CPC between them. They worked in shifts of 18 hours a day, seven days a week; while one of them used the computer, the other wrote code on paper. To save time they built a map editor into the game itself. They did not know how big the castle would be, so they fitted in as many rooms as the memory would hold, and each day they loaded the game, placed sprites around the rooms and saved the map data again.

The money changed before the game came out. Instead of £10,000, the Darlings offered a royalty of 13.5p a copy on a first run of 20,000, about £2,700, and the twins signed. The royalties did pass £10,000 in the end. Code Masters had the game converted to the Spectrum and the Commodore 64 and paid the twins 5p for each of those copies too; Philip estimated that *Super Robin Hood* earned them about £20,000 in all. By April 1987 *Computer Gamer* was reporting that it had sold more than 11,000 copies, and that the twins were 19, running a company called Complex Computer Software with the help of the government's Enterprise Allowance Scheme, which paid them £40 a week.

## Playing it

Maid Marian is locked in the East Tower of the Sheriff of Nottingham's castle. Robin runs, jumps, ducks and climbs through a castle of flick-screen rooms joined by ladders, lifts and sliding platforms, shooting the Sheriff's guards with his bow. Spiders, fireballs and crossbow bolts wear down his health, which starts at 99; tablets top it up. Spinning keys start the lifts and platforms that lead to new parts of the castle, and red hearts are scattered through the rooms. Only when Robin has collected all of them can he reach Marian. If his health runs out, "Robin fires an arrow Heavenward and dies", as *CRASH* put it; *Amstrad Action* pointed out that this was "true to the legend", in which he is buried where the arrow lands.

The Amstrad version talks. Marian calls "Help me, Robin!" or "Save me, Robin!" when a heart is collected, and the book says the voice was the twins' mother, Jenny. Their friend Jon Paul wrote the music.

## What the magazines said

*Amstrad Action* reviewed it in March 1987 and gave it a Rave. The reviewer admitted that "super" was one of their least favourite adjectives, then called it a super game, with "great animation, some tough problems and excellent speech synthesis": "tremendous speech and in a budget game too". The review named the programmers, "Philip and Andrew Oliver", and welcomed an option that switched off the background scenery. In the same issue's round-up, *Super Robin Hood* was one of three budget games that "make the grade".

Not everyone agreed. *AMTIX!* found the speech "extremely annoying" and the scenery "just plain garish", but still called it "a pleasant platform puzzle game that is worth two quid of anyone's money", and gave it 69%. *Computer Gamer*'s reviewer started from the other end: the ladders-and-platforms game "has been done to death", he wrote, and "Code Masters has been grave robbing again".

The Spectrum version, converted by Mark Baldock, was reviewed in mid-1987. *CRASH* split. One reviewer saw "yet another platform game which falls into the deepening void of trashable Spectrum games"; another praised a "superbly presented Code Master game" with a title tune by David Whittaker. It scored 52%. In *[Sinclair User](/vault/magazines/sinclair-user)*, Tony Dillon thought it "technically ... all well up to Codemasters standard": "Terrific graphics, good sound FX, smooth animation and well planned screen layout." But, he added, "It's so boorrring!"

The Commodore 64 version followed in the summer of 1987. *[Your Commodore](/vault/magazines/your-commodore)* called it "my favourite Code Masters game", with action that was "fast and furious and so smooth". By January 1988 Code Masters was advertising it in *[ACE](/vault/magazines/ace-magazine)* as a "Top Ten best selling platform game".

## Later lives

In 1990 Code Masters put *Super Robin Hood* on *Quattro Adventure*, a £2.99 compilation with *Vampire*, *Ghost Hunters* and *Dizzy*.

The twins came back to it for the [NES](/vault/systems/nintendo-entertainment-system). They worked on a new version from September 1991 to April 1992, expecting the code they had written for *The Fantastic Adventures of Dizzy* to make it quick (it took longer than they hoped), and Camerica put it on its own *Quattro Adventure* cartridge alongside *Linus Spacehead*, *Boomerang Kid* and *Treasure Island Dizzy*. *Game Informer* previewed that cartridge in March 1992, and *N-Force* reviewed it in August 1993, when it found the Robin Hood game suffered from sprite flicker and slowdown. The twins then had their staff convert the NES game back to the Spectrum, Amstrad, Atari ST and Amiga. They did not want to call it *Super Robin Hood 2*; it was *Amazing Robin Hood* during development and came out early in 1993 as *Robin Hood: Legend Quest*, at £3.99.
