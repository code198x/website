## What the press gave it

*CRASH* reviewed it in **May 1984** and rated it **95% overall** — 99% for value
for money, 98% for addictive qualities, 96% graphics — under the summary "to
date, one of the most addictive and finest Spectrum games". *Personal Computer
Games* gave it 10/10 the same month, *Sinclair User* 9/10 in June, and *Big K* a
full 3/3.

## Overview

*Jet Set Willy* is [Matthew Smith](/vault/people/matthew-smith)'s 1984 sequel to *[Manic Miner](/vault/games/manic-miner)*, released through [Software Projects](/vault/companies/software-projects) at **£5.95** for the 48K Spectrum. Where *Manic Miner* was twenty linear single-screen puzzles, *Jet Set Willy* is open-world for 1984 values of open-world: **60 rooms** in a mansion the player can explore in any order, with **83 objects** to collect across the house and its grounds before Maria, Willy's housekeeper, will let him get to the four-poster bed.

*CRASH* had run a colour preview in April and reviewed it in May, by which point the game had been on sale "only a very few days" and had already entered the magazine's reader chart at 41 — behind *Manic Miner*, which was still at number one and, *CRASH* noted, "as yet unaffected by the release of *Jet Set Willy*".

The premise: Miner Willy from *Manic Miner* has become Jet Set Willy, having spent his mining fortune on a sprawling mansion. After a party, Maria refuses to let him go to bed until every party item — bottles, plates, glasses, and the more surreal — is gathered up. Each item is in a different room. Some rooms are dangerous. Some require precise jumping. Some — and this is where things get interesting — were broken in the shipping version.

## The 60-room mansion

The mansion is laid out as a 2D map of single-screen rooms connected by exits left, right, up (jumping), and down (falling). The geography is roughly coherent — a bathroom near the top, kitchen on the ground floor, an attic, exterior areas including a beach and a banyan tree — but the room layout is also gleefully surrealist. *We Must Perform a Quirkafleeg*. *The Nightmare Room*. *The Hall of the Forefathers*. The names alone gave the game a cult identity that *CRASH*'s readers quoted for years.

Each room is a precision platforming challenge in *Manic Miner* style: avoid enemies, jump conveyor belts, navigate moving platforms, collect the items, exit to a neighbouring room. The one-touch-death rule from *Manic Miner* carries over. The pressure is no longer the air-supply meter (gone), but the sheer scope of the map and the precision required across dozens of distinct challenges.

## The colour code

*Jet Set Willy* did not just load and run. **Software Projects shipped a colour
code card with the inlay**, and the game would not start without a correct code
entered after loading — *CRASH*'s review warned that the card "must be looked
after at all costs".

It is an early and unusually physical piece of copy protection: the thing you
cannot photocopy in 1984 is colour. It also tells you what the industry now
thought it was losing to tape-to-tape duplication, eight months after
*Manic Miner* had shown how large a Spectrum hit could get.

## The Attic bug

*Jet Set Willy* shipped broken, and *CRASH* documented the failure in detail in
June 1984 — precisely enough that the mechanism is still legible.

**Visiting The Attic corrupts the game.** Once you have been there, a chain
reaction sets in: the guardians in the Chapel and several other locations move to
places where they kill you, and entering **The Kitchen, West of Kitchen, East
Wall Base or We Must Perform a Quirkafleeg** then costs you every remaining life
at once. *CRASH*'s advice was to leave The Attic until last and, having been
there, to avoid the obvious route back through the Kitchens and retrace through
The Banyan Tree instead. There was no in-game recovery: "if you complete the game
you will have to reload it to remove the block caused by visiting The Attic."

Software Projects apologised, and *CRASH* was fair about the scale of it — "this
is actually the only mishap with JSW".

**The other way to lose all your lives was not a bug at all.** Falling through the
bottom of certain screens drops Willy into a loop, because each new life restarts
you in exactly the position you entered the room. Enter by falling, and you fall
again, and again, until the lives run out. *CRASH*'s verdict on that one: "if you
think about it logically it seems fair enough. Such carelessness deserves a total
loss of life!"

### What the magazines did

The received story is that the magazines published POKEs to fix the game. In June
1984 *CRASH* explicitly **refused** to: "thanks to all those clever dicks who have
sent in mini-progs for giving infinite lives, etc. We're not publishing those
yet!" The POKE culture around *Jet Set Willy* is real, but it ran ahead of the
magazines rather than out of them, and at least one magazine held the line for a
while on the grounds that infinite lives were cheating rather than repair.

What *CRASH* did publish, in the same issue, was **a full map of the mansion**
across two pages — split into the House, the Drive and Mega Tree, and the Beach
and Yacht, with a numbered key to every room and a list of the non-obvious
connections between them: the secret passage out of The Forgotten Abbey, the
vertical link from The Priest Hole to Half Way Up, and the fact that jumping off
the roof of the Watchtower takes you upwards into the Off Licence.

That is the more interesting behaviour, and the more characteristic one. The
magazine's response to a 60-room game was not to break it open but to survey it.

### A room that changed its name

*CRASH*'s April preview used Smith's pre-production copy, and several things
changed before release. One of them is now the game's best-known room name: **the
"Gaping Pit" became "We Must Perform a Quirkafleeg"**.

## The music

Like *Manic Miner*, *Jet Set Willy* plays continuous music — "If I Were a Rich Man" from *Fiddler on the Roof*. Same beeper-driver approach: port `$FE` bit 4 toggled in time with the game logic, an interleaved music engine running off the main loop. The composition is longer than *Manic Miner*'s and loops less obtrusively. See [beeper music](/vault/techniques/beeper-music) for how the technique works.

## Reception and legacy

Reviews were near-unanimous despite the bugs. *CRASH*'s reviewer reached for
pharmacology — if a game being "deadening, frustrating and fun" were grounds,
"then *Jet Set Willy* should certainly be put on the Government's list of
proscribed drugs" — and for the graphics: "the dreadful Maria with her pointing
hand of accusation, the flickering candles, the grinning heads, the leaping
security guards, just everything has been worked as far as it can go."

The same review made the point that matters for the design: this is not a sequel. "Very sensibly, it is a very different game to *Manic Miner*... much more
of an adventure in which the player can move freely between the linking rooms and
work out the structure of Willy's strange house." Random elements were built in —
hazards changing places or vanishing, rooms that cannot be entered from a
particular direction.

The room-by-room exploration template was copied extensively; *Pyjamarama* (1984)
and *Everyone's a Wally* (1985) are the obvious British descendants.

A sequel, *Jet Set Willy II: The Final Frontier*, appeared in 1985 — an expanded
version, not designed by Smith. A planned third game never materialised, and that
absence is part of the [Matthew Smith mythology](/vault/people/matthew-smith).

## Afterwards

*Jet Set Willy* has been ported, remade and re-imagined many times over, and its
room names have outlived the machine — "we must perform a Quirkafleeg" survives
as British retro-gaming vernacular among people who never owned a Spectrum. The
bugs have aged into period detail. The surrealism has not aged at all.
