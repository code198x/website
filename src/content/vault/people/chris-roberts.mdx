In the spring of 1987 Superior Software, looking for new programmers, printed a testimonial from one of its authors in *Acorn User*. At his previous publisher, he said, getting his royalties "was like getting blood out of a stone". At Superior, "just 6 weeks after the release of *Stryker's Run* I received my first royalty cheque which amounted to more than £5000." The author was Chris Roberts, a teenager from Manchester. By 1988 he was working in Texas, and by the end of 1991 his space combat game *[Wing Commander](/vault/games/wing-commander)* was *Computer Gaming World*'s game of the year. He went on to put Mark Hamill in a computer game, direct a Hollywood film of his own game, produce other people's films, and raise more than a billion dollars from players for a game that is still unfinished.

## Fast facts

- **Born:** 1968, in the United States; grew up in Manchester, England.
- **BBC Micro:** *Wizadore* (Imagine, 1985), the BBC version of *[Match Day](/vault/games/match-day)* (Ocean, 1985) and *Stryker's Run* (Superior Software, 1986).
- **[Origin Systems](/vault/companies/origin-systems):** *Times of Lore* (1988), *Bad Blood* (1990), *[Wing Commander](/vault/games/wing-commander)* (1990) and its sequels, *Strike Commander* (1993).
- **Digital Anvil:** founded in Austin in 1996; *Starlancer* (2000); sold to [Microsoft](/vault/companies/microsoft), which finished *Freelancer* (2003) without him.
- **Films:** directed *Wing Commander* (1999); produced *Lord of War* (2005) and *Lucky Number Slevin* (2006).
- **Cloud Imperium Games:** *Star Citizen* and *Squadron 42*, crowdfunded from 2012 and still in development.

## BBC Micro games

Roberts was born in America but, as *The One* put it in 1992, "spent most of his formative years in Manchester". *Amiga Computing* heard his "Manchester twang" behind his Texan accent in 1988. The composer [Martin Galway](/vault/people/martin-galway), who later scored his games, was an old schoolfriend.

His first commercial game was *Wizadore*, a BBC Micro arcade adventure published in 1985 at £7.95 under the [Imagine](/vault/companies/imagine-software) name, which [Ocean](/vault/companies/ocean-software) had bought from the liquidators. *Computer Gamer*'s reviewer called it "Imagine's second release for the BBC Micro since their recent revival": a hero with limited magic, who draws his power from scrolls, crossing forests and dungeons past archers and guards. The graphics were "excellent, being colourful and smooth", but the early levels were "far too difficult". *The One* later dated *Wizadore* to "around 1983", but the reviews and charts place it in 1985.

The same year he converted Ocean's football game *[Match Day](/vault/games/match-day)* to the BBC Micro. *Computer Gamer* noticed the problem he had faced, the BBC's lack of hardware sprites, and credited him by name: "The programmer (Chris Roberts) has made a great attempt at 'pseudo-sprites', however, and the players and ball are satisfyingly large and convincingly animated."

*Stryker's Run* (1986) was his breakthrough. Superior Software's advertisement named him as "Game Author" and promised "probably the best graphics ever seen on the BBC Micro": Commander John Stryker runs, shoots and crawls across a war zone to carry secret documents to the Allies' headquarters. It cost £9.95 on cassette, and Superior called it its first release with a specially enhanced version for the 128K BBC Master. In February 1987 it was number one in *Acorn User*'s chart of BBC Micro games: "Lieutenant John Stryker runs up to the top". Not every reviewer agreed with the advertising: *Acorn User* admired the "absolutely excellent graphics" but found the sprites flickered and the action slow.

The sequel, *Codename: Droid* (1987), was not his. *Acorn User* credited it to "the authors of *Ravenskull*".

## Origin: Times of Lore and Bad Blood

*Amiga Computing* reported in 1988 that Roberts had "worked for Imagine in Britain before Origin's desire to develop a populist role-playing game transported him across the Atlantic". That game was *Times of Lore* (1988), released for the Commodore 64 and other machines, with graphics by Denis Loubet and music by Galway. It was an action role-playing game controlled entirely with the joystick: the player picked a knight, a valkyrie or a barbarian, spoke to villagers through menus of keywords, and watched a candle burn down instead of a count of hit points. *Computer Gaming World*'s Scorpia called it "a beginner's computer role-playing game", and [Richard Garriott](/vault/people/richard-garriott) praised its interface as better than his own *Ultima*'s. In Britain, where MicroProse sold it, the advertisements called Roberts an "acclaimed British designer".

*Bad Blood* (1990), a post-nuclear role-playing game for the PC, fared worse. Charles Ardai of *Computer Gaming World* judged it "top-flight work" in its mechanics but found "no compelling reason to play *Bad Blood* and several reasons not to", and accused Roberts of not taking his own creation seriously.

## Wing Commander

While finishing *Bad Blood*, Roberts had the idea for a space combat game. It was released in September 1990 as *[Wing Commander](/vault/games/wing-commander)*, with bitmapped ships that scaled and turned as they closed in, a score that changed with the fighting, and a story that went on between missions. *Computer Gaming World* made it the game of the year for 1991; the [Wing Commander](/vault/games/wing-commander) entry tells the full story. [Warren Spector](/vault/people/warren-spector) was among its producers.

*Wing Commander II* followed in 1991. Origin advertised Roberts's next game, the air combat game *Strike Commander*, "produced and directed by Chris Roberts", for Christmas 1991; it reached the shops in 1993. By then Roberts was preparing *Wing Commander III: Heart of the Tiger* (1994), with filmed actors in place of drawn characters: Mark Hamill, Malcolm McDowell and John Rhys-Davies. He was its director and executive producer. "In the past, when I created the first two *Wing Commander* games, I tried to be as cinematic as possible, but the technology held me back," he told *PC Gamer* in 1994. Accounts of its budget differ widely, from about $3 million to about $18 million. For *Wing Commander IV* (1996) he returned "to the director's chair", in *Computer and Video Games*' words, with a budget "reputedly nearing $10 million" and scenes shot on 35mm film on real sets.

## Digital Anvil and the film

Roberts left Origin, by then owned by [Electronic Arts](/vault/companies/electronic-arts), in 1996. With his brother Erin and Tony Zurovec, the designer of *Crusader*, he founded Digital Anvil in Austin. "One of the problems I faced at EA was that I could get any amount of money for another *Wing Commander*, but not something new," he told *Edge* in 1997. He wanted small teams of experienced people, not the 300 he said Origin had grown to, and Digital Anvil signed with Microsoft, which also bought a share in the company in 1997.

Its first game, *Starlancer* (2000), was a *Wing Commander*-style combat game made largely by Erin Roberts with the British developer Warthog, while Chris worked on the much larger *Freelancer*. Digital Anvil also made a film. Roberts directed *Wing Commander*, released in American cinemas in 1999 on a reported budget of $30 million; *Computer Gaming World* did not think it "even rental-worthy".

At the turn of 2001 Microsoft bought Digital Anvil outright and Roberts left. *PC Player* quoted him, in German: "I don't really want to work for a big publisher again. If I did, I would have stayed at Electronic Arts." He stayed on *Freelancer* as a consultant, and Microsoft released it in 2003.

## Hollywood

Roberts then worked in film. Through his company Ascendant Pictures he produced, among others, the arms-dealer drama *Lord of War* (2005) and the thriller *Lucky Number Slevin* (2006). When *Edge* interviewed him in 2012, it summed up the decade as "film directing with *Wing Commander* and then movie production for a few years".

## Star Citizen

He came back to games in 2012 with Cloud Imperium Games and *Star Citizen*, a space game he announced at the Game Developers Conference in October. Its Kickstarter asked for $500,000 and, as *Retro Gamer* reported, finished at $2,134,374. The crowdfunding carried on after the Kickstarter and passed $1 billion on 24 May 2026, with the game still in development and no release date. Its single-player campaign, *Squadron 42*, which Roberts calls a "spiritual successor to Wing Commander", has Mark Hamill in its cast again. In August 2026 its release was moved to the second quarter of 2027.
