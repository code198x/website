When Ocean wanted a coin-op on the Spectrum at the end of the 1980s, it sometimes sent a briefcase to Liverpool. Inside was the arcade board itself, with a joystick panel, and Jim Bagley had to make the home version from what he could see on the screen. "I didn't have access to any of the code which made the things tick," he remembered, "so I had to play through both just to see the action and then I had to reproduce what I could see as closely as possible on the destination machine." That was how he made the Spectrum *Cabal* and *Midnight Resistance*, and *CRASH* gave them 91% and 93%. More than thirty years later he was still programming Spectrums, and helping to design a new one.

## Fast facts

- **First job:** Consult Computer Systems, 1986, at 17; his first published game was Melbourne House's *Throne of Fire* (1987).
- **Special FX:** the only programmer on eleven games Ocean published: *Gutz* (Spectrum); *Batman: The Caped Crusader* (Amstrad CPC); *Red Heat*, *Cabal* and *Midnight Resistance* (Spectrum and Amstrad); *Hudson Hawk* (Spectrum, Amstrad and Game Boy).
- **Later:** *Doom* for the Sega Saturn (1997), at Rage Software.
- **Since 2017:** one of the founding team of the [ZX Spectrum Next](/vault/systems/zx-spectrum-next).

## Consult and Special FX

Bagley was 17 when he asked a local computer shop whether there were any games companies nearby. There were. In 1986 he joined Consult Computer Systems, where his first game to be published was *Throne of Fire*, released by [Melbourne House](/vault/companies/melbourne-house) in 1987. It was a design by [Mike Singleton](/vault/people/mike-singleton), who, *CRASH* noted, "hadn't programmed it" himself, and *CRASH*'s review credited it simply to "Consult". It took 75%. Bagley then moved to Canvas Software, where he wrote two more games and helped finish a third.

His next move was to Special FX, the Liverpool studio that Paul Finnegan and [Jonathan "Joffa" Smith](/vault/people/jonathan-smith) had set up after leaving [Ocean](/vault/companies/ocean-software) in 1987. "I've always stated that I never worked for Ocean," Bagley said, but nearly everything he wrote there was published by Ocean, and he made many trips with Finnegan to its Manchester offices. "We always joked at Special FX that we did the games the Ocean team didn't want to do."

His first Spectrum game there was *Gutz* (1988), with graphics by Chas Davies. The player is swallowed by a ten-million-tonne alien that also has its eye on Earth, and has to destroy it from inside. Smith, he said, showed him "his legendary push screen scroller technique, like the one in *Cobra*, which I used for the maze sections". *CRASH* gave it 62%. One reviewer called it "Hardly the frantic action of *Firefly*, more like a walk in the country."

## The briefcase conversions

The arcade conversions went better. "Most games back then were done to tight three-month schedules," Bagley told *Retro Gamer*, and some of that time went on simply playing the original. For *Cabal* and *Midnight Resistance* Ocean sent the arcade boards in a briefcase, "thankfully it was free-play". Bagley calls the method a "visual port": watching a game closely enough to work out what its code must be doing, then writing it again from scratch for a different machine.

*Cabal* (1989) was "a hard task to squeeze a big colourful arcade game onto the Spectrum, especially with the huge on-screen baddy count that it had". *CRASH* gave it 91%, praising its crowds of cartoon soldiers with "big clown feet". *Midnight Resistance* (1990) was harder still, "a huge game with lots of graphics, baddies, bullets and big maps, big baddies, and lots of colour. Chas and I managed to get it all in, even the cutscenes." *CRASH* made it 93% and wrote that Special FX had "come up with the goods". Bagley counts it among the games he is proudest of.

## Hudson Hawk, made for finishing

*Hudson Hawk* (1991) was a licence of the Bruce Willis film, which had flopped in cinemas. Bagley told *Retro Gamer* that the team never saw the film: "all we got as reference was the script". While he was still writing the Spectrum version, he explained to *CRASH* what he wanted from it:

> It's really going to be a fun game, not too difficult — something everyone'll find playable and nearly everyone should be able to complete!

The preview itself promised something tougher: the last screen of Da Vinci's castle was the gold room, and "wait 'till you see what devious puzzles await you in there!" When *CRASH* reviewed the finished game in December 1991 and made it a Smash, one reviewer admitted that "for the first few attempts, Hudson Hawk's pretty hair-tearing". The verdict was that the game was "an arcade puzzle fan's dream come true". Bagley also wrote the Game Boy version. When its Japanese text needed checking and nobody at Special FX could read Japanese, he said, Finnegan went outside and found some Japanese tourists to look it over, and Bagley put their names in the high-score table.

## What the magazines credited

Period credits are not always reliable, and Bagley's are a good example. *CRASH*'s review of *Midnight Resistance* said it was "programmed by the guy who brought you Batman", and its *Hudson Hawk* review called him "the man who brought *Batman — The Caped Crusader* and *Midnight Resistance* to your screens". The magazine's own review of the Spectrum *Batman: The Caped Crusader* had credited Jonathan Smith. Bagley's list of his games explains the mix-up: the *Batman* he wrote was the Amstrad CPC version. It ran the other way too. In 1989 a *CRASH* reader drew up a chart of Ocean's best programmers and put *Gutz* among Smith's games, though the magazine's own review had credited it to Bagley and Davies.

## Doom on the Saturn

After Special FX closed its label in 1992, Finnegan carried on as Rage Software, and Bagley was there when Rage converted *[Doom](/vault/games/doom)* to the Sega Saturn in 1997. He told *Retro Gamer* that his first demo for id Software used the Saturn's 3D hardware to draw the walls and floors. A couple of days later, he said, "I got a call from [John Carmack](/vault/people/john-carmack), who stipulated that under no circumstances could I use the 3D hardware to draw the screen." He wrote the renderer again to run on the console's two SH-2 processors, as the PC version did, but "it kneecapped the game and the speed-framerate suffered greatly". Fabien Sanglard's history of *Doom*'s engine records that the finished game managed up to 20 frames a second and often fell to single figures, and that Bagley slowed down all movement to compensate, which players hated. Carmack later wrote that "in hindsight, I probably should have let [him] experiment."

## Still writing for old machines

Bagley kept programming the old machines for fun. In 2005 *Retro Gamer* picked out his homebrew Game Boy Advance version of *Bruce Lee*, which "preserves the Speccy version's insanely playable gameplay". In 2012 he put [Manic Miner](/vault/games/manic-miner) on the original *[Pac-Man](/vault/games/pac-man)* arcade board, as *PacManicMinerMan*. That took "about a month of weekends", trimming each room by two characters to fit the narrower screen and squeezing 20 levels, at 1K each in the original, into 16K. In 2013 he wrote *Jim Bagley's ZX81 Racing* for a ZX81 with 16K and high-resolution graphics. Old hardware, he said, "has more memories and I love finding ways around limitations".

He went to his first retro event in 2009 and found, he said, "that there were huge numbers of people who enjoyed my games which Ocean published, and they still play them to this very day!" Since 2017 he has been part of the ZX Spectrum Next project. SpecNext's team page lists him among its founding members, responsible for "several functions of the Next (such as new video modes and sprites)". Mike Dailly, who wrote the #CSpect emulator, recalls Bagley borrowing an early version "to save him from carrying his dev board in and out of work all the time", and getting his *Donkey Kong* demo running on it. *Next* magazine credits Bagley with designing the NEX file format, which packs a whole Next program into one file, and he has taught Next programming in his live "Code along with Baggers" streams. "Thirty-three years later, I'm now involved in making the ZX Spectrum Next," he told *Wireframe* in 2018. "It'll be like the good old days, although the games won't be for sale in retail shops, sadly."
