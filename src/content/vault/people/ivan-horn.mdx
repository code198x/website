## Overview

Ivan Horn drew the graphics for some of the biggest-selling Spectrum games ever made. He appears in **39 issues** of this library's British magazines and in **no credit field anywhere in its structured data**, because the magazines of the period recorded artists in sentences and never in boxes.

## Fast facts

- ***CRASH***, reader's ranking of Ocean and Imagine staff: "**IVAN HORN — graphic artist for *Operation Wolf*, *Rambo III* and *Combat School*.**"
- **On *Thunderbolt*:** "Ivan Horn (graphics) were the guys who worked on the Speccy *Thunderbolt*, with ample help from their mate **[Jonathan Dunn](/vault/people/jonathan-dunn)** on music."
- **At:** Ocean, Manchester.

## What a Spectrum artist was doing

Drawing for the Spectrum is not drawing. The machine gives you 256×192 pixels in one colour of ink and one of paper per **8×8 cell**, which means the artist's real medium is a grid of colour decisions laid under a monochrome bitmap.

An *Operation Wolf* or a *Rambo III* had to look like its arcade cabinet or its film poster — that was the entire commercial proposition — while being assembled from that. The craft is in knowing where to spend: which objects get their own cell colour, where two things must share and therefore must be the same colour, and where a black outline can hide the seam. Get it wrong and you get [attribute clash](/vault/techniques/colour-clash) all over a licence somebody paid a fortune for.

Horn's games are among the ones that look expensive, which on this machine is a statement about an artist's judgement, not about a budget.

## Why he is nearly invisible

His invisibility is a fact about the record as much as about the man.

The Vault's structured layer holds **1,200 credit strings** from every magazine in the library, and not one of them is an artist credit. The reason is specific and measurable: *CRASH* prints a field labelled `Graphics:` on almost every review — 16.3 times an issue — and it contains an **adjective**. *excellent*. *average*. *very good*. *none*. The magazine had a slot for graphics and used it to rate them instead of to credit them.

So the artists of the British 8-bit era survive only where a writer happened to name them in running text: in a preview, a studio profile, or — in Horn's case — **a reader's letter**. Somebody in Humberside sat down, listed fifteen people at Ocean and Imagine, and posted it to Ludlow, and that is why this page can exist.

## Why he matters

Horn stands for **the artist as an author**, and for the fact that an entire craft has been nearly lost from the record by a formatting decision made in 1984.

Others from the same list remain single-sourced and unwritten — **Alison Jeftha**, graphic artist on *Typhoon*; **Mark R Jones** on *The Vindicator* and *Gryzor*; **William Harbison** on *Daley Thompson's Olympic Challenge*. They are named here because being named once, in print, is better than not being named at all.
