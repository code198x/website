Martin Galway was one of the defining voices of the SID chip era. Working primarily for Ocean Software, he composed soundtracks that treated the C64 as a serious musical instrument—*Wizball*, *Times of Lore*, *Parallax*—proving that game music could be ambitious, emotional, and technically sophisticated.

## Fast facts
- **Started young:** began composing on the C64 as a teenager in Belfast.
- **Ocean years:** joined [Ocean Software](/vault/companies/ocean-software) and became their go-to composer for prestige titles.
- **Signature style:** rich chord voicings, smooth bass lines, and an ear for melody that felt more pop than chiptune.
- **Later career:** moved to [Origin Systems](/vault/companies/origin-systems) in Texas, working on *[Wing Commander](/vault/games/wing-commander)* and other PC titles.
- **Industry shift:** transitioned to audio direction and sound design as game music professionalised.

## Almost entirely a Commodore figure
Galway's presence in this library is lopsided in a way worth stating, because it
is the clearest single measure of where he worked. *Martin Galway* appears on
**49 pages** of the Commodore press held here and **6** of the Spectrum press.

Of those six, four are the same Origin advertisement for *Times of Lore*,
repeated across consecutive issues of *Your Sinclair*, which lists "music by
Martin Galway" and names the Spectrum among the formats. ⚠ That is not evidence that his music is in the Spectrum build. A multi-format advert credits the product, not each conversion, and reading one as a per-version credit is a standing trap of the period's advertising. *Times of Lore* did reach the Spectrum, and reviewed well there — 94% in
*CRASH*, 91% in *Your Sinclair*, 90% in *Sinclair User*, all in July 1989 — but
who scored that version is not established by the sources held here.

The remaining two Spectrum-press mentions are a reader thanking Ocean "and in
particular Martin Galway, for providing some excellent title" music, and a
letter about music drivers that notes Galway used what its author called **the
Ocean Routine** while suspecting the routine itself was written by Jonathon
Smith.

## What that letter shows
The letter is worth more than its Galway sentence. Writing to *CRASH* in
September 1988, a reader classifies Spectrum game music by **which sound driver
produced it** — naming "the Ocean Routine", "the Music Box" and a Probe/Whittaker
routine, listing the games each covers, and challenging the developers to
confirm or deny.

Players could hear the difference between sound engines, and cared who wrote
them. That is the audience these composers had, and it is why a chart of game
music worked at all.

## How he got in

Galway walked into [Ocean](/vault/companies/ocean-software)'s Manchester office to demonstrate
music he had written for the BBC Micro. He left with a C64 and somebody else's music routine to
study, which is a fair description of how the job was learned at the time.

His first work for them was the loading music for *Daley Thompson's Decathlon* — and it was a
cover, of **Yellow Magic Orchestra's *Rydeen***, by way of the arcade game *Super Locomotive*
that had used it first. He was not alone for long:

> At that time I realised I had some competition. I thought I was the only one taking it
> seriously.

The competition was [Rob Hubbard](/vault/people/rob-hubbard). Ocean's *Rambo* became Galway's
calling card, a deliberate splice of the film score with SID sound, and *Arkanoid* is credited
as **the first commercial release to carry sampled sound alongside its music** — a claim worth
holding lightly, since firsts of that kind are usually contested, but the direction of travel
is not in doubt.

## Notable soundtracks
- **Wizball** (1987): dreamy, melodic themes that matched the game's surreal visuals.
- **Times of Lore** (1988): orchestral aspirations on 8-bit hardware.
- **Parallax** (1986): pulsing electronic soundtrack that enhanced the shooter's intensity.
- **Arkanoid** (1987): a cover of the arcade original that showcased Galway's arranging skills.

## Technical approach
Galway's music stood out for its harmonic sophistication:
- **Chord voicings:** used the SID's three voices to create full chords rather than simple arpeggios.
- **Filter sweeps:** employed the SID's resonant filter for expressive, evolving timbres.
- **Composition-first:** prioritised musicality over showing off technical tricks.
