Lloyd Mangram wrote *CRASH*'s Forum, its features, its retrospectives and a great deal of everything else, and appears on the masthead as Staff Writer. **He was not a person.** The name was a Newsfield house pseudonym, shared between staff and contributors — confirmed since by several of the people who wrote under it.

## Who he was, in the words of one of him
The disclosure comes from **Matthew Uffindell**, a *CRASH* reviewer and
contributing writer, in a later retrospective:

> A little, if at all known fact was that he never existed! Lloyd's input was
> the joint combination of text and opinion from both Roger and myself.

"Roger" is **Roger Kean**, *CRASH*'s editor. Uffindell adds that he did not
think anyone had ever admitted it before.

The archive corroborates the arrangement without ever revealing it. *CRASH*
issue 7, August 1984, runs a retrospective feature under a joint byline —
"**MATTHEW UFFINDELL and LLOYD MANGRAM** take keyboard and reputation in hand" —
so one of the two men writing as Mangram is credited alongside Mangram, in the
same headline, as though they were colleagues. Uffindell appears on 36 pages of
the Spectrum press held here under his own name, and on many more under the
other one.

## Fast facts

- **Byline on:** the Forum (the letters page), features, regulars and retrospectives.
- **On the masthead:** as Staff Writer, nine times across this library's issues.
- **Bylined across:** 191 pages of the Spectrum press held here — from *CRASH* issue 1, February 1984, onwards.
- **Status:** a house pseudonym — Roger Kean and Matthew Uffindell, by Uffindell's own
  account afterwards. Nothing in the period archive shows it: the magazines print the name
  exactly as they print a real staff writer's.

## Why a magazine invents a person

Three reasons, all of them practical.

**A small staff sounds bigger.** *CRASH* was made by a handful of people in Ludlow. A masthead and a contents page thick with distinct bylines reads like a substantial operation, and readers judged magazines partly on that.

**A column needs a consistent voice more than a consistent author.** The Forum answered readers' letters month after month; whoever was free that week could write it, and the reader still got the same person replying. A named house voice is a **shared, reusable persona** — the same idea as a reusable game engine, applied to editorial.

**It separates the role from the individual.** Staff leave. A column bylined to a person dies or awkwardly changes hands when they go; a column bylined to Lloyd Mangram continues, and the magazine keeps a relationship the reader has invested in.

## The problem it creates for a record

The byline matters as much for what it does to the evidence as for what it says about *CRASH*.

The scale is the point. "Lloyd Mangram" appears on **191 pages** of the Spectrum press held in this library — more than [Derek Brewster](/vault/people/derek-brewster) on 186, more than editor Roger Kean on 170, and comparable to [Mike Gerrard](/vault/people/mike-gerrard) on 204. On the evidence of the corpus alone he is one of the most prolific writers of the British Spectrum press, and he is nobody.

Everything this library knows about magazine staff comes from mastheads and bylines. Those are the strongest sources available — a labelled role, a name, a date. And here is a name occupying that slot for years, in exactly the format a real person occupies it, that resolves to **several different people and no single one**.

There is no test inside the corpus that catches this. Read the pages and you find a Staff Writer called Lloyd Mangram appearing across dozens of issues, exactly as the pages report him. Only testimony from outside — people who worked there, saying so afterwards — settles it.

That is worth generalising. **A record can be accurate and still not be true**, and the archives that look most reliable are the ones where a convention has been applied consistently enough to be invisible. Anywhere a byline recurs with unusual reach and no other trace, the possibility of a house name has to stay open.

## Why it matters

Mangram is **the pen name as editorial infrastructure**, a normal magazine practice of the period that later readers take at face value.

He is also, in a strict sense, the most prolific writer in this Vault: the byline is on more *CRASH* pages than any individual's. The work is real, the reader relationship was real, and the person was a convention.
