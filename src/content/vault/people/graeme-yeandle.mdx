Graeme Yeandle wrote [The Quill](/vault/tools/the-quill), which did more to
shape British adventure output in the 1980s than most publishers managed.

He was not a games professional when he did it. *Sinclair User*'s April 1985
profile of [Gilsoft](/vault/companies/gilsoft) describes The Quill as having
come along "almost accidentally", through Yeandle — a friend of
[Tim Gilberts](/vault/people/tim-gilberts) and a systems analyst by profession.

## What he wrote
- **[The Quill](/vault/tools/the-quill)** (1983). *Sinclair User* scored it 9/10 three separate times, and it charted at #34 in *CRASH*'s reader chart in December 1984 — a utility competing with games for shelf position.
- ***Magic Castle*** (1984), a £5.95 machine-code Spectrum adventure credited to him by name in *CRASH*'s May 1984 review.
- **[PAW](/vault/tools/paw)** (1987), co-credited with Tim Gilberts, Phil Wade, Alex Williams, Kevin Maddocks and Dicon Peeke. *CRASH* gave it 97%.
- **S.W.A.N.**, the System Without A Name, reported by *Sinclair User* in August 1988 as developed with Gilberts and others.

## Why it mattered
The Quill's design decision was to make adventure authoring a data-entry task
rather than a programming one: locations, objects, messages and condition
tables, filled in through menus and compiled to a compact runtime.

That is an unglamorous description of the thing that let a generation of people
who could not write Z80 assembly ship a real game — and sell it. The adventure
columns of the period reviewed Quilled games beside commercial releases, which
is the practical measure of how well the tool worked.
