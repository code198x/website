Switch on a ZX Spectrum and the first program it runs is Steven Vickers's. The 16K ROM sets up the hardware, clears the memory, prints "© 1982 Sinclair Research Ltd" and waits for you to type. Behind that one line sits a BASIC interpreter, a line editor, a keyboard scanner that runs 50 times a second, the tape loading and saving routines and the code that draws every character on the screen. Vickers wrote most of it, with his colleague John Grant, and his name is on the cover of the book that taught Spectrum owners how to use it.

## From mathematics to ROMs

In July 1982 *Sinclair User* described Vickers as 29 years old, with a mathematics degree from King's College, Cambridge, and a PhD from Leeds. He had come to computers late. "Two years ago I did not even know what a ROM was," he told the magazine.

In 1980, after writing to several computer companies, Sinclair among them, he joined Nine Tiles, a software consultancy near Cambridge. Nine Tiles had already written the BASIC for Sinclair's ZX80. Vickers was a Nine Tiles contractor, not a Sinclair employee, and Sinclair's ROMs were written under contract.

## The ZX81

His first job was to turn the ZX80's 4K ROM into the 8K ROM of the [ZX81](/vault/systems/sinclair-zx81), and he wrote the ZX81's manual as well. The main addition, as he later described it, was a floating-point maths pack. The ZX80 could only handle whole numbers; the ZX81 advertised a BASIC that "handles logs and trig". *CRASH* later described how the floating-point code was squeezed in: as a small interpreter inside the ROM, "written very compactly in a FORTH type language", so that a square root took only seven bytes of code but "an awful long time to run". The Spectrum kept the same routines, which is why its arithmetic is slow too.

## The ZX Spectrum

The Spectrum's rough specification was set in September 1981, with colour, high-resolution graphics and a better tape interface, and a fixed deadline: the computer show at Earl's Court the following April. Richard Altwasser designed the hardware. Vickers and John Grant wrote the ROM, again on contract from Nine Tiles, with Vickers writing most of it. It is a 16K program that grew out of the ZX81's. *CRASH* pointed out that a Spectrum still contains a 24-byte routine that checks for ZX81 program names.

The deadline was real, and Vickers said so: "There is a definition of a deadline: that it is the date before which something should not be completed, but that is not the case with Sinclair."

### What Sinclair BASIC could do

[Sinclair BASIC](/vault/tools/sinclair-basic) on the Spectrum kept the ZX80's two best ideas. Each keyword went in with a single keypress, and each line was checked for mistakes as it was entered. The Spectrum version added:

- colour and sound commands (INK, PAPER, BORDER, BEEP);
- graphics commands built in: PLOT, DRAW and CIRCLE;
- one-line user-defined functions with DEF FN;
- numbered report codes with short messages, such as "2 Variable not found".

It had limits, too. Every program line needed a line number. There were no named procedures: a program reused code with GO SUB and RETURN.

## The manual

The Spectrum came with *Sinclair ZX Spectrum BASIC Programming*, whose title page reads "by Steven Vickers, edited by Robin Bradbeer". *Sinclair User* in 1982 described Vickers as "assisting with the manual", so the book was not his alone. It works up through 26 chapters from the keyboard, through line numbers, loops, strings and graphics, to the system variables and machine code, with a reference section at the back.

## Jupiter Cantab

In mid-1982 Vickers and Altwasser left Sinclair Research to set up their own company, which became Jupiter Cantab. Its advertisements called them "leading computer designers" who had played "the major role in creating the ZX Spectrum". Their [Jupiter Ace](/vault/systems/jupiter-ace) went on sale by mail order at £89.95 with the language Forth in its ROM instead of BASIC.

It did not sell. Altwasser left in 1983, leaving Vickers as a co-director. By the end of the year the company was in liquidation, after selling about five or six thousand Aces. In the Ace owners' newsletter from that year, Vickers can be found politely correcting the editor on how the Z80 addresses its input and output ports.

## Later career

Vickers returned to mathematics. He became a lecturer in the School of Computer Science at the University of Birmingham, working on the mathematical logic that underpins computing.
