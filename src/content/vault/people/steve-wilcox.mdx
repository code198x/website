In 1985 Steve Wilcox sat in a London hotel suite with his father, Brian, bidding for the home computer rights to Capcom's *[Commando](/vault/games/commando)*. Brian offered £25,000. Steve, sure the game would sell in six figures, turned that into 100,000 copies at a royalty of 65p each, and the offer went up to £65,000 on the spot. "My old man, to his credit, didn't even flinch," he told *Retro Gamer* twenty years later. *Commando* was the best-selling game of that Christmas, and Wilcox spent the next two years turning arcade machines into Spectrum, Commodore 64 and Amstrad games for the family firm, [Elite Systems](/vault/companies/elite-systems).

## From shop to publisher

Wilcox took a chemistry degree, spent ten months in a big industrial company, got frustrated, and opened a computer shop in a back street in Walsall. He called it Bowies, after his favourite musician, which by his own account "confused everybody". By autumn 1983 he was selling Spectrums "by the truckload", *Commodore Computing International* reported.

His younger brother, Richard, had taught himself to program and written a shoot-'em-up for the Atari 400. Their father told them they should be making games, not just selling them. Richard converted his game to the Spectrum as *Blue Thunder*, and when the brothers took it to W H Smith's head office, Steve recalled, the buyers ordered 5,000 copies, about £12,000 worth. It came out in 1983 under the name Richard Wilcox Software. Richard was 15 and still at school, so Steve hired four local teenagers to write more, and their *Kokotoni Wilf* was the first release under the new name. *CRASH* reported in October 1984 that "Elite Systems Ltd., formed from Richard Wilcox Software" had launched it, with Steve Wilcox as sales and marketing manager.

Period sources name Steve and Brian Wilcox as Elite's founders and owners. *Commodore Computing International* found them in June 1987 in offices above a tandoori restaurant and a hairdresser, with a black Jaguar and a gold Lotus Esprit in the car park, "father and son, Brian and Steve Wilcox, who own Elite completely". It described Steve, then 27, as tall, slim and moustached, a nervous smoker who was "reputedly" the youngest software millionaire in Britain, and credited his father, a retired foundry owner, with bringing "quality control" to the business. *Edge* still called them "founders Steve Wilcox and his father Brian" in 1994. By 2005 *Retro Gamer* was describing Steve as Elite's chairman.

## Writing to a formula

Wilcox was open about what Elite did and did not do. "We write to a formula," he told *Your Computer* in July 1986, when Elite had had three games, *Frank Bruno's Boxing*, *Airwolf* and *Commando*, in the top twelve sellers of 1985. "You do not have to read a 15-page instruction manual to play one of our games. … They have played it in the arcades, they want to buy it, load it and play it. After a short time they will get fed up with it, go out and buy another one." He admitted the weakness that came with it: "If we have a weakness, it is that we cannot write anything original of any great consequence." Elite had no in-house programmers then, he said, but about 40 freelancers it could call on.

The formula depended on licences, and Wilcox chased them himself. After the *Commando* deal was signed in August 1985 he flew to a game show in Tokyo that September to see what was coming. He found, he said, that he was the only British publisher there. He phoned his father, who told him to sign up everything worth having, and he came back with the rights to *[Paperboy](/vault/games/paperboy)*, *[Space Harrier](/vault/games/space-harrier)* and *[Bomb Jack](/vault/games/bomb-jack)*, among others. "I returned to the show the following year and it was absolutely swarming with UK publishing people." By Wilcox's figures Elite turned over about £600,000 in its first year, £1.5 million in its second and more than £3 million in 1986, the year *Computer and Video Games*' readers voted it Software House of the Year.

He could also take criticism. When *ZZAP!64* printed its readers' awards for 1986, Wilcox, described as "Joint Director of Elite", thanked the programmers and readers and added: "We recognise that our 64 conversions are perhaps the weakest amongst those we publish, so we intend to improve the standard of products over 1987."

He was careful with new machines. Asked by *8000 Plus* in October 1986 whether Elite would publish for Amstrad's PCW, he said: "obviously it's an extension to the market, but we've really got to suck it and see."

## After the licences

By Wilcox's account Elite gave up on licences when agents pushed the prices up: one of Atari's agents wanted £50,000 up front for *720°*, and Elite walked away. It spent 1987 to 1989 on original games, which he admitted sold poorly. He had seen the Famicom's success on his trips to Japan, and had a subcontractor reverse-engineer it so that Elite could write NES games. From 1990 Elite published for Nintendo's consoles, and its Super NES football game *Striker* sold more than 200,000 copies, he said. Wilcox even appeared in one of his own games: *Computer and Video Games* noted in 1989 that "'thrifty' Steve Wilcox, as he is known in the trade", made "a starring appearance" in Elite's *A Question of Sport*.

At the end of 1994, Wilcox said, Sony cut the price it paid publishers for new products just before Christmas, and Elite lost £2 million. It became a developer instead, and by his account its PC game *Test Drive: Off-Road* was its first million-seller. From about 2002 he took Elite back to its old catalogue, re-acquiring the rights to *Bomb Jack*, *Buggy Boy*, *Ikari Warriors* and *Paperboy* for mobile phones. "We're still here today, 21 years on, and I don't imagine I'll ever do anything else," he told *Retro Gamer* in 2005.

In October 2010 Elite released *ZX Spectrum: Elite Collection* for the iPhone, which reached number seven in the UK paid chart, and in 2012 it put *Manic Miner* on Xbox Live Indie Games. Wilcox called Elite's software a "facilitator" rather than an emulator, and told *Retro Gamer* that the market for Spectrum games in 2011 had been worth about £250,000 at retail prices: "And we have significantly more than half of that market." In 2014 Elite sought £60,000 on Kickstarter for the Recreated ZX Spectrum, a Bluetooth keyboard shaped like a Spectrum 48K (see [Elite Systems](/vault/companies/elite-systems)).
