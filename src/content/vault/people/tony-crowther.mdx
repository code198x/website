## Overview

Tony Crowther appears in **sixty-eight issues** of the British magazines held in this library — a rate almost no other programmer matches — across a career that starts with C64 arcade games in 1984 and reaches *Captive* and *Liberation* on the Amiga a decade later.

## Fast facts

- **Early C64:** *Blagger* for [Alligata](/vault/companies/alligata), *Potty Pigeon*, *Loco*, *Son of Blagger*.
- **Later:** *Captive 2 / Liberation* and *Captain Planet*, published on the Amiga by [Mindscape](/vault/companies/mindscape).
- ***ZZAP!64*** ran a joint **Tony Crowther and [Martin Galway](/vault/people/martin-galway) interview**, and credits him elsewhere for sound as well as code.

## Prolific, in a period when that was possible

Crowther's early output is the clearest illustration of what a single C64 programmer could sustain when a game was a few weeks of work. *Blagger* in 1984, then a sequel, then several more, at a pace no team-built game could match.

That pace is not laziness or cynicism; it is the actual shape of the industry before games got big. A programmer who could finish something good in six weeks could have four or five titles in the shops a year, each one paying, each one an experiment. The **iteration loop was short enough to learn from**, which is why programmers of this generation improved so visibly across their catalogues.

Note also that *ZZAP!* credits him for **sound effects** on other people's games and interviewed him alongside Martin Galway. The boundary between programmer, musician and designer had not yet hardened into job titles.

## Two eras, one person

The Amiga work is the other half. *Captive*'s sequel and *Liberation* are first-person, real-time, party-based games — a genre with a completely different set of problems from a C64 platform game, on hardware three generations along.

Few of the people in this Vault made that crossing and stayed good at it. Most 8-bit programmers either stopped, moved into management, or produced 16-bit work that read as an 8-bit programmer's idea of what a 16-bit game was. Crowther's late work does not.

That is why sixty-eight issues is the number in the subtitle, not a game title. **The press kept writing about him because he kept being relevant**, through a decade in which the technology, the economics and the audience all changed completely.

## Why he matters

Crowther is the entry for **longevity as a form of skill**. The Vault has plenty of people who made one extraordinary thing; he is one of the few who kept making things as the ground moved.

He also sits at the junction of several other entries here — [Alligata](/vault/companies/alligata) in Sheffield at the start, [Mindscape](/vault/companies/mindscape) publishing him on the Amiga at the end — which makes his career a usable thread through the whole period instead of a point in it.
