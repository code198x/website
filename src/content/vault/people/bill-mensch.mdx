**Bill Mensch** was one of the engineers behind the [6502](/vault/hardware/6502), the processor family used across machines including the [Apple II](/vault/systems/apple-ii), [BBC Micro](/vault/systems/bbc-micro) and [Commodore 64](/vault/systems/commodore-64). He later founded **Western Design Center**, carrying the architecture into CMOS and extended designs.

That history joins two jobs which can be confused: designing a processor’s logic and making a chip that can be manufactured economically. The 6502’s appeal to computer builders depended on both.

## An engineering team

In his Computer History Museum oral history, Mensch describes moving from Motorola to MOS Technology with colleagues including [Chuck Peddle](/vault/people/chuck-peddle). He distinguishes Peddle’s commercial and organising role from his own circuit-design work. The team history also identifies Mensch as a co-designer, rather than assigning the processor to a single inventor.

His account discusses the manufacturing work behind the design, including masks and yields. These are recollections recorded in 2014, not measurements that can establish a particular factory’s output on their own. They explain why an engineer might spend as much attention on production as on the instruction set visible to a programmer.

The distinction remains useful when comparing the processor with a complete home computer. A machine’s memory map, display, keyboard and storage were further designs made around the CPU. Sharing a processor family did not make the Apple II and BBC Micro interchangeable.

## Continuing the architecture

Mensch’s later Western Design Center work included the **65C02** and **65C816** families. CMOS implementation and the 65C816’s wider programming model were different kinds of change: a successor could preserve familiar instructions while also adding capabilities that software had to request and understand.

This continuation helps explain why learning 6502 assembly could remain useful beyond the first generation of machines. Familiar operations survived, but a program still needed to account for the actual processor variant, operating modes and surrounding hardware.

Mensch’s career gives that continuity a human connection. The original design was a team’s work; its later development was also a sustained engineering project, rather than the automatic afterlife of a successful chip.
