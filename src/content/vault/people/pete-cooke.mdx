Pete Cooke is one of the most heavily discussed programmers in this library's British press — named across some forty-five issues — and *CRASH* put him on the cover of issue 42, July 1987, as a **"Programmer: Pete Cooke"** feature. The magazines treated him as a subject in his own right, not just as the person behind a title.

## Fast facts

- **Known for:** *Tau Ceti* (1985), *Academy* (*Tau Ceti II*), *Micronaut One*, *Room Ten*, *Juggernaut*.
- ***CRASH*** issue 42, July 1987, ran a **programmer profile** of him as a cover item.
- **Recurring interest:** solid 3D, and a world that carries on without you.

## What Tau Ceti is

*Tau Ceti* looks like a 3D shooter and behaves like a simulation. You land on an abandoned colony planet, drive a skimmer between cities in a solid, shaded, filled-polygon landscape, and shut down a runaway reactor.

The parts worth noting are the ones no arcade game needed:

**A world with state.** The planet has cities, each with its own installations and defences, and shutting the reactor down requires collecting rods from across the map. The game is a logistics problem wearing an arcade costume.

**Day and night.** Time passes, visibility changes, and the tactics change with it.

**A vehicle, not a crosshair.** The skimmer has momentum, a heat budget, and a rate at which things go wrong. You are managing a machine, not aiming a gun.

Doing that in solid 3D on a Z80 at 3.5 MHz — with filled surfaces, not [wireframe](/vault/techniques/vector-graphics) — put it among the small group of Spectrum games that made the machine look like it had been underestimated.

## Why the magazines profiled him

The programmer profile was a recognisable eighties magazine form and it had a reason to exist. When a game was one person's work, the interesting question was **how one person did that**, and the answer was not in the review.

Cooke got the treatment because his games raised the question loudly. *Micronaut One* is a tunnel-crawling 3D game with a level editor; *Room Ten* is three-dimensional zero-gravity ball games; *Juggernaut* is a lorry-driving simulation with a load that shifts. They have almost nothing in common with each other except an interest in **simulating a system and then finding the game inside it** — the opposite of the usual direction of travel.

## Why he matters

Cooke works in **simulation-first design on 8-bit hardware**, a lineage that sits beside the arcade tradition instead of beneath it, and that produced *Elite*, *Midwinter* and eventually most of what people now call systemic design.

He is also a reminder of what the press was for. A magazine that puts a programmer's name on the cover is telling its readers that the person is the reason to care — and forty years later, that cover line is the reason this entry can be written at all.
