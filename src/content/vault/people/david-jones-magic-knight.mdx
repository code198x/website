> **Not to be confused with** [Dave Jones of DMA Design](/vault/people/dave-jones), who made *Lemmings* and *Grand Theft Auto*. *Your Sinclair* ran a competition on the confusion in 1986; see below.

## Overview

David Jones wrote the four **Magic Knight** games — *Finders Keepers*, *Spellbound*, *Knight Tyme* and *Stormbringer* — for [Mastertronic](/vault/companies/mastertronic) and its [Added Dimension](/vault/companies/mastertronic-added-dimension) label between 1985 and 1987. They sold at £1.99 and £2.99, and they contain an interface that full-price games of the period did not attempt.

## Fast facts

- **The series:** *Finders Keepers* (1985, Mastertronic, £1.99, **85% in *CRASH***), *Spellbound*, *Knight Tyme*, *Stormbringer* (M.A.D., £2.99).
- ***CRASH*** describes *Stormbringer* as the fourth Magic Knight game, naming the three before it.
- **The interface:** "Windimation" — pull-down menus, activated by a keypress, over a running arcade adventure.

## Windimation

The Magic Knight games are arcade adventures: rooms, an inventory, characters to interact with, puzzles that need notes and a map. What makes them unusual is how you play them.

Instead of typed commands or a fixed set of keys, pressing a key brings up **pull-down menus** over the game — objects to take or drop, people to talk to, spells to cast, actions to try. Jones called it *Windimation*, and the British press liked it: *CRASH*'s reviewers called the system "a delight to use".

This is 1985, on a machine with 48 kilobytes and no mouse. A windowed, menu-driven interface has to be drawn over a moving game, dismissed without corrupting what is underneath, and driven from the keyboard fast enough not to be irritating — on a display with [attribute colour constraints](/vault/techniques/colour-clash) that make overlapping graphics expensive.

The wider point is where it appeared. Menu-driven interaction is usually traced through the Macintosh, then [SCUMM](/vault/techniques/scumm) and the point-and-click adventures at full price on 16-bit machines. It also turns up here, first, on a **£2.99 Spectrum cassette**.

## "Will The Real David Jones Please Stand Up"

*Your Sinclair* issue 12, 1986, records the name collision — with a third David Jones.

The magazine reports that "David 'Spellbound/Knight Tyme' Jones" had handed out Magic Knight T-shirts at the PCW Show and offered ten more to *YS* readers. To win one, you had to answer: **"Which David Jones is now a Thin White Duke?"** Entries went to the *Will The Real David Jones Please Stand Up Compo*.

The answer is David Bowie, born David Jones. The joke is a useful reminder that this problem is not an artefact of modern indexing — the confusion was live at the time, and the magazines played with it.

## Why he matters

Two reasons.

**The technical one:** an ambitious interface, executed well, on the cheapest tier of the market, years before the industry treated menus as the normal way to run an adventure.

**The archival one:** this entry exists because the Vault previously held *two* entries for the DMA Design founder and none for him. A name is not an identifier. Where a credit is the only evidence, the honest options are to disambiguate explicitly or to leave the link out — never to guess.
