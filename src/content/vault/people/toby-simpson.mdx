In February 1990 *[ACE](/vault/magazines/ace-magazine)* sat five young programmers down with the engineers of Flare Technology in Cambridge, the team behind the Konix Multisystem, to discuss the games console they had designed on paper. Their chairman was Toby Simpson, a 19-year-old freelance programmer. Their machine had four processors, up to six playfields and 32-voice quadraphonic sound, and Flare's engineers told them it would cost far too much: "Price is absolutely all in this game." Three years later Simpson was lead programmer at [Millennium](/vault/companies/millennium-interactive), and his game *Diggers* was in the box with a real console, Commodore's [Amiga CD32](/vault/systems/amiga-cd32).

## Fast facts

- **Early work:** chairman of The Mind Boggles (TMB) Development Team, 1989–90; Amiga utilities on magazine cover disks.
- **[Millennium](/vault/companies/millennium-interactive):** designer of *Global Effect* (1992), designer and programmer of *Diggers* (1993) and its sequel *Extractors* (1995); later producer.
- **Writing:** programming and hardware expert and C tutorial writer for *[Amiga Shopper](/vault/magazines/amiga-shopper)*, 1992–95; author of *ARexx: Your Amiga's Built-In Turbocharger* (Future Publishing).
- **CyberLife:** creative director and producer on *Creatures 2* (1998) and *Creatures 3* (1999).
- **Later:** *Time of Defiance* at Nicely Crafted Entertainment (2002); head of software design at DeepMind; co-founder of Fetch.ai.

## The Mind Boggles

Simpson first appears in the Amiga press in August 1989, writing to *Amiga Computing* from Spixworth, near Norwich: "Our group (The TMB Dev.Corp) is currently working on a new game for the Amiga. We need original music rather desperately." Two months later the magazine put one of his programs on its cover disk, a utility that ran a list of favourite startup commands from a single word, and introduced him as a "19-year-old freelance programmer" whose ambition was "to become excessively rich and one of the most respected Amiga programmers in the world. We suspect he probably will."

*ACE* took the group more seriously still. The magazine had issued a £20,000 challenge to encourage discussion of games hardware, and The Mind Boggles Development Team answered with the TMBPC, their idea of "the perfect games computer". As "dedicated Amiga programmers", the five said they had "experienced all the mistakes that the original developers made with the Amiga's internal custom chips design". Flare's reply was the lesson of the Konix: "You haven't engineered the price in. It's a lovely idea, but you won't be able to sell it because it's too expensive. Our skill is cost engineering."

## Global Effect

At Millennium, Simpson led the development of *Global Effect* (1992), a world-management game for the Amiga and PC published with Electronic Arts. *PC Review* reported that he had been working on it for two years, and that the computer opponent would "aim to keep a good environmental balance", attacking only when provoked. *Computer Gaming World* named him as its designer and summed the game up as "SimCity meets Sim Earth meets Balance of the Planet". Every action cost energy, even moving around the map.

He was also writing for the magazines. From 1992 *Amiga Shopper* listed him as its expert on programming and hardware, its "code clinician" for "anything from C to Assembler", and he wrote C programming tutorials for it until at least late 1995. In one he admitted that the off-line reader he had written for the CIX conferencing service years before was "written so Amiga-specific" that porting it to the PC was not "remotely feasible". By early 1995 Future Publishing was selling his book on ARexx, the Amiga's scripting language.

## Diggers and the CD32

*Diggers* was the first game written only for the CD32, and *Amiga Power* saw it before it saw any other. In the preview Simpson spelled out the scale: "the graphics alone take up ten megabytes of space", which the magazine's Tim Tucker translated as "about 12 floppy disks, you know". Players controlled a team of miners from one of four races on the planet Zarg, digging for jewels to sell, against a computer team. "You can play it as a violent struggle against the computer opponent, or as a strategic strip-mining affair," Simpson said, and the treasure moved every time a level restarted. The instructions were an "interactive book" on the disc.

In *Amiga Format*'s CD32 supplement he explained what the format meant to a developer. Game music was usually composed on synthesisers and "scaled down" to fit; on CD "there is no scaling down, you can have CD-quality digital music". "When you have 600Mb of space, you don't worry how much space a large intro might take, or how much space those extra 30 levels use," he wrote. "From a programmer's point of view, developing for CD32 is a dream come true. You can assume a 68020 CPU, 2Mb of RAM, Kickstart 3.1 and lots of other assorted goodies." He thought development for the CD32 was "cheap and fairly low risk" for publishers, because its games would "port nicely to the A1200".

Commodore launched the CD32 in Britain in 1993 with two games, *Diggers* and [Flair](/vault/companies/flair-software)'s *Oscar*. Before Christmas it added *Wing Commander* and *Dangerous Streets* and kept the price at £299, and in May 1994 it added *Microcosm* and *The Chaos Engine*, with the price staying at £299.99. *Diggers* stayed in the box through every version of the pack. [Commodore](/vault/companies/commodore) announced its liquidation on 29 April 1994.

*Amiga Power* gave the CD32 version 80%. Its reviewer of the A1200 version admired the game's ambition but found it "not, actually, fun to play": three of his miners could be busy for an hour and a half before the computer's team won without warning. When the sequel *Extractors* arrived in 1995, set 150 years later, the magazine's original reviewer admitted that he had given *Diggers* "an astonishing 80%" partly because "it was the first CD32 specific game, so maybe in my naive youth I thought a good mark might aid sales".

## Creatures

Simpson moved on to artificial life with CyberLife, the company built around Millennium's *Creatures*, in which players raise and breed small creatures called Norns whose behaviour comes from simulated genes, biochemistry and brains. In 1998 *Edge* introduced him as CyberLife's creative director. "What made *Creatures* attractive was the interactions with what you were able to believe were real living systems," he said of *Creatures 2*. "In the original *Creatures* we presented a lot of science in front of people, when actually all along they just wanted to play with the animals!" He was surprised by the fans who swapped eggs and genes online: "I used to say we were second only to the Quake Web populace but that's not true any more — we are the biggest."

As producer of *Creatures 3* in 1999 he described Norns with "a whole new brain" and a ship full of machines that players could wire together from "'or' gates, 'and' gates, 'not' gates, counters, delays", adding: "You could theoretically build a working computer out of the parts."

## Later career

In 2002 *PC Gamer* interviewed him at Nicely Crafted Entertainment about *Time of Defiance*, an online strategy game whose empires could synchronise "thousands of objects over a 9,600 bps modem or less" and run on while their owners were away. He later joined DeepMind, which Google went on to buy, as head of software design, and co-founded Fetch.ai, where he has served as chief operating officer and chief technology officer. Interviewers there still introduce him as the producer of the *Creatures* games.
