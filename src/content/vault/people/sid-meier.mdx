For the first few years of MicroProse, Sid Meier did almost everything on its games himself. "I actually did all the art up until *Silent Service*," he told *Retro Gamer*, "and then we hired an artist and I realised he could do a much better job than I could." He did the sound effects too. By 1987 his name was selling the games: *Pirates!* came out as *Sid Meier's Pirates!*, and the habit stuck through *Railroad Tycoon* and *[Civilization](/vault/games/civilization)*. In 1989 he explained what he was looking for in a subject: "We try to pick situations where one player is the hero and has all the interesting decisions to make." The idea became his best-known saying, usually quoted as "a game is a series of interesting decisions": Bruce Shelley credited it to him in 1998, and Meier himself put it as "a series of interesting choices" in 2006.

## Fast facts

- **Born:** 24 February 1954, in Sarnia, Ontario; he grew up in Detroit.
- **Before games:** a computer science degree from the University of Michigan, then programming at General Instrument.
- **MicroProse:** co-founded with Bill Stealey in 1982; left in 1996.
- **Firaxis Games:** co-founded in 1996 with Jeff Briggs and Brian Reynolds.
- **Best known for:** *F-15 Strike Eagle*, *Silent Service*, *[Pirates!](/vault/games/pirates)*, *Railroad Tycoon* and *[Civilization](/vault/games/civilization)*.

## A bet over Red Baron

Meier studied history and computer science at the University of Michigan, and afterwards wrote software for cash registers. He was working at General Instrument when he met Bill Stealey, an accountant and former US Air Force pilot. The Atari 800, his first personal computer, had got him writing games "for fun", starting with a version of *Space Invaders*.

Both men told *[Edge](/vault/magazines/edge)* in 1997 how [MicroProse](/vault/companies/microprose) began. At a business meeting in Las Vegas they played Atari's coin-op *Red Baron*. "The high score on the machine was something like 32,000 and I scored 35,000," Stealey said. "Sid marched right past me with 62,000." Meier said he could write a better game; Stealey bet him he couldn't, and promised to sell it if he did. The result was *Hellcat Ace*, one of three [Atari 8-bit](/vault/systems/atari-8-bit) games in MicroProse's first advertisement, in *Antic* in October 1982. Meier later summed up the partnership: "I enjoyed creating the games and programming and he enjoyed selling them and running the company."

## Simulations

*Computer Gaming World* listed Meier as the author of MicroProse's early games, from the arcade game *Floyd of the Jungle* to the wargame *NATO Commander*. *F-15 Strike Eagle* (1984), credited to Meier and Grant Irani, made the company's name in flight simulation, and in January 1986 the magazine printed Meier's designer's notes on the submarine game *Silent Service*. In November 1986 *Compute!'s Gazette* said Meier had "designed or directed the design of virtually all of MicroProse Software's popular simulation and strategy games". In 1988 he worked with Tom Clancy on *Red Storm Rising*, based on Clancy's novel.

Even the military games were built around a person making choices. "It's not the mechanic, it's not the guy who designed the plane, it's not the guy back at headquarters telling them where to fly," Meier told *Compute!'s Gazette* in 1989. "It's the pilot who has the most interesting job." Stealey supplied the realism: he had contacts, Meier said, who "could get us secret air force manuals or cool information about aeroplanes".

## Pirates! and the name on the box

*Pirates!* (1987) was Meier's first game to leave the military simulations behind. MicroProse's advertisements called it "another winner from Sid Meier", which "combined the best of simulation, role-playing and strategy games": the player was a 17th-century privateer captain, raiding treasure ships on the Spanish Main. In Britain it was billed as "the world's first swashbuckling simulation" and cost £14.95 on the Commodore 64.

It was also the first game with Meier's name in its title. According to *Retro Gamer*, Stealey "was adamant that Meier's name be on the box". Meier was less sure about it. "My name on the box is not a guarantee," he told *Computer Gaming World* in 2001. "I still have to write good games."

## Railroad Tycoon and Civilization

His next designs moved further from combat. He often started a game, put it away and came back to it. *Railroad Tycoon* (1990), made with Bruce Shelley, "started off as a model railroad game," Meier said. *Civilization* began as "a *SimCity* type of game, with zones for farms and cities", until he felt he "was playing the game with gloves on"; he shelved it, made the spy game *Covert Action* (1990), and returned to it. Even halfway through he was not sure it would work: "In the back of my mind, I was going, 'You're a geek and no one will understand this.'"

*Civilization* came out in late 1991 and became the game he is known for; its own entry tells that story. Its success was not built on research. "We have a saying round here," Meier said, "which is 'We do the research when the game is finished'," because he did not want to fill a game with obscure facts.

## Firaxis

The 1990s were hard for MicroProse. Spectrum HoloByte took it over in 1993, and in 1996, amid layoffs, Meier left with Brian Reynolds and Jeff Briggs to found Firaxis Games in Maryland. He told *Computer Gaming World* he had "gotten my name back": MicroProse could use *Sid Meier's Civilization* only for games made while he was there.

Firaxis kept making the kind of game he had made at MicroProse. *Sid Meier's Gettysburg!* (1997) was a Civil War battle game, and *Sid Meier's Alpha Centauri* (1999), published by Electronic Arts, began where *Civilization*'s space race ended. When Infogrames bought MicroProse's owner, the *Civilization* series itself came to Firaxis, starting with *Civilization III* (2001). Meier remade *Pirates!* in 2004.

At Firaxis he worked more and more through other designers. He told *Edge* in 2006 that he encouraged people to contribute ideas, "because you never know where the next good one's going to come from", and that working with Bruce Shelley, Brian Reynolds and Soren Johnson let them "really blossom as designers". *[XCOM: Enemy Unknown](/vault/games/xcom-enemy-unknown)* (2012), Firaxis's remake of Julian Gollop's *[X-COM](/vault/games/x-com-ufo-defense)*, was "really his design and his vision", he said of its lead designer, Jake Solomon; his own involvement was "fairly limited". He also prototyped games that never came out, including a dinosaur game that went through three versions over a month or two each and "just wasn't as cool as dinosaurs needed to be".

He went on making smaller games of his own, such as *Sid Meier's Ace Patrol* (2013) and *Sid Meier's Starships* (2015), and in September 2020 W. W. Norton published *Sid Meier's Memoir!: A Life in Computer Games*.

## Honours

In 1999 Meier was the second person inducted into the Academy of Interactive Arts and Sciences' Hall of Fame, after Shigeru Miyamoto. He received a Lifetime Achievement Award at the Game Developers Conference in 2008.
