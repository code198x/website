## Overview

Mike Singleton wrote *Lords of Midnight* (1984) and its sequel *Doomdark's Revenge* for the 48K Spectrum, and went on to *Midwinter* and *Starlord* on 16-bit machines. He is the clearest British example of a programmer whose reputation rests on a **technique**, not on a genre or a company.


## How he got there
*Sinclair User* profiled him in August 1985 in a feature about making a living
from programming, and the route is not the one the *Lords of Midnight*
reputation implies.

He came to computing in 1980 and spent six months on it before deciding to learn
**6502 machine code**. His first game, *Space Ace*, went to a software house
called Petsoft — already defunct by the time the article ran — and **sold about
a hundred copies**. His verdict on that:

> The royalties in those days were pathetic, you couldn't last a week on them.

Games for various publishers followed, the most successful being *Snake Pit* for
Postern, written on a PET and later converted to the Spectrum. The break came in
**1983**, when [Beyond](/vault/companies/beyond-software) approached him. Nine
months later *Lords of Midnight* shipped, and
[*Doomdark's Revenge*](/vault/games/doomdarks-revenge) followed.

*Sinclair User* notes that after *Space Ace* he never had to approach a
publisher again — they came to him. His advice to programmers, in a feature
where most of the other interviewees were discouraging, was about which side of
the deal to take:

> If you've got faith in your own abilities, go for royalties rather than a
> salary.

Coming from someone whose first game sold a hundred copies, that is a
recommendation with the risk fully understood.

## Landscaping

*Sinclair User* reviewed *Lords of Midnight* in September 1984 and described the method precisely enough to be useful forty years later:

> The graphics are built of **standard pictures for various parts of the landscape**; it is claimed that there are **32,000 possible views**, all different in some respect from each other… the response time is excellent, with **pictures generated almost instantaneously**.

That is the whole idea. The game holds a map of what is where, plus a modest library of parts — mountain, forest, tower, keep — and when you look in a direction it **assembles** the view from the parts, sized and layered by distance. Turn around and it assembles a different one.

The alternative, storing 32,000 pictures, is not just impractical on a 48K machine; it is impossible by three orders of magnitude. What makes the trick work is that a landscape seen from a distance is highly compressible: you do not need a unique picture of every hill, only a rule for where hills are and how big one looks from here.

The consequence is a game that **feels far larger than the tape it came on** — one of the era's recurring effects, and here achieved deliberately, not by luck. Singleton called it landscaping. Most of what the term describes is now called [procedural generation](/vault/techniques/procedural-generation).

## Two games at once

The other Singleton habit is structural. *Sinclair User* noticed it immediately: *Lords of Midnight* contains **a quest and a wargame at the same time**, and the two interfere with each other.

Morkin has to carry the icecrown into the heart of Doomdark's realm. Meanwhile Luxor's armies have to hold Xajorkith. Pressure on either side moves the other — Doomdark's attention on Morkin is attention off the armies, and vice versa. It is one design decision that produces a strategic dilemma without any special-case code for it.

Character morale works the same way. Commanders have qualities, and *Sinclair User* records the effect: "if a commander is very frightened, he will be less likely to consider joining a battle". The player commands people who can decline.

## How the two were received
*Lords of Midnight* and its sequel drew sharply different verdicts, and the
difference is the point.

[*Doomdark's Revenge*](/vault/games/doomdarks-revenge) took **10/10** from
*CRASH* in February 1985 and 10/10 from *Personal Computer Games*, 9/10 from
*Sinclair User* and *Personal Computer News* — and **3/5** from *Your Computer*
and 7/10 from *C&VG*. Two perfect scores and a 3/5 within three months is not
reviewer error. It is a game that rewards players who already know the world and
gives newcomers almost nothing, which is exactly what a sequel built for the
people who finished the first one will do.

*CRASH* credited Singleton by name and wrote about Shareth the Heartstealer's
Icemark — detail that only lands if you played *Lords of Midnight* first.

*ACE* scored it 940/1000 in a December 1987 retrospective, its highest mark for
any of the Beyond games in this Vault. Retrospectives are written by exactly the
audience the game was built for.

## Why he matters

Singleton is the entry for **generating rather than storing**, the single most productive idea available to a programmer facing a hard memory limit. The lesson generalises past the 8-bit era: the reason a modern open world fits on a disc at all is a direct descendant of the reasoning behind those 32,000 views.

He is also a reminder of how much of the British scene was one person's work. *Lords of Midnight* is a world, a wargame, an adventure and a renderer, all for a machine with 48 kilobytes, by a former teacher working alone.
