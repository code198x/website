## Overview

John Twiddy programmed on the Commodore 64 through its best years and then moved to Amiga conversions of console games. What makes him worth an entry is that **the magazines treated his name as information** — *ZZAP!64* would tell readers that a game was his, in the expectation that this meant something to them.

## Fast facts

- **On the C64:** *Last Ninja 2*, *International Karate +*, *California Games* and others for [System 3](/vault/companies/system-3) and Epyx.
- **On the Amiga:** *Aladdin*, *Cool Spot*, *Global Gladiators* — console games brought across, credited in *Amiga Power* review headers, 1993–94.
- ***ZZAP!64***: "John Twiddy's programming keeping it all very slick to play."

## A programmer as a selling point

"John Twiddy's programming keeping it all very slick to play" is a small sentence doing an unusual job. It assumes the reader knows the name, and it uses **the programmer as evidence about the product** — the way a film review uses a director.

This was normal for a few years and then stopped. In 1987 a C64 game was one or two people's work and the gap between a good programmer and an average one showed in the first ten seconds of play, so magazines named names. By 1995 a game had a team of twenty and the same sentence would have been meaningless.

That shift is why the Vault's people list thins out after the 8-bit era, and why it is worth recording the individuals while the record still names them.

## Conversion, again

Twiddy's nineties work is the same craft the [Krisalis](/vault/companies/krisalis) and [Probe](/vault/companies/probe-software) entries describe, seen from the programmer's side.

*Aladdin*, *Cool Spot* and *Global Gladiators* are Mega Drive and SNES games. Bringing them to the Amiga meant reproducing the feel of hardware built for exactly this — hardware scrolling, big sprite budgets, a console's fixed frame timing — on a computer that had different strengths and a floppy drive. **Nobody gets credit for a conversion that works**; they only get blamed for one that does not, and the target is a game the reviewer has already played on better hardware.

Doing that repeatedly, well enough that a magazine still names you, is a real distinction.

## Why he matters

Twiddy worked in the brief window of **the programmer as a known quantity**, when British magazines credited individuals by name and readers followed them.

He also connects the two halves of the British industry's story. The same person is on *Last Ninja 2* in the C64's golden years and on an Amiga *Aladdin* in the machine's last ones, which is the whole decade in one credit line — and the only reason it is traceable is that two magazines happened to print his name.
