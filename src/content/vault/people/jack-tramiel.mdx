Jack Tramiel survived Auschwitz, emigrated to America, repaired typewriters, and built one of the most important computer companies in history. His philosophy—"computers for the masses, not the classes"—drove Commodore to make affordable machines that put computing power in millions of homes. His management style was brutal, his vision was clear, and his impact on the industry was immense.

## Fast facts
- **Born:** 1928 in Łódź, Poland, as Idek Tramielski.
- **Holocaust survivor:** interned in Auschwitz; family members perished. Emigrated to the US in 1947.
- **Commodore founded:** started as a typewriter repair shop in Toronto (1954), pivoted to calculators, then computers.
- **MOS acquisition (1976):** bought the chip manufacturer to secure supply and gain engineering talent.
- **The big hits:** PET (1977), VIC-20 (1980), [Commodore 64](/vault/systems/commodore-64) (1982).
- **Departure (1984):** resigned from [Commodore](/vault/companies/commodore) after boardroom conflicts; immediately took over [Atari](/vault/companies/atari)'s consumer division.
- **Passed away:** April 2012, at age 83.

## Typewriters, in his own words

The pre-Commodore years are usually compressed to a line — survived the camps, repaired
typewriters, started a company. *The Story of the Commodore 64 in Pixels* carries transcripts
from one of the last interviews Tramiel gave, and he tells it as a sequence of decisions rather
than a rise.

The typewriters were not incidental. The wartime US Army ran on them, to the point that it
became illegal for a civilian to buy one, and the Army put Tramiel to work repairing them in
Manhattan, with training at IBM. That is where the trade came from.

The first move was his own. Sent to negotiate on his employer's behalf, he came back with a
contract to repair **2,000 typewriters** and his boss, Ruby, was "in heaven" — and gave him
nothing:

> I waited a month, six weeks, and I was still drawing fifty dollars a week. So it came Friday
> and I told Ruby, "I'm quitting." [...] "The reason why is because you are very dumb. A person
> who brings you that kind of a contract, you should definitely reward them, which you didn't."

He left, and in the early 1950s, from an office in the Bronx, made a deal with a Czechoslovak
company to sell their typewriters in North America. When cheaper Japanese imports made that
unprofitable he moved to adding machines — and then bought the Berlin manufacturer outright,
2,000 employees, because "to me that was an extension of the typewriter".

⚠ These are Tramiel's recollections late in life, reported in a modern book. They are the best
first-person account available and they are still testimony.

## What the camps left

Tramiel spent five years in Nazi camps, Auschwitz among them, and the book is direct about how
that shaped the man rather than treating it as background. It gave him "a yearning for
freedom", and it also left him with a habit: he was used to rising at five, so army reveille
held no terror.

His own summary of what he took from the years after is the clearest statement of the
philosophy that later produced "computers for the masses, not the classes":

> When I was in the army I learned who the Americans are and I came up with the idea that if
> you work hard, you can do whatever your ability is.

And the ambition behind it, in a metaphor worth keeping:

> I could see that people are being trained to follow but not to lead — I believe there was a
> shortage of conductors but there were many musicians, and it's important to assemble a good
> orchestra and then to know how to conduct, and I felt I can do that.

## The calculator wars
In the early 1970s, Texas Instruments threatened to destroy Commodore:
- TI made both calculators *and* calculator chips
- When TI entered the retail market, they could undercut anyone
- Tramiel's response: buy [MOS Technology](/vault/companies/mos-technology), become vertically integrated

This move—controlling chip fabrication—became Commodore's defining competitive advantage.

## Building the empire
Tramiel's Commodore operated on a simple principle: undercut everyone.
- **PET (1977):** the first all-in-one personal computer, targeting business and education.
- **VIC-20 (1980):** the first computer to sell a million units, priced under $300.
- **Commodore 64 (1982):** launched at $595, soon dropped to $200. Competitors couldn't touch it.

Tramiel's willingness to slash prices—even cannibalising his own products—terrified rivals. Texas Instruments exited the home computer market. Atari bled money. Only Apple, protected by different market positioning, survived intact.

## Management style
Tramiel was infamous for:
- **"Business is war":** his stated philosophy toward competition.
- **Demanding intensity:** long hours, aggressive targets, high turnover.
- **Cost obsession:** every penny mattered; engineers learned to design cheap.
- **Loyalty and betrayal:** promoted fiercely, fired faster.

The engineers who thrived under Tramiel created remarkable machines. Many burned out. The culture produced results but left casualties.

## The Atari years
When Tramiel left Commodore in 1984:
- Bought Atari's consumer division from Warner for a bargain price
- Launched the Atari ST (1985), a 16-bit computer competing with the Amiga
- Ironically, Commodore had just acquired Amiga Corporation—partly to spite Tramiel

The ST found success in Europe, particularly for MIDI music production, but never achieved Commodore 64-level dominance.

## Legacy
Tramiel proved that computers didn't have to be expensive. His price wars made computing accessible to ordinary families, launching careers and creating communities. The methods were harsh, the results were transformative. Without Tramiel's Commodore, the 8-bit era would have looked very different.
