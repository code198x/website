Demonstrating *Skyrim* to *Edge* in 2011, Todd Howard reached a barrow in the mountains just as a dragon swooped down and landed between him and the door. The encounter was not scripted. Howard explained that the game's story system decided when a dragon should appear, and told the magazine about the time a designer added three dragons flying past a town as a piece of scenery: "They saw me and it was party time. I was like: 'Who did this?!'" He ran for the mountains to lose them, and found it "really terrifying". "Though I did make the designer take it out. Three dragons is too many." Howard has spent most of his working life on games like that: worlds big enough for things to happen in them that nobody planned.

## Fast facts

- **Company:** [Bethesda Softworks](/vault/companies/bethesda) from the mid-1990s; head of its development team, [Bethesda Game Studios](/vault/companies/bethesda-game-studios).
- **Early work:** *The Terminator: Future Shock* (1995).
- **Project leader:** *[The Elder Scrolls III: Morrowind](/vault/games/morrowind)* (2002).
- **Executive producer:** *The Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion* (2006) and *Fallout 3* (2008).
- **Director:** *The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim* (2011), *Fallout 4* (2015) and *Starfield* (2023).

## Joining Bethesda

Howard has said that the role-playing games *[Wizardry](/vault/games/wizardry)* and *Ultima III: Exodus* inspired him, and Wikipedia's account of his career says that Bethesda Softworks, the Maryland company behind *The Elder Scrolls*, hired him in 1994 after he kept asking it for a job. His first projects were Bethesda's *Terminator* games, and he later traced his studio's working method back to the first of them. Bethesda builds its places from kits of prefabricated pieces, he told *Edge* in 2011: "The paradigm for how we build our worlds has not changed since *Terminator: Future Shock*. We've just got better at it."

## Morrowind

Howard was project leader on *[Morrowind](/vault/games/morrowind)*, the third *Elder Scrolls* game, and he was blunt about its predecessor. "We didn't really like the result of *Daggerfall*," he told *Computer Gaming World* in 2001. "There were about 20 to 30 hours of good gameplay, but then it began to just be the same thing over and over again. We went into *Morrowind* thinking that less is definitely more." *Daggerfall* had generated its world at random; on Vvardenfell every place and every character was made by hand. To build so much by hand the team needed tools, and Howard told the German magazine *PC Player* that it had decided early on to make "an actual role-playing operating system, instead of simply a game". The editor, the Construction Set, shipped with the game so that players could do the same.

## Oblivion and Fallout 3

For *The Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion* (2006), made for Microsoft's new Xbox 360 as well as the PC, Howard was executive producer, and he wanted to keep more players with it. "Everybody likes the size of our games, but a lot of players were lost and confused," he told *Electronic Gaming Monthly* in 2005. "With Oblivion, we're still offering a massive world, but we're going to hold players' hands a little more." He also looked forward to selling extras through Xbox Live: "One of the first mods we're offering is horse armor." And he admitted that the world's characters could surprise even him. "There is a unicorn in the game, and the other day when I was playing I found it dead. The Minotaur killed it; he's supposed to protect it, but his AI is just too aggro."

Bethesda then took on *[Fallout](/vault/games/fallout)*. Howard, executive producer of *Fallout 3*, told *Hyper* in 2005 that the first two games had "really let me choose to play a certain character, and the level of immersion was outstanding. I was that guy on the screen, wandering the wastelands trying to survive." To fans who feared a console *Fallout* would be shallow, he answered: "Anyone who says a console game can't have depth hasn't played enough of them. The platform is 100% irrelevant." *Fallout 3* came out in 2008.

## Skyrim

Howard directed *The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim*, released on 11 November 2011. "We rewrote the entire renderer," he told *Edge* during its preview. "We have full shadows now. We rewrote the pathing, the AI systems, the quest systems, the dialogue, the interface and the animation system." The landscape was drawn by hand rather than generated: "Procedural generation was a big thing in 2004 and 2005. As our tools got better we actually backed off that in certain areas and added it to others. So now, none of the landscape is procedural — it's all created by our artists." The quests went the other way, borrowing from *Daggerfall* the idea of procedural elements.

He also cut the character system down, from eight attributes and 21 skills in *Oblivion* to three attributes and 18 skills, and defended the change when players complained of "dumbing down": "A fan may say 'You removed my eight attributes!', and my answer is, 'Which ones do you want? They're all a trickle down to something else'." *Edge* gave *Skyrim* nine out of ten, printing Howard's account of its making beside the review, and summed up the bargain his games strike: the developer builds "worlds of unrivalled scope, rich in lore and history", and players do all they can "to ignore the rough edges that come, apparently, as the cost of such ambition".

## Fallout 4 to Starfield

*Fallout 4* followed in 2015 and the online *Fallout 76* in 2018, whose launch *Wireframe* covered for its bugs. Bethesda, with its parent ZeniMax, became part of [Microsoft](/vault/companies/microsoft) in 2021. In 2019 Howard said that the next *Elder Scrolls* game "has to last ten years". Before *Starfield* came out in September 2023, *Wireframe* wrote that its success would rest on whether "studio director Todd Howard can live up to his promise of making over 1000 planets uniquely diverse". It was Bethesda's first new series in more than 25 years.
