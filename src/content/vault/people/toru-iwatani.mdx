In 1983 an American magazine could still treat *Pac-Man*'s designer as a piece of trivia. "Trivia wizards will know that the man who invented Pac-Man is Toro [sic] Iwatani, an employee of Tokyo-based Namco," *Electronic Fun* told its readers, and it hoped Namco would "soon send Iwatani to the U.S. so we can meet and get to know the man who made Pac-Man". By then there were 100,000 *Pac-Man* machines in American arcades. Iwatani had set out to make something that women and couples would enjoy in arcades full of shooting games, and he built it around a single word: eat.

## Fast facts

- **Born:** 1955, in Tokyo.
- **Namco:** joined in 1977; left in 2007.
- **First games:** the video pinball games *Gee Bee* (1978), *Bomb Bee* and *Cutie Q*.
- **Best known for:** [Pac-Man](/vault/games/pac-man) (1980).
- **Later games:** *Libble Rabble* (1983), *Pac-Land* (1984) and *Pac-Man Championship Edition*, among others.
- **Teaching:** professor in the game department of Tokyo Polytechnic University's Faculty of Arts, from 2007.

## Video pinball

Iwatani joined [Namco](/vault/companies/namco) in 1977, shortly after leaving college. *Edge* reported that he wanted to design pinball tables, but Namco had moved "full steam into videogames", so he compromised. His first games were video pinball: *Gee Bee* in 1978, then *Bomb Bee* and *Cutie Q*. *Wireframe* describes *Gee Bee* as the first game Namco developed internally, "a creative mix of Breakout and pinball". *Cutie Q* already had the Japanese *kawaii*, or cute, style of drawing that he would use again.

Namco's president, Masaya Nakamura, had his own ideas about who made good game designers. "For designing games, the knowledge acquired in school is not helpful," he told the American children's magazine *Microkids* in 1983. "I want people who think in unusual ways, whose curiosity runs away with them." The magazine held up Iwatani as proof that it worked.

## Pac-Man

In 1979 Iwatani decided to make what *Edge* called a "real" videogame. "Most arcade videogames of the time were violent and focused on the male player, so the game centres became places frequented mainly by men," he said in 2010. "We decided to change that demographic by designing games that could appeal to women and thus to couples, therefore making game centres desirable places to go on a date." "When I imagined what women enjoy, the image of them eating cakes and desserts came to mind so I used 'eating' as a keyword."

The shape came at lunch. "As I was eating pizza, I took a slice away and looked at what was left," he told *Edge* in 1998. The name came from *paku-paku*, Japanese slang for a mouth opening and closing as it eats. The rest followed from the eating: "After that, I decided to add the element of four ghosts as enemies. Just moving around an open field would be too much freedom and no fun, so I set up a maze environment." He made the whole game work from one joystick, so that "even girls who hadn't played many arcade games could play and enjoy" it.

He kept the hero as plain as he could, "without any features such as eyes or limbs", so that each player could imagine him. The ghosts mattered as much. "All of the ghosts have different AI algorithms that reflect their comical personalities," he said. "It's sort of like asking whether Tom or Jerry is the star of the Tom and Jerry cartoons." If every ghost had chased Pac-Man the same way, they "would look like a string of beads. Where's the thrill in that?" The power pellet, which lets Pac-Man turn and eat the ghosts, was not in the plan. "The inspiration for the power pellet was the spinach in the TV cartoon Popeye," he told *Retro Gamer*. "The power pellets didn't exist in the planning stages at all — they emerged during the development stage." [Ghost AI](/vault/techniques/ghost-ai) explains how the four ghosts choose their paths.

*Pac-Man* reached Japanese arcades in 1980, as *Puck-Man*. "In Japan, the game met with a lukewarm reception at first, but it then proved to be a long seller over several years," Iwatani said. At the American trade show that November, the trade expected Namco's *Rally-X* to be the hit. "Pac-Man was designed for people to play with ease and when relaxed without 'excitement'," he said. "I myself could not imagine that it would be loved by so many people and be such an international hit." The [Pac-Man](/vault/games/pac-man) entry tells the story of its success.

## The $3,500 story

In *Game Over* (1993), David Sheff wrote that Nakamura "awarded the engineer who came up with the game a piddling $3,500", and that the engineer, "disgusted", left the video-game business. Sheff did not name him, and the second half of the story does not match Iwatani's career: he stayed at Namco for more than 25 years after *Pac-Man*.

## After Pac-Man

His next big idea was harder to play. *Libble Rabble*, in 1983, gave the player two joysticks, each steering one end of an elastic line to wrap around enemies. It was unpopular "because it was so hard to play", he later admitted, but in the mid-1980s he still called it his best game. *Wireframe* also credits him with Namco's racing game *[Pole Position](/vault/games/pole-position)*. He worked on *Pac-Land* (1984), in which Pac-Man gained arms and legs and ran through a sideways-scrolling world, and on *Pac-Mania* (1987). *Pac-Land* was his favourite: "It pioneered action videogames in which the scene flows horizontally," he told *Retro Gamer*, adding that Shigeru Miyamoto had said *Super Mario Bros.* was influenced by it. *Retro Gamer* also lists *Ridge Racer* and *[Time Crisis](/vault/games/time-crisis)* among the games he worked on in later years.

In 2006 he went back to his own maze. *Pac-Man Championship Edition*, for the Xbox 360's download service, split the maze in half and turned the game into a race against the clock, with scores ranked online. "My aim with the game was to achieve legitimate evolution, with simplicity of game design and absence of the superfluous as my guiding principles," he said, "because, in my opinion, few players look for complexity in gameplay." Asked what he would change in the original, he said nothing: "Pac-Man is complete to such an extent that to add or to subtract anything at all would be unacceptable."

## Teaching

In 2007 Iwatani left Namco and became a professor at Tokyo Polytechnic University, in its Faculty of Arts, where a game department opened in 2010. In 2015 the Computer Entertainment Developers Conference (CEDEC) in Japan gave him a special award for *Pac-Man*, and in April 2016 he became president of the Digital Games Research Association of Japan. In 2005 Guinness World Records recognised *Pac-Man* as the most successful coin-operated arcade game, and in 2010 it recognised Iwatani as its creator. He also made a brief appearance in the 2015 film *Pixels*.
