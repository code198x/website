In the mid-1980s, Sega's arcade games were built to be seen from across the room. You sat astride a motorbike and leaned it into the bends, or strapped into a seat that rocked and swayed as you flew, or drove a red Ferrari in a cabinet that moved with the road. Yu Suzuki directed most of them. He started at Sega as a programmer, and the hardware grew around his games: "by and large, we were making hardware for the express purpose of the games I and everyone else at Sega were working on," he told Gamasutra in 2011. In the 1990s he took Sega into polygon 3D with *Virtua Racing* and *[Virtua Fighter](/vault/games/virtua-fighter)*, and then spent much of his later career on one very long story, *[Shenmue](/vault/games/shenmue)*.

## Fast facts

- **Born:** 1958, in Iwate Prefecture, Japan. Published sources disagree on the day.
- **Joined Sega:** 1983, as a programmer.
- **1980s arcade games:** *[Hang-On](/vault/games/hang-on)* (1985), *[Space Harrier](/vault/games/space-harrier)* (1985), *[Out Run](/vault/games/out-run)* (1986), *[After Burner](/vault/games/after-burner)* (1987).
- **1990s:** head of the division that became [Sega AM2](/vault/companies/sega-am2); *Virtua Racing* (1992), *Virtua Fighter* (1993), *[Daytona USA](/vault/games/daytona-usa)*.
- **Later work:** *Shenmue* (1999) and *Shenmue II* (2001) for the [Dreamcast](/vault/systems/sega-dreamcast); *OutRun 2* (2003) as producer; his own studio, Ys Net, from 2008; *Shenmue III* (2019).

## A programmer at Sega

Suzuki was in his mid-twenties when he joined [Sega](/vault/companies/sega) in 1983. A Brazilian profile in 2000 said he had studied science in Okayama. His first game was *Champion Boxing* for Sega's first home console, the SG-1000. *Wireframe* reported that his managers liked it so much that they turned it into an arcade machine "without changing a line of code".

## Taikan: games you sit on

*Hang-On* (1985) was the first of what Sega called *taikan*, or "body sensation", games. The player sat on a replica motorbike and tilted it left and right to steer. Its board used two 16-bit processors to scale the pictures of the road, the riders and the scenery, so they grew smoothly as they came towards you. Sega called the technique "Super Scaler": it drew a convincing 3D world without a single polygon.

*Space Harrier* (1985) put the player in a seat that rocked and shifted with the movements on screen. It began as a flying game with a helicopter, then a Harrier jump jet. Suzuki found the aircraft too hard to animate, replaced it with a running and flying figure carrying a cannon, and moved the game to a fantasy world. Sega was wary of 3D shooting games, which had not made money before, and according to his programmer Satoshi Mifune, Suzuki offered to give up his salary if *Space Harrier* failed.

*Out Run* (1986) came next. Its producer, Yoji Ishii, said that Suzuki directed it and "deserves credit", and that while it was being made the two of them drove around Europe: Sega's president, Hayao Nakayama, had vetoed a drive across America as too dangerous. *After Burner* (1987), inspired by the film *Top Gun*, put the player in an enclosed, pod-like cabinet.

Few homes could offer any of this. Suzuki's games came to Britain as home computer conversions, and US Gold's versions of *Out Run* for the Spectrum, Commodore 64 and Amstrad drew very mixed reviews in 1988. Sega sold its own, more faithful versions for the [Master System](/vault/systems/sega-master-system).

Suzuki worked fast, often sleeping on a fold-out bed under his desk. He wanted realism. "Making more and more realistic games is the direction I want to go in," he said in a 1988 interview. "Games aren't reality, of course, so you do need a lot of tricks and techniques to bring a sense of realism to a game." In the same interview came a complaint every developer would recognise: "If only I could create things without having to worry about the cost."

## Polygons: AM2 in the 1990s

The team Suzuki led became Sega's AM2 division. *Virtua Racing* (1992) was Sega's first polygon 3D game, running on its new Model 1 arcade hardware. *Wireframe* noted that it was not the first 3D racing game, but it was the first to feel as fast and thrilling as the sprite-based ones. *Virtua Fighter* (1993) asked whether fully 3D human fighters could compete with the sprite-based *Street Fighter* games. When the [Saturn](/vault/systems/sega-saturn) went on sale in Japan in November 1994, *Edge* found *Virtua Fighter* the only worthwhile game available and called the home version "a near-perfect conversion". *Daytona USA* followed on Sega's Model 2 board, and Suzuki produced *Virtua Cop* and *Virtua Fighter 2*.

## Shenmue

In 1986 Suzuki had told Japan's *Beep!* magazine that after *Hang-On* he wanted to make "a 3D adventure or role-playing-style game", but that it "would have taken too long to develop". A decade later he began one for the Saturn, first built around the *Virtua Fighter* characters. It moved to the Dreamcast and became *Shenmue*: an open 3D town with a large cast of characters living their own routines, and a revenge story told over dozens of hours. Around 300 people worked on it. "At first we said it would all fit one disc maybe, then two discs, then three," Suzuki recalled in 2000. *Edge* called it "one of the most expensive and graphically impressive videogaming projects to be undertaken"; *Wireframe* reported a figure of $47 million.

*Shenmue* came out in 1999 and was a critical success, but it could not save the Dreamcast. Sega announced in January 2001 that it would stop making the console, and *Shenmue II* followed that September. When Sega explained its new strategy as a software publisher in June 2001, Suzuki was one of the studio heads on the stage. Several of his projects in the 2000s were cancelled, among them *Shenmue Online* and an arcade fighting game, *Psy-Phi*. He returned to the road as producer of *OutRun 2* (2003).

## Ys Net and Shenmue III

Suzuki founded his own company, Ys Net, in 2008, and later left Sega. In 2010 *Edge* reported that Ys Net was working on *Shenmue City*, a PC and mobile game for Japan set in the first game's Yokosuka. The real sequel was announced in June 2015 with a Kickstarter campaign, and fans who had waited since 2001 paid for it. *Shenmue III*, developed by Ys Net and published by Deep Silver, came out for PC and PlayStation 4 on 19 November 2019. It picked up where the second game had stopped, with Ryo Hazuki arriving in rural China. *Wireframe* found that it "looks and feels like a continuation of the Dreamcast series, warts and all". In 2020 Suzuki talked of a fourth *Shenmue* that would appeal to "a broader audience".
