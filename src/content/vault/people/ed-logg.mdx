Ed Logg put his name inside his games where players would never see it. The program for *Millipede* carries the words "PROGRAMMED BY ED LOGG" in Morse code, and "MILLIPEDE BY ED LOGG" in the six-bit character code of Control Data computers, the machines he had worked on before Atari. Between 1978 and 1985 he wrote or co-wrote *Super Breakout*, *[Asteroids](/vault/games/asteroids)*, *[Centipede](/vault/games/centipede)*, *Millipede* and *[Gauntlet](/vault/games/gauntlet)*, and he kept making games with the same group of Atari engineers until the studio closed in 2003. His business card, he told *Retro Gamer*, read "Super Duper Game Guy".

## Fast facts

- **Born:** 1948.
- **Employers:** [Atari](/vault/companies/atari)'s coin-op division from 1978, which became [Atari Games](/vault/companies/atari-games) in 1984 and Midway Games West in 1999; the studio closed in 2003.
- **Arcade games:** *Super Breakout* and *Video Pinball* (1978), *Asteroids* (1979), *Centipede* (with Dona Bailey, copyright 1980), *Millipede* (1982), *Gauntlet* (1985), *Gauntlet II* (1986), *Xybots* (with Bob Flanagan, 1987), *Steel Talons* (with Ed Rotberg) and *Space Lords*.
- **Home games:** *Othello* for the Atari 2600; NES versions of *Centipede*, *Millipede* and *Tetris* for Tengen; later *San Francisco Rush* conversions, *Wayne Gretzky's 3D Hockey* for the Nintendo 64 and *Dr Muto*.
- **Award:** the Academy of Interactive Arts & Sciences' Pioneer Award, 2012.

## Before Atari

Logg spent two years as a graduate student in mathematics at Stanford, where he played *Spacewar!* on a pair of linked PDP computers in the student coffee house. By his own account he lost most of his games. Teaching did not appeal ("I just liked programming more"), so he joined Control Data Corporation, where, he admitted, he converted the FORTRAN versions of *Adventure* and *Star Trek* to run on other machines in his spare time. In 1977 he built a computer at home and wrote a version of *Sea Wolf* for it. A former colleague who had gone to Atari told him the company paid people to write games. The home computer and *Sea Wolf* impressed the interviewers, and in 1978 he joined the coin-op division.

## Super Breakout and Asteroids

His first project, a motorbike game called *Dirt Bike* taken over from Dennis Koble, failed its field test. Programs then went off to be typed in and took about an hour to assemble, so Logg used the waiting time to start something nobody had asked for. Owen Rubin had told him of Nolan Bushnell's wish for an update to *Breakout*, and Logg wrote it on the hardware he already had. "The best part was that it was not approved by management," he told *Digital Press*. "So they first saw it when it was running and everyone was already having fun playing it." The listing of *Super Breakout* records the start date as 1 April 1978. Six variations went to field test; three survived, and the one players liked best was *Progressive*, in which the wall of bricks descended on the bat. *Video Pinball*, begun on 7 June 1978, followed, with a nudge control for thumping the table. He also worked on the lettering for Atari's first vector game, *Lunar Lander*.

In 1979 Lyle Rains asked him to make a game about shooting rocks, and Logg insisted it should be drawn with vectors. The [Asteroids](/vault/games/asteroids) entry tells that story: the saucers, the high score table he borrowed from Exidy's *Star Fire*, and the change to the small saucer's timing that let players "lurk" for days on a single quarter. It became Atari's best-selling coin-op, with more than 70,000 machines built. Logg's first wife had her own *Asteroids* machine before they met. "So naturally, she loved the creator," he said.

Logg did not take part in the sequel, *Asteroids Deluxe*, because Atari had promoted him to supervisor. He hated it. "I didn't like the hiring and firing and all the people issues. The bottom line was, I wasn't doing games, so I quit after a year and a half," he said. He also wrote *Othello* for the Atari 2600, using the display code Carol Shaw had written for *Checkers*; Atari listed it among its new VCS cartridges at the start of 1981.

## Centipede and Millipede

*[Centipede](/vault/games/centipede)* came out of Atari's book of game ideas, where a one-line entry described "a multi-segmented creature" that "breaks into pieces when shot". [Dona Bailey](/vault/people/dona-bailey), a programmer who had left General Motors for Atari, took it on as her first project, with Logg as project director. The program lists both of them as programmers and gives the start date as 19 July 1980. Bailey told *Blip* that she got a lot of help from Logg, and that it was her idea to use a trackball after trying buttons and a joystick. Logg gave her the credit for the game's audience: "*Centipede* was definitely aimed at the women's market. I'm not sure that without Dona's viewpoint it ever would have made it there." *Centipede* also kept its high scores after the power went off. "By *Centipede*, we'd got battery backup to keep scores in memory," Logg said, though the game's own documentation shows the scores were kept in an EAROM, a memory chip that holds its contents without a battery; *Asteroids* had forgotten them.

Logg wrote the sequel himself. The *Millipede* listing, begun on 1 April 1982, names him as both programmer and project leader. "I wanted to fix some things I hadn't done in the first one," he said: expert players could start at a higher level, and up to eight spiders could be on the screen at once, which "got them off the machine quicker".

Not everything worked. His laserdisc game *Road Runner* failed its field tests, and marketing sent him suggestions for saving it. He quoted one of his own rules, "Ed Logg's Laws": "What you get from the first test is pretty much what you're gonna get." When he was asked to move it to a different board, he told them to get someone else.

## Gauntlet

When Warner split Atari in 1984, the coin-op division survived and went to Namco. "Hell, the Tramiels could have killed us there and then," Logg said, "but Time Warner owed money to Namco and ended up offering our division to them." At the renamed [Atari Games](/vault/companies/atari-games) he designed *[Gauntlet](/vault/games/gauntlet)* in 1985, a dungeon game for four players at once, drawing on *Dungeons & Dragons* and the Atari 800 game *Dandy*. The engineer Pat McCarthy designed a board for it that could change moving objects on the fly. Atari took out five patents on the game, four in Logg's name; the company made him sign his rights over for $1, and he framed the dollar the lawyer threw at him.

*Gauntlet II* followed in 1986. A third *Gauntlet*, *Catacombs*, was to have gone into three dimensions until marketing wanted no more *Gauntlet* games. Logg and his co-programmer Bob Flanagan gave it a new theme, and it came out in 1987 as *Xybots*, with a joystick that twisted to turn the player's view. Players struggled with it. "It became increasingly clear in our field tests that many people didn't know that the controller rotation would actually change their viewpoint," Logg said. "I wish I'd stuck to my guns and kept it as *Catacombs*."

For *Steel Talons*, a helicopter game, he worked with Ed Rotberg of *Battlezone*. He wanted players to feel they had been hit, so the team put a pinball "thumper" under the seat; the engineer put more current through it than planned, and "it was like someone was hitting the seat with a hammer". Logg's initials headed the default high score table in its hardest mode. "I just put myself at the top of the high scores. Just a little vanity, okay!"

## Consoles

From the mid-1980s Logg also wrote NES games for Atari Games' home label, Tengen, among them conversions of *Centipede* and *Millipede* and a version of *[Tetris](/vault/games/tetris)* he wrote from scratch. Tengen's *Tetris* was withdrawn after Nintendo went to court over the rights (see [the Tetris legal battles](/vault/phenomena/tetris-legal-battles)). Tengen had also made its own copy of the NES lockout chip. "I was nothing to do with it," Logg said, "but I knew the games Nintendo were playing and didn't like it either." He still thought his *Tetris* the best: "Yeah, I'll take my version."

According to the Academy of Interactive Arts & Sciences, he spent 1993 and 1994 at Electronic Arts before returning to Atari Games to write console games. In the 1990s he converted the *San Francisco Rush* driving games for the [Nintendo 64](/vault/systems/nintendo-64) and later the Dreamcast, and worked on *Wayne Gretzky's 3D Hockey* for the Nintendo 64. *Edge* found him in December 2002 still working with "the same group of developers from the late '70s — now known as Midway Games West", finishing *Dr Muto*, a game about a mad scientist, for the PlayStation 2, Xbox and GameCube. He missed the old way of working, the magazine reported, when a prototype took one or two people a few weeks instead of 30 people a year and a half.

## Later

Midway closed the studio in 2003. Logg set up a small company to make games for mobile phones, three of which were published by GenPlay, and then turned to games for cable television. In 2006 he told *Retro Gamer* he was retiring shortly. In February 2012 the Academy of Interactive Arts & Sciences gave him its Pioneer Award at its Interactive Achievement Awards in Las Vegas, presented by Mark Cerny.
