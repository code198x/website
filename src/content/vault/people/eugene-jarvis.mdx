In 1982 *Joystik* magazine sat down with a 27-year-old who already had three hits to his name: *[Defender](/vault/games/defender)*, *Stargate* and *[Robotron: 2084](/vault/games/robotron-2084)*. Eugene Jarvis was, the magazine said, a rare thing, "both a creative game designer and a creative computer programmer". His games were fast, crowded and hard, and he meant them to be. "I'm an action player," he said. "I like to be aggressive. I don't like to be on the run." More than forty years later he was still making arcade machines, at a company he founded when most of the American arcade business had given up.

## Fast facts

- **Born:** 1955, in California.
- **Education:** electrical engineering and computer science at the University of California, Berkeley.
- **Companies:** Atari (pinball), [Williams Electronics](/vault/companies/williams-electronics), Vid Kidz, Williams and [Midway](/vault/companies/midway) again, Raw Thrills.
- **Key games:** *[Defender](/vault/games/defender)* (1981), *Stargate* (1981), *[Robotron: 2084](/vault/games/robotron-2084)* (1982), *Blaster* (1983), *NARC*, *Smash TV* (1990), the *Cruis'n* series.

## Pinball first

Jarvis planned to be a biochemist. "I wanted to cure, like, death!" he told *Retro Gamer*. At school he had learned FORTRAN on an IBM computer behind glass, and written a chess program. He studied electrical engineering and computer science at Berkeley instead, and in 1976 joined Atari's pinball division in California, next door to the video game department then busy with *Breakout*.

Pinball tables had become computers too, and Jarvis wrote their programs. At Atari he worked on *The Atarians*, *Time 2000* and *Airborne Avenger*, the last with the designer Steve Ritchie, and learned about electronic sound synthesis on the way. When Atari's pinball division closed he moved to Williams, the Chicago pinball maker, where his tables included *Firepower*.

## Defender

Williams was thinking about video games, and Jarvis, who had decided after playing *[Space Invaders](/vault/games/space-invaders)* that he wanted to make them, pushed to be given one. The result, *[Defender](/vault/games/defender)*, is told in full in its own entry. It was Steve Ritchie who suggested flying over a planet; Jarvis turned that into a ship flying left and right over a scrolling world. Sam Dicker, whom Jarvis had hired, made the explosions that scattered every pixel of a destroyed ship across the screen, and Larry DeMar wrote the attract mode on the night before the game's first show. *Joystik* called it "the biggest success of 1981".

Success brought growth, and Jarvis did not like what came with it. In his telling, Williams decided that if two people could produce a hit, forty could produce more, and the bureaucracy that followed drove him out. He and DeMar left to form their own company, Vid Kidz, in Chicago. Its games were built and sold by Williams.

## Vid Kidz

Williams wanted a *Defender* sequel within four months, and Vid Kidz wrote it under contract. To get the most from a $50,000 Motorola development system, Jarvis programmed by day and DeMar by night. *Stargate* added an "Inviso" button and a Stargate that let expert players carrying rescued humanoids skip whole levels. Jarvis thought it "the better game". Williams later renamed it *Defender II* after a trademark dispute.

*[Robotron: 2084](/vault/games/robotron-2084)* came next, in 1982. Jarvis described the aim as taking *Space Invaders* "into two dimensions": one screen, the player in the middle and enemies pouring in from every side. The controls came partly from frustration. He loved Stern's *[Berzerk](/vault/games/berzerk)* but hated standing still to shoot, and after a car accident that shattered his right hand he found the game almost impossible to play. The answer was two joysticks, one to move and one to fire. According to Jarvis, a 1 MHz graphics chip and "very clever machine code and micro code" let the game handle about 150 enemies at once, while the sound came from a single channel shared out by priority. "You cannot play a casual game of *Robotron*," he said. "It demands your everything."

The last Vid Kidz game, *Blaster* (1983), tried to fake 3D on *Robotron*'s hardware with scaled images drawn by hand, as many as 32 sizes of each object. Only about 1,000 cabinets were made, and the home version died with Atari's collapse.

Through 1982 Jarvis also wrote a coin-op review column, "Coin-Op Shop", for the American magazine *Video Games*, which introduced him as "everyone's favorite game designer". He was not gentle. When he wrote that Namco's *Dig Dug* resembled *[Pac-Man](/vault/games/pac-man)*, a reader wrote in to argue.

## Back at Williams

The industry crash of the mid-1980s took Jarvis out of games for several years, which he spent studying business and computer science at Stanford. Williams brought him back in 1987. There he made *NARC*, an anti-drug shoot-'em-up with digitised graphics that *Sinclair User* singled out as the one surprise among the video games at that year's American operators' show. Jarvis described it as "Rambo in the Hood". "Why shouldn't games be based on politics?" he said later. "I did have really strong feelings regarding the devastating effect that drugs were having on society."

*Smash TV* (1990), made with the programmer Mark Turmell, was *Robotron*'s twin-stick play wrapped in a satire of television game shows, with borrowings from *RoboCop* and *The Running Man*. It was Turmell's first coin-op game and the artist [John Tobias](/vault/people/john-tobias)'s first video game. The first test, in a Chicago arcade, had only three waves finished. "The players mobbed it right away," Jarvis recalled; two players reached the third wave, which never ended, and the team went out for pizza rather than be there when they found out. The finished game promised hidden "Pleasure Domes" for players who collected enough keys. The team had run out of time to build them, and the first release had none. "It was a total scam to keep players going for some impossible goal," Jarvis admitted. About six months later a new release added them.

In the 1990s he moved into 3D and driving. *Cruis'n USA* (1994), a coast-to-coast race across the United States, came from Williams (WMS Industries), with a version promised for Nintendo's Ultra 64 console; *Cruis'n World* and *Cruis'n Exotica* followed. *Exotica* was his last game for Midway, by then part of the Williams group.

## Raw Thrills

In 2000, with things at Midway "intolerable" in his word, younger colleagues persuaded Jarvis to start again. His new company, Raw Thrills, kept making arcade machines when most American companies had stopped. Its games included the gun game *Target: Terror* and *The Fast and the Furious*, which Taito showed at Japan's AOU trade show in 2005, and *SuperBikes*. In 2007 *Edge* described Raw Thrills as "one of arcade gaming's most powerful new developers" and summed up Jarvis's approach as "easy to learn, tough to master".

Raw Thrills also built cabinets for others. In 2007 Jarvis asked Steve Ranck, who had made *Hydro Thunder* at Midway, whether he wanted to make a sequel; Ranck set up Specular Interactive, whose games, beginning with *H2Overdrive* (2009), came out through Raw Thrills. Their *Batman* driving game, shown at the end of 2013, had a towering bat emblem and 500 lights on its cabinet. Raw Thrills also sold the *Big Buck* hunting games with Play Mechanix.

The International Game Developers Association gave Jarvis a lifetime achievement award, which he collected in the suit he had worn to *Defender*'s launch. He has always been clear about what computers are for: "The only legitimate use of a computer is to play games."
