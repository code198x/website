When King Features licensed Popeye to DK'Tronics, it insisted that the game's sailor look like the cartoon one. Don Priestley took the demand literally. "I sat down with a large grid and came up with a figure of Popeye which was seven characters high and six wide — 42 characters to move for each frame!" he told *[CRASH](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine)* in 1986. "I nearly abandoned it then. It was just too big!" He kept going, and the result was a Spectrum game in which a figure half the height of the screen walked about in full colour without the [colour clash](/vault/techniques/colour-clash) that dogged almost everything else on the machine. One *CRASH* reviewer wrote: "I've never seen anything like it." The same big, bright, slow-moving figures carried Priestley through *Trap Door*, *Flunky*, *Through the Trap Door* and *Gregory Loses His Clock*, and reviewers came to call the look "Don Priestley-style graphics".

## Fast facts

- **ZX81:** *The Damsel and the Beast*, *Dictator* and *Mazogs* (June 1982), for [Bug-Byte](/vault/companies/bug-byte).
- **DK'Tronics (Spectrum):** *Maziacs*, *3D Tanx*, *Jumbly*, *Minder* (1985), *Popeye* (1985, 90% and a *CRASH* Smash) and *Benny Hill's Madcap Chase*.
- **[Piranha](/vault/companies/piranha) (Spectrum):** *Trap Door* (1986, 88% in *CRASH*), *Flunky* (1987, 60%) and *Through the Trap Door* (1987, 70%).
- **Last games:** *Up for Grabs* and *Target* for [Alternative Software](/vault/companies/alternative-software), and *Gregory Loses His Clock* ([Mastertronic](/vault/companies/mastertronic), 1989, 91% in *CRASH*).
- **Before and after games:** a teacher, and a teacher again after he left the industry in 1989.
- **Died:** August 2024, aged 84.

## A teacher at night school

Priestley did not fit the picture of a games programmer. *CRASH* described him in 1986 as "gangly and bearded", and he told the magazine that he had been described in the press as "a pensioner. Well, I'm not, though I am getting a bit long in the tooth." He had been a teacher until 1979. Thinking his teenage son ought to learn something about computers, he enrolled the two of them at a night school to learn Pascal. The son dropped out after a month; Priestley stayed. He bought a ZX81 to convert a program he had written on the course into BASIC.

His first commercial game started with a program called *Mugwump* that he found in a public library. He reworked it as *The Damsel and the Beast* and, as Martyn Carroll later told the story, sold it to Bug-Byte for £75. More ZX81 games for Bug-Byte followed. *Dictator*, a trading game set in a banana republic, was later converted to other machines. *Mazogs*, in June 1982, put large figures in a solid maze built from the ZX81's block graphics. "I was trying to get away from all those games which revolved around a dollar sign being chased by an asterisk," Priestley said. On the Spectrum it became *Maziacs*.

## DK'Tronics

In March 1983 Priestley joined DK'Tronics, the Yarmouth firm that also sold Spectrum add-ons, as a director. He stayed for a little over a year, then went back to freelancing; Carroll writes that he was not interested in the business side or in working with others. For DK'Tronics he wrote *3D Spawn of Evil* ("too smart for its own good", he said), *Meteoroids* and *3D Tanx*, which he called "one of the most successful games ever written": by his account it sold about 5,000 copies a month for nearly 15 months. *Jumbly*, a sliding-block puzzle, did not sell, though he remained proud of it.

*Minder*, his first television licence, was a trading game "played in true Arthur Daley style": the player bargained with dealers by typing phrases such as "I'VE GOT SOME ...". A *CRASH* reviewer in June 1985 found it "time-consuming and annoying": "a tenner's a bit steep". Priestley admitted it had got out of hand: "in fact, the game was written twice. The whole structure of the game was built on sand, not rock."

## Popeye

*Popeye* had been advertised for a year before it appeared. An earlier version by another programmer had been, in Priestley's words, "duff — a platform and ladders game", and he was asked to do it again. His 42-character Popeye could walk forwards and backwards between layers of the street as well as left and right, passing in front of and behind buildings and other characters. *CRASH* reviewed it in September 1985 at £6.95. One reviewer was "amazed to see no problem at all; for the first time masses of colour has been used and with no colour clash", and called it "a very well finished game that proves that even the impossible can be done with a clever bit of programming". Another saw how it had been done and what it cost: "all of the clever shadowing and masking has slowed this game down quite a lot". It scored 90% and was a *CRASH* Smash.

DK'Tronics was losing interest in software, and it offered the game to Macmillan, whose new label Piranha re-released it. Priestley wrote one more licence for DK'Tronics, *Benny Hill's Madcap Chase*, built the same way, but did not want to talk about it: "The less said about Benny Hill the better."

## Trap Door and Piranha

*Trap Door* was based on a children's television series of plasticine monsters that had not yet been shown. Berk, the castle's blue servant, was "given to me on a plate", Priestley said: "Berk was large, easy to draw, and best of all, he's entirely blue." *CRASH* gave it 88% in October 1986; one reviewer admired "the terrific animation and amazing lack of colour clash". Priestley thought of himself as a storyteller rather than a technician. He did not work from storyboards. "If you plan something from beginning to end then you fall into the trap of doing the same thing again and again — like Agatha Christie or Barbara Cartland." Someone else wrote *Trap Door*'s music, and his copy protection was written for him. He remembered reading articles about interrupts and thinking, "What are these interrupts? Why does everybody keep talking about them?"

The style that won *Popeye* a Smash began to cost him marks. Carroll sums up the method: building the figures from whole character blocks "avoided colour clash issues, but shifting such large characters around the screen was slow". (For how such techniques work, see [attribute-aware design](/vault/techniques/attribute-aware-design).) *Flunky* (1987), in which the player is a footman to the royal family, had what *CRASH*'s comments box called "typical big, unmanageable Don Priestly characters". One reviewer complained of "the same old blocky and jerky animation"; another thought Priestley's "masking technique must be nearing the end of its usefulness". It scored 60%. The reviewers split over *Through the Trap Door* that December. One found it "very addictive" and suggested "perhaps Don Priestley could find a new technique sometime"; another wrote that "as in all Don Priestley's games, the graphics look good but their slumping around the screen soon gets irritating". It took 70%.

## Gregory, and leaving

*Gregory Loses His Clock*, a dream-world puzzle game about a boy whose clock has been stolen, came out at £2.99 from Mastertronic in 1989. *CRASH* gave it 91%: "despite the huge graphics, there is no colour clash at all. This doesn't mean there isn't any colour though — there's loads!!" That year Priestley left the industry, believing, Carroll writes, that the days of the lone programmer were over. As he was pulling out, he offered two new games, *Up for Grabs* and *Target*, to Alternative Software, which was re-releasing *Popeye*. "They weren't his usual big chunky graphic games," Alternative's Roger Hulley recalled, "but we couldn't dictate what he did. He was very much his own man."

He went back to teaching. After retiring he moved to Ireland, where he divided his time between pottering and field archery. He died in August 2024, after a short illness, aged 84; the news reached the retro press in February 2025. "He was very humble about his achievements," wrote his archery friend Alan Thorpe.
