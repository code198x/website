Don Priestley's Spectrum games look nothing like anyone else's. *Popeye*, *Trap Door*, *Through the Trap Door*, *Flunky*, *Minder* — all of them are built around **characters several times the usual size, in several colours at once**, on a machine notorious for allowing neither.

## Fast facts

- **Known for:** *Popeye*, *Trap Door* (88% in *CRASH*), *Through the Trap Door*, *Flunky*, *Minder*, *The Great American Cross-Country Road Race*.
- **Published by:** [Piranha](/vault/companies/piranha), among others.
- ***CRASH*** on *Trap Door*: "**huge characters, shining with colour**, and a particular style of humour brings tears to your eyes!"

## The trade

The Spectrum's display stores colour separately from pixels, at **one colour pair per 8×8 character cell**. Move a multicoloured sprite across that grid a pixel at a time and its colours smear into the cells it is passing through — [attribute clash](/vault/techniques/colour-clash), the machine's defining visual problem.

Priestley's answer was to stop fighting it. His characters are drawn **on the character grid**, and they move **in whole character steps** — eight pixels at a time. Because a character never straddles a cell boundary mid-step, every cell it occupies can be given its own colour, and the clash disappears.

What you buy is size and colour: figures far bigger and brighter than the machine's usual monochrome sprites. What you pay is smoothness, and the period reviewers put their finger on it exactly. *CRASH* on *Through the Trap Door*:

> Large graphics and cuddly characters make Through The Trap Door look very good, but the game itself is of no real interest. **As in all Don Priestley's games, the graphics look good but their slumping around the screen soon gets irritating.**

"Slumping" is a good word for eight-pixel movement. That is not a bug being described; it is the price of the technique, visible on screen, in a magazine, at the time.

## Why the trade was worth making

It is tempting to read the criticism as a verdict against the approach, and the market said otherwise: *Trap Door* took 88% and sold, on the strength of characters that looked like the television series they came from.

The general lesson is the one worth keeping. **A hardware limit is rarely a wall; it is a menu.** The Spectrum will not give you smooth multicolour movement, but it will give you either smooth monochrome or chunky multicolour, and which you take is a design decision, not a technical one. Most programmers took the first without noticing they had chosen. Priestley took the second, consistently, for years, and built a recognisable house style out of it.

## Why he matters

Priestley is the case for **choosing your constraint**, and one of the clearest examples of a technical decision producing an authorial signature. You can identify his games from a screenshot.

He also sits at the point where television licences met the Spectrum. *Trap Door* and *Minder* had to look like the things they were licensed from, and a machine that could not do faces in colour was a genuine obstacle to that — until somebody worked out which limit to spend.
