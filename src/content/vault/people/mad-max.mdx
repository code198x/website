In December 1990 the French *[ST Magazine](/vault/magazines/st-magazine)* asked Jochen Hippel, known on the scene as Mad Max, how it felt to hear most of the Atari ST's demo groups using his music, when perhaps they should not. "It doesn't bother me!" he said. And the groups that had built little music editors on top of his routines? "On the ST?!" he laughed. "I wasn't aware!" (quotations translated). Two and a half years later, the same magazine explained why so many games crashed on Atari's new [Falcon](/vault/systems/atari-falcon030), in a section headed "Mad Max est coupable!" (Mad Max is guilty!). The writer had fought with his routines to make them work, "and since everyone uses routines more or less derived from his ...". Hippel's music had become part of the machinery of ST software.

## Fast facts

- **Name:** Jochen Hippel; scene name Mad Max.
- **Group:** The Exceptions (TEX), one of the first demo groups on the [Atari ST](/vault/systems/atari-st).
- **First game:** *Bad Cat* (Rainbow Arts), an arrangement rather than an original score, by his own account.
- **Game work:** for [Rainbow Arts](/vault/companies/rainbow-arts), [Hewson](/vault/companies/hewson-consultants), [Thalion](/vault/companies/thalion), Starbyte, [Grandslam](/vault/companies/grandslam) and others; music for Thalion's *Wings of Death* and *Lethal Xcess*, and the Amiga conversion of *A Prehistoric Tale*.
- **Tools:** his own music software and replay routines; Amiga players later supported "Hippel" and "Hippel-COSO" as music formats.

## From the C64 to The Exceptions

Hippel started on the Commodore 64. Michael Raasch, the TEX coder known as Daryl, told *ST Magazine* in 1991 that he had known Jochen "since the C64 days". When Daryl invited Erik Simon and Udo Fischer, two of the group's founders, to a party at his home, "they saw that Jochen was making music and asked him to join, which he did". Fischer said that the soundtrack of his *BIG Demo* was Hippel's job: "it was Jochen who had to convert all the C64 music."

Doing that meant getting Commodore 64 tunes out of a very different chip. The ST's sound came from a Yamaha YM2149, a version of General Instrument's [AY-3-8910](/vault/hardware/ay-3-8910) with three square-wave voices and a noise generator, and it had none of the SID's filters or waveforms. Hippel wrote his own tools to do it. When the Italian magazine *K* visited Thalion in 1990, it called the 18-year-old "an incredible music programmer" who recreated the music and effects of C64 and Amiga games and demos "just for fun". It watched him redo a classic [Rob Hubbard](/vault/people/rob-hubbard) tune in a few minutes with a sound editor he had written himself, typing in the hexadecimal at a speed the reporter found as impressive as the tune (translated).

## Music for games

By the time *ST Magazine* interviewed him for its November 1991 feature on TEX, with Daryl asking the questions, Hippel was 20 and composing full-time, "if you can call that work, because for me it's a pleasure". He had no qualifications: "as soon as I finished school I started making music on computers." His first game had been Rainbow Arts' *Bad Cat*, "an adaptation, not a composition"; since then he had done "an enormous amount" for Rainbow Arts, Thalion, Hewson, Starbyte, Grandslam and others, and he was working on Thalion's shooting game *Lethal Xcess* and its next role-playing game. Demo music was easier to write, he said, because it only needed "a good theme" to go round for hours; game music had to create an atmosphere. One demo tune had taken him two hours, another nearly eight. He wrote all his own software apart from his sampler, which Michael Bittner had designed and he had improved, and he worked surrounded by a Roland D-20, a piano, two STs, two Amigas and a Commodore 64 (translated).

Databases of ST software credit him with music for Hewson's *Astaroth* and *Cybernoid II*, Thalion's *Chambers of Shaolin*, *Dragonflight* and *Amberstar*, and many more. Chris Wilkins's history of Amiga games picks out "the stunning visuals and sublime soundtrack by Jochen 'Mad Max' Hippel" in Thalion's shoot-'em-up *Wings of Death* (1990). *[The One](/vault/magazines/the-one)* introduced him in 1992 simply as "Thalion musician Jochen Hippel", reviewing a public-domain disk of his tunes; it found the music "a bit on the bland side".

He worked on the Amiga too. The Amiga version of *A Prehistoric Tale*, a game by the ST group The Lost Boys, carries a message inside it: "AMIGA-CONVERSION BY JOCHEN HIPPEL HALLO LEUTE !!!!!" ("hello everyone"). His work crossed paths with [Chris Huelsbeck](/vault/people/chris-huelsbeck)'s. "Jochen Hippel translated some of my tunes to his own Atari ST sound system, which was really efficient and emulated the Amiga sound chip," Huelsbeck recalled. For *Turrican II* he ported Hippel's code back to the Amiga, mixing four simulated voices into one channel, "and with Jochen's help" ended up with seven voices on a four-channel machine.

## Everyone's routines

Hippel's tunes and routines spread far beyond his own releases. In August 1992 *ST Magazine* reported a single demo screen holding 257 Mad Max tunes, eight and a half hours of music that ran on a 520 ST. Public-domain libraries sold disks of his "sound chip music". Amiga music players supported his formats: the EaglePlayer module player listed "Hippel" and "Hippel-COSO" among the formats it supported, alongside players named after Rob Hubbard and David Whittaker.

That reach had a cost when Atari launched the Falcon 030 in 1993. *ST Magazine*'s guide to why old games would not run on it blamed, among other things, the 68030 processor's caches, which can disagree with memory when a program modifies its own code, a "technique used to excess in demos and games". The writer said he bore Hippel no personal grudge: "It's just that I fought with his routines to make them run on the Falcon. And since everyone uses routines more or less derived from his ..." (translated).
