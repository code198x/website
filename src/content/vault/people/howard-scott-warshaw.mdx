In 1982 Atari's own club magazine printed a checklist of every new cartridge the company had released that year. Three of them were the work of one programmer, Howard Scott Warshaw: *Yars' Revenge* in May, *Raiders of the Lost Ark* in November and *E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial* in December. The first became one of the best-selling games on the [Atari 2600](/vault/systems/atari-2600). The last, written in about five weeks, became the best-known symbol of the [1983 video game crash](/vault/phenomena/1983-crash). Warshaw has spent the decades since making films about Atari, writing about game design and, since 2012, working as a psychotherapist.

## Fast facts

- **Born:** 30 July 1957; grew up in New Jersey.
- **Education:** economics at Tulane University in New Orleans, then a master's degree in computer engineering.
- **At [Atari](/vault/companies/atari):** January 1981 to 1984.
- **Games:** *Yars' Revenge* (1982), *Raiders of the Lost Ark* (1982), *[E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial](/vault/games/et-the-extra-terrestrial)* (1982); *Saboteur*, unfinished when he left.
- **Later:** the documentary series *Once Upon Atari*; 3DO in the late 1990s; licensed marriage and family therapist in California from 2012; the memoir *Once Upon Atari: How I Made History by Killing an Industry*.

## From Hewlett-Packard to Atari

Warshaw told *Retro Gamer* that he avoided computers at school in New Jersey and took his first computer class reluctantly, as part of an economics degree, then finished the whole semester's work in days. He went on to a master's in computer engineering and a programming job at Hewlett-Packard, which bored him. "I was really bored at HP," he told *ClassicGamer Magazine* in 2000. A colleague's wife worked at Atari and thought he would fit in there.

Atari at first turned him down. He recalled that they thought him "too straight" for the place, and he took a pay cut to get in. *Edge* dates his start to January 1981. What he found was, in his words to *ClassicGamer*, "the most fun place I ever worked": programmers who played bocce with lemons in the corridors, total creative freedom, "as long as we kept the games coming out".

## Yars' Revenge

His first assignment was a 2600 version of the arcade game *Star Castle*. Warshaw decided that a straight conversion "would really not translate well to the VCS at all", so he took *Star Castle* apart, kept the parts that worked, and built a different game around them. The player's Yar, an insect, eats away at a shield to expose the Qotile, then calls up the Zorlon Cannon from the left edge of the screen to fire at it. He wanted a game with movement on both axes at once, and a full-screen explosion "because I wanted there to be a big pay-off".

The working title was *Time Freeze*. Warshaw disliked it and wrote his own story, packed with the cartridge as a comic book. He named the insects Yars after Atari's chief executive, [Ray Kassar](/vault/people/ray-kassar): "Yar" is "Ray" backwards, and the game's full title, "The Yarian Revenge of the Razak Solar System", hides "Kassar" backwards too.

*Electronic Games* reviewed it in October 1982 as "a potential blockbuster that misses greatness by an eyelash". The sound was excellent and the comic book gave the action a story, but the game had only one objective, repeated indefinitely, and "in this regard, Yar is far too static". It sold well all the same: *Edge* and *Retro Gamer* have both described it as the best-selling original game for the VCS, as opposed to an arcade conversion or a licence.

It also carried Warshaw's initials. Atari did not credit its programmers on its boxes, and in its September–October 1982 issue *Atari Age*, Atari's club magazine, told readers that "HSW" were "the initials of the programmer who invented and designed the game", and that the key to finding them was in the instruction book. That month a reader wrote to *Electronic Games* that "six letters — HSWWSH — appear on the scoring screen when it's found": his initials forwards and backwards. The British magazine *TV Gamer* later reported initials in his *Raiders* and *E.T.* as well.

## Raiders of the Lost Ark

For his second game Warshaw wanted "to make the biggest adventure game possible for the VCS. Not just the most screens, but the most different scenarios and the most immersive environment". He respected [Warren Robinett](/vault/people/warren-robinett)'s *[Adventure](/vault/games/adventure)* but did not want simply to repeat it. His *Raiders of the Lost Ark* sends Indiana Jones in search of the Ark of the Covenant through 13 locations, with snakes, black-market sheiks and hidden areas, and it uses both joysticks at once: one to move Indy, the other to work his inventory.

He flew to Los Angeles to show the game to Steven Spielberg, who had directed the film. To get into character he carried a ten-foot bullwhip around Atari's offices and cracked it behind people's backs. "People hated it," he said. The game took him eight months, he said in 2000. It came out in November 1982, and both *ClassicGamer* and *Edge* call it his second million-seller.

## E.T.

The meeting with Spielberg led to *E.T.* After Atari's licence deal for the film was done at the end of July 1982, the game had to be finished by 1 September to be made in time for Christmas. "That left you with five weeks to do the whole game," Warshaw said. No one else would take the job. He had a development system moved into his house and worked almost continuously. The [E.T. entry](/vault/games/et-the-extra-terrestrial) tells the story of the game, its reviews and the landfill.

He looked back on it in 2012 with some humour: "I submitted a complete, non-trivial game on time, so in that sense it was a success. Many didn't enjoy playing it; so on that level it was a failure. It sold well over a million — a success! It needed to sell seven million to make money — a failure!"

His last Atari game, *Saboteur*, was an original shooter that he told *Retro Gamer* was 95 per cent complete when Atari bought the licence for the television series *The A-Team* and asked him to rework it. Then, in his words, "Atari imploded". He left in 1984. *Saboteur* was eventually released decades later on one of the Atari Flashback plug-and-play consoles.

## After Atari

Over the next fifteen years Warshaw worked in real estate, industrial robotics, writing, teaching and photography. By the late 1990s he was making videos. *Once Upon Atari* was a four-part documentary in which he interviewed the programmers he had worked with; he made the last episode, "The Agony and the Ecstasy", first, and *Digital Press* reviewed it in 1997. By 2000 he was back in games at 3DO, which he later described to *Retro Gamer* as "a reality crash": the company closed and left him and others owed wages.

In 2000 he told *ClassicGamer* that he had no personal knowledge of the story about Atari burying *E.T.* cartridges in the desert: "I've never seen any pictures. I've never been there myself." In April 2014 he was at the Alamogordo landfill in New Mexico when a documentary crew for the film *Atari: Game Over* and a team of archaeologists dug the site. He described the day in *Wireframe*, signing the fans' *E.T.* memorabilia and meeting the novelist Ernest Cline, who arrived in a DeLorean.

He had meanwhile trained as a psychotherapist, and on 14 November 2012 he became a licensed marriage and family therapist in California, with a practice in Los Altos. He calls himself "the Silicon Valley Therapist" and works with engineers and people in high-pressure technology jobs. In the first issue of *Wireframe*, in 2018, where he began a long-running column on game design, he wrote that becoming a psychotherapist was "the first thing I've found since Atari that's given me the depth of joy and satisfaction I first experienced making video games". His memoir, *Once Upon Atari: How I Made History by Killing an Industry*, appeared in 2020, with a second book, *Inspired Therapist*.

He has long treated the charge that he caused the crash as a joke. "To think I single-handedly toppled a billion dollar industry just cracks me up," he told *Retro Gamer*.
