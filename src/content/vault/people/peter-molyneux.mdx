In February 1989 *[The One](/vault/magazines/the-one)* reported that there were probably only seven kinds of computer game, "but Bullfrog Productions' Peter Molyneux reckons that his company has just invented the eighth". The game was *[Populous](/vault/games/populous)*, and within a year magazines had a name for its kind: the god game. Molyneux spent the next three decades making variations on it, at three companies, in a career marked as much by what he said his games would do as by what they did. In 2012, starting again with a small studio, he put it himself: "A lot of people are sick of me talking about stuff and never really delivering on promises."

## Fast facts

- **Born:** 1959, Guildford, Surrey.
- **Companies:** Taurus (from 1985), [Bullfrog Productions](/vault/companies/bullfrog) (1987–1997), [Lionhead Studios](/vault/companies/lionhead) (1997–2012), 22cans (from 2012).
- **Designed:** *[Populous](/vault/games/populous)* (1989), *Powermonger* (1990), *[Black & White](/vault/games/black-and-white)* (2001), *[Fable](/vault/games/fable)* (2004), *Curiosity* (2012), *Godus* (from 2013), *Masters of Albion* (2026).
- **Led as head of Bullfrog:** *[Syndicate](/vault/games/syndicate)*, *Magic Carpet*, *[Theme Park](/vault/games/theme-park)* and *[Dungeon Keeper](/vault/games/dungeon-keeper)*.
- **Honours:** OBE (2005 New Year Honours); BAFTA Fellowship (2011).

## Taurus and a wrong number

Molyneux started out in business software, not games. *The One* reported in 1989 that he and Les Edgar had started in 1985 writing business programs for the Amiga under the name Taurus. Molyneux later told *[Edge](/vault/magazines/edge)* how Taurus got its first Amigas: Commodore rang to offer machines for "our product", in a call meant for Torus, a maker of network cards, and he took them without saying so. The *[Populous](/vault/games/populous)* entry tells the story in full.

Molyneux and Edgar formed Bullfrog in 1987. Its first work was small: an Amiga conversion of *Druid II* for Firebird, and the shoot-'em-up *Fusion* for [Electronic Arts](/vault/companies/electronic-arts), released in 1988.

## Bullfrog

*Populous* came out in the spring of 1989. Molyneux designed and programmed it, with Glenn Corpes drawing it, and the team first worked out its world with a Lego model. It won awards across Europe, and by the early 1990s magazines were quoting sales of between 750,000 and more than a million. "We had no idea at all it would be as successful as it was," Molyneux told *[ST Format](/vault/magazines/st-format)* in 1990. "It was terrifying."

He saw the games that followed as the same idea at smaller and smaller scales. "After that there was Powermonger, which simulated a country – instead of a whole world – in a lot more detail," he told *Edge* in 1998. "Moving forward from that was Syndicate, which was a city." In the United States *[Computer Gaming World](/vault/magazines/computer-gaming-world)* was by 1991 bracketing him with the designer of *[SimCity](/vault/games/sim-city)*: "God-game gurus Will Wright (SimCity, SimEarth) and Peter Molyneux (Populous, Powermonger)".

Bullfrog grew into one of the best-known studios in Britain, based in Guildford. *Edge* listed Molyneux's highlights in 1996 as *Populous*, *Theme Park*, *Magic Carpet*, *Syndicate* and *High Octane*, and noted that *Theme Park* had been "many critics' Game of the Year in 1994". All of Bullfrog's games had been published by Electronic Arts, and in 1995 EA bought the company. *Computer Gaming World* put the price at "over $40 million", *Edge* at "around $40 million".

## Leaving Bullfrog

In the summer of 1997 Molyneux left. *Edge* reported that he was unhappy as head of a company and wanted to keep the "hands-on approach he returned to with the development of epochal PC strategy title, Dungeon Keeper". *Computer Gaming World* wrote of "frustrations with an increasingly corporate environment at Bullfrog". Two years later *Edge* put the question to Les Edgar, who had stayed with EA, and he answered: "Of course it stifles certain types of creativity — all companies do."

## Lionhead

Molyneux's new company, [Lionhead Studios](/vault/companies/lionhead), started in Guildford in 1997. Mark Webley was a co-founder, and *Edge* reported that the Games Workshop co-founder Steve Jackson had joined. Its name, Molyneux told *Computer Gaming World*, had belonged to Webley's hamster, which died soon after they chose it: "We felt that perhaps this wasn't a very good omen." They kept it anyway.

Lionhead's first game, *[Black & White](/vault/games/black-and-white)*, took more than three years. Molyneux saw it as the end of the path that began with *Populous*. *Edge* described a blend of "realtime resource management, elements of RPG and action-adventure, beat 'em up, and Tamagotchi-style creature care". *Edge* compared its long development to John Romero's troubled *Daikatana*, then added: "The key difference between the two products is that Black & White delivers. And it delivers in spades." *PC Gamer* gave it 94% and called it "a PC game that redefines the way that we play games".

*[Fable](/vault/games/fable)*, an Xbox role-playing game, followed in 2004. Before release Molyneux had described many features that did not appear in it. *Edge*'s review found a game that, after "a thermal of continuous hype", was "fittingly grounded". Players complained on Lionhead's own forums, and Molyneux apologised there. He told *Edge*: "The real catalyst was the complaints that we were getting on [Lionhead's forums], which we take very seriously, that people felt cheated certain features hadn't made it into the game. We never had any intention to mislead." He did not expect to change: "I'm incapable of sticking to any pre-planned PR strategy for very long... I'm always going to talk about my dreams, but I'll be clearer that these are dreams that may or may not make it into a finished game."

[Microsoft](/vault/companies/microsoft) bought Lionhead in 2006, and Molyneux stayed with it until 2012.

## 22cans

In March 2012 Molyneux left Lionhead and Microsoft to start again, at 52. His new studio, 22cans, took rooms in a business centre in Guildford, less than a minute's walk from Lionhead. Its first release was *Curiosity: What's Inside the Cube?* (2012), an experiment for phones in which players around the world tapped away the surface of one enormous cube. Molyneux promised that the one player to reach the centre would find something that would change their life.

Bryan Henderson, from Edinburgh, reached it in May 2013. The prize was to be the "god of gods" in 22cans' next game, *Godus*, with one per cent of its revenue. *Godus*, which 22cans had funded on Kickstarter in 2012 and described as a spiritual successor to *Populous*, never gained the role. When Eurogamer reported in 2015 that the studio had lost touch with Henderson, Molyneux accepted the blame: "I totally and absolutely and categorically apologise. That isn't good enough and I'll take it on my own shoulders."

Microsoft closed Lionhead in 2016. In April 2026 22cans released *Masters of Albion* on early access, an open-world god game that Molyneux described as the culmination of his work and said would be his last.
