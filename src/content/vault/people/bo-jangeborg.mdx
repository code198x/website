In 1985 Bo Jangeborg flew from Sweden to London to sign a publishing contract for his Spectrum drawing program, *The Artist*. He expected to stay a week. "A one-week visit became seven months," he told *[Edge](/vault/magazines/edge)* in 2004, and in those months he wrote *[Fairlight](/vault/games/fairlight)*, the isometric adventure that *[CRASH](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine)* gave 95% in November 1985. It was, *Edge* noted, his first complete game. It was also his last.

## Fast facts

- **From:** born in Sundsvall and brought up in Gothenburg, Sweden, as he told the Swedish magazine *Atari-STen* in 1989. *Sinclair User* gave his age as 23 in December 1986.
- **Spectrum programs:** *The Artist* (1985), *Fairlight* (1985), *The Artist II* (1986) and *Fairlight II: A Trail of Darkness* (1986).
- **Publisher:** Softek, of Covent Garden in London, which became [The Edge](/vault/companies/the-edge).
- **Tools:** Grax, the room-drawing system behind *Fairlight*'s Worldmaker technique (see [Worldmaker and Grax](/vault/tools/worldmaker)).
- **Later:** *Flash!*, the graphics program bundled with MGT's SAM Coupé (1989).

## From a dot on the screen

Jangeborg's interest in programming started at school, on the ABC 80, a Swedish computer built around the Z80 processor, the same chip as the Spectrum's. He was 16 or 17, he told *Sinclair User*, and he taught himself from the Spectrum's manual and a Z80 reference card: "Probably the best way for anyone to learn code is not a course but a mixture of books and designing your own simple routines."

*The Artist* was the first thing he wrote. "It grew from a simple little machine code program that allowed me to set a dot on screen and move it around," he told *Edge*. "There were lots of little problems such as making a fast flood fill and a fast circle and line drawing function. Fitting all of it into 48K was a bit of a problem, too." Softek published it.

## A week in Covent Garden

When he arrived in London, Jangeborg found one of Softek's programmers struggling with an isometric routine. Every Spectrum publisher wanted an answer to Ultimate's *Knight Lore*, and this one was not working. "I don't think he really knew what he was doing," he recalled. "They had shown me the problem the day before," and when he came back to show them the final copy of *The Artist*, he also showed them a little piece of isometric code of his own. "I started work on *Fairlight* right away." He lodged at the house of Softek's owner, Tim Langdell.

*CRASH* sent John Minson to see the game in September 1985. He found Jangeborg behind a Spectrum fitted with disk drives. He had been working on *Fairlight* "since March, from ten in the morning to eleven at night, sometimes sleeping at his micro". The hero did not yet have a name; Minson suggested Graeme, after *CRASH*'s editor, but the game shipped with Isvar. Minson also reported that role-playing games had been one of Jangeborg's pastimes for seven years, and that he had devised his own game system, from which *Fairlight*'s background came. Jangeborg said the same to *Edge* nearly twenty years later: "I remember that I definitely wanted to create a castle. I suppose my background in playing role-playing games such as Dungeons & Dragons served as an inspiration."

He did not draw everything himself. Jack Wilkes drew the loading picture and most of the sprites and objects, using *The Artist*; Jangeborg drew the main character and the backgrounds. Some things were left out. *Fairlight* plays in silence after its two-channel title tune, and Jangeborg explained why: "I really had no experience in writing games, and getting an interrupt-driven sound system working with the sprite engine at the time would have been a daunting task."

*Edge* put *Fairlight*'s sales at more than 50,000 copies.

## Grax

The engine was the part Jangeborg was proudest of. In November 1985 Minson went back to see Grax, the utility "at the heart of the Worldmaker technique which gives *Fairlight* its realistic solidity". It worked like *The Artist*: the designer drew a room in overhead perspective with rubber-banded lines and 26 fill patterns, then placed pre-drawn doors and windows into it. It had begun as a way of drawing half-screen pictures for adventures written with *[The Quill](/vault/tools/the-quill)*, and Minson picked out the result that mattered on a 48K machine: "Bo has found a way of reducing all these room details to a minimum of code." That is how *Fairlight* fitted 60 castle rooms and 20 caves into the Spectrum.

Asked by *Sinclair User* in 1986 for his greatest programming achievement, Jangeborg named "the Grax programming language — my own language for graphics".

Minson's report also said that Jangeborg was "returning to Sweden and his own company, Xcellent Software", leaving Grax behind with The Edge as the basis for more adventures and, one day, a product that readers could buy. In September 1986 *CRASH* reported that The Edge was planning an icon-driven adventure-writing system, Dreamscape, which "just might include" Grax.

## Fairlight II and The Artist II

Jangeborg did come back. *Fairlight II: A Trail of Darkness* used the same Worldmaker system with, *CRASH* reported in December 1986, a playing area twice the size. It loaded in two parts on the 48K Spectrum and in one on the 128K. *The Artist II* arrived at the same time, "courtesy of the productive Bo Jangeborg". *[Your Sinclair](/vault/magazines/your-sinclair)*'s chart had *Fairlight II* entering at number 14. *CRASH*'s reviewers were cooler than they had been about the first game; one found it "no real step forward from the original". Looking back, *Edge* reported in 2004, Jangeborg regarded the released version as incomplete.

In his *Sinclair User* interview he named [Mike Singleton](/vault/people/mike-singleton) as the programmer he admired, and said the best thing about the Spectrum was that its "general architecture is very open making it easy to try new ways of doing things". The worst was "the painful layout of the screen in memory".

## Leaving The Edge

By May 1987 Minson found Jangeborg at a [Telecomsoft](/vault/companies/telecomsoft) party, having "had enough of life on The Edge". In 1989 he told *Atari-STen* that a game called *Resolution* and "the conflict with The Edge" had taken up the previous two years. Asked whether he had any comment on The Edge, he said (in translation) "what has been, has been", and that it was "something I would rather forget". *Edge* later put his departure down to a contractual dispute.

*Resolution* was a horizontally scrolling game, previewed by *[ACE](/vault/magazines/ace-magazine)* in January 1988 for Nexus. The next month *ACE* reported that Nexus had gone into liquidation, and the programmer then running it promised that the games in development, *Resolution* among them, "will all go to nice, good homes". *Sinclair User* reported in March 1988 that Jangeborg had signed with Telecomsoft to write two games. By 1989 he had given *Resolution* up. "I got tired of it," he told *Atari-STen*. It could have been turned into a cheap shoot 'em up, but making it into a real game would take too much effort, so he had decided to drop it. He told *Edge* the same story about Telecomsoft: "I did sign on with BT after that, but the game I was developing was probably too technically difficult to make, so I cancelled that contract."

In the same 1989 interview he said he was putting the Spectrum away for the Atari ST and probably the Amiga, that there would be no *Fairlight III*, and that he wanted to write a strategy game about space empires.

## Flash! and the SAM Coupé

His last widely seen 8-bit work was for the Spectrum's would-be successor. MGT, the company building the SAM Coupé, set up its own software team in 1989, and *Computer and Video Games* reported that it had commissioned "a host of big names, including *Fairlight* coder Bo Jangeborg and tunesmith Dave Whittaker" to write tools for the machine. *Your Sinclair* reported that Jangeborg was writing a set of graphics utilities and a new art program for it. That program was *Flash!*, and MGT's advertisements announced that it came free with every Coupé: "a software package by ace Swedish programmer, Bo Jangeborg, designed to give you total control over these powerful graphics." *Retro Gamer* later described it as an update of his *Artist* programs.

## Afterwards

By 2004 Jangeborg was running his own company, Softwave, in Sweden, and still programming, "mainly a payroll system at the moment", he told *Edge*. He looked back on the *Fairlight* months kindly: "Despite what followed later I regard it as a very exciting time, and Covent Garden was very nice."
