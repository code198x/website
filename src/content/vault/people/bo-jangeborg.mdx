## Overview

**Bo Jangeborg** wrote *[Fairlight](/vault/games/fairlight)*, one of the technical
high points of the 48K [ZX Spectrum](/vault/systems/zx-spectrum), and one of the
few games of the period whose engine was a documented product in its own right.

He is Swedish, and worked for a London publisher, [The Edge](/vault/companies/the-edge),
from its offices in Covent Garden — a fact worth stating plainly because the
British 8-bit story is usually told as a national one and was not.

## Working on Fairlight

*CRASH*'s John Minson visited The Edge in September 1985 while the game was being
finished, and the profile is short but specific.

Jangeborg had been working on *Fairlight* **since March**, "from **ten in the
morning to eleven at night, sometimes sleeping at his micro**". He was sitting
behind a Spectrum fitted with disk drives. When Minson arrived the hero still had
no name — Minson suggested Graeme, after *CRASH*'s editor, and was ignored; the
character shipped as **Isvar**.

Minson also noted, in the way of 1985 games journalism, that Jangeborg's English
was better than his own Swedish by a wide margin.

## Tools first

The part of Jangeborg's work that this project cares about most is that he did not
start with the game.

He wrote **Grax**, a room editor, and **Worldmaker**, the system that assembles
rooms into a world — and Grax itself had begun as a utility for drawing half-screen
illustrations for [Quill](/vault/tools/the-quill) adventures. *CRASH* reported the
crucial engineering result in one sentence: "**Bo has found a way of reducing all
these room details to a minimum of code.**"

That is what made 80 rooms fit in 48K, and it is why *Fairlight*'s achievement is
a tooling achievement wearing a game's clothes. See
[Worldmaker and Grax](/vault/tools/worldmaker).

## What happened next

Jangeborg returned to Sweden and his own company, **Xcellent Software**, but left
the tools with The Edge, which intended to build "a series of diverse adventures"
on them and eventually to sell Grax as a product. Neither arrived.

He did write the sequel. *Fairlight II: A Trail of Darkness* (1986) doubled the
playing area on the same system, and *CRASH* was markedly cooler about it —
"pretty but unplayable", the magazine decided by 1987.

He was still working on Spectrum-lineage machines at the end of the decade: in
September 1989 *CRASH* noted that "Bo Jangeborg, creator of the *Fairlight* duo of
games, has written a graphics utility especially for **Sam**" — MGT's SAM Coupé,
the Spectrum's unofficial successor. Ten years in, and still shipping the tool
rather than the game.
