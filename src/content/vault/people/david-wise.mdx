David Wise wanted to be a drummer who became a songwriter, "somebody like Phil Collins". He was working in a music shop, demonstrating a Yamaha CX5 music computer, when two customers asked who had written the tunes it was playing. They were Tim and Chris Stamper, the founders of [Rare](/vault/companies/rare). "I thought 'yes, I've sold another one', so I took them upstairs, started getting the finance papers out – and they offered me a job." The music he went on to write for Rare had to fit into a few kilobytes and be finished in weeks. On the Super Nintendo, it became the soundtrack of *[Donkey Kong Country](/vault/games/donkey-kong-country)*.

## Fast facts

- **With Rare:** from 1985 until 2009, as a freelance musician at first and then on the staff.
- **NES and Game Boy:** *Slalom*, *Wizards & Warriors*, *R.C. Pro-Am*, *[Battletoads](/vault/games/battletoads)*, *Monster Max* and many licensed games.
- **Super Nintendo:** most of the music for *Donkey Kong Country* (1994), with Eveline Fischer and Robin Beanland, and *Donkey Kong Country 2: Diddy's Kong Quest* (1995).
- **Later Rare work:** *Diddy Kong Racing* (1997), *Star Fox Adventures* (2002), the Game Boy Advance *Donkey Kong Country 3* (2005).
- **Freelance:** *Donkey Kong Country: Tropical Freeze* (2014), *Snake Pass* and *Yooka-Laylee* (2017).

## Hex codes and a doorbell

Wise had had piano lessons, played trumpet in a brass band and drums in a punk band, and had no other formal musical training. At the music shop he had been wiring synthesisers and drum machines together over MIDI. At Rare there was none of that. On his first game, *Slalom*, Rare's first NES title, "I found out I would have to hand code HEX values for note pitches and note durations into sub-routines on a computer." He called the NES "very much a glorified doorbell": a few channels of plain tones and "a noise channel that wasn't particularly glorious". For sound effects he adapted a library that Rare's lead programmer had built up.

The pace was fast. Rare wrote games for other publishers as well as Nintendo, and Wise remembered having one, two or, "if I was very, very lucky", four weeks to write and produce a whole soundtrack. *Eurogamer* counted more than a dozen games in a single year. For *Battletoads*, he said, "Tim, the creative force at Rare, was very much into German Rock bands at the time." Some of the music he wrote for Rare's arcade *Battletoads*, on a board Chris Stamper had designed, was converted for the NES and Super Nintendo versions, he said. On the Game Boy, *Retro Gamer* singled out his "rockin' guitar licks" in *Monster Max* (1994) as showing what the handheld's four-channel sound chip could do. When [Grant Kirkhope](/vault/people/grant-kirkhope) joined Rare later in the decade, it was Wise who showed him how music was written for the Game Boy.

## Donkey Kong Country

Wise started on *Donkey Kong Country* while still freelance. He expected his tunes to be placeholders: "I understood how important the Donkey Kong license was to Nintendo, so assumed my work would eventually be replaced by a Japanese composer." He was asked for three jungle demo tunes, which were joined together as "DK Swing", "and they offered me a full time position at Rare". *Edge* credited the music to Wise, Eveline Fischer and Robin Beanland; Wise later said that he wrote most of it, with Fischer as co-composer and Beanland contributing one track.

The Super Nintendo was, in his words, "like being given an orchestra", but every sample had to fit into 64K of memory. *Nintendo Magazine System* reported in January 1995 that Wise had used a compression technique developed at Rare to get more out of that 64K. Wise explained his own method. He borrowed the idea of "Wave Sequencing" from the Korg Wavestation synthesiser, which built rich sounds by stringing together very short samples, many of them single cycles of a waveform. "Aquatic Ambience", the underwater theme, was his first attempt, and his "first piece of music for Rare as a full time employee". For *Donkey Kong Country 2* he went further, sampling a Roland Juno-2 synthesiser at different filter settings and typing in the hex routines that put the waveforms back together. Cutting the ends off single-cycle waves to save memory, he found, added "subtle distortion and harmonics" that he liked. He said that "Stickerbush Symphony" from the sequel "really pushed the very limits of the sound chip on the SNES".

## After the Super Nintendo

On the Nintendo 64, Rare had more composers, and Wise's share of the work fell. For *Diddy Kong Racing* (1997) he was "unashamedly paying tribute" to the music of *Super Mario Kart* and *Mario Kart 64*; *Retro Gamer* later called its soundtrack some of "the best music on the N64". *Star Fox Adventures* began as *Dinosaur Planet* for the Nintendo 64 and became a [Star Fox](/vault/games/star-fox) game on the GameCube late in development, so the familiar themes went in near the end. After Microsoft bought Rare in 2002 he worked on handheld games, and on Xbox projects that were never released.

He left in 2009. "The reality was we had just too many composers for the titles we were producing," he told *Eurogamer*. To *The i Paper* he described how Rare had grown from ten people to about 250 under Microsoft: "There was a lot of management."

Freelance, he set up his own studio and wrote for the mobile adventure *Sorcery!* and the indie game *Tengami*. In 2014 he returned to the series with *Donkey Kong Country: Tropical Freeze* for [Retro Studios](/vault/companies/retro-studios): "being given the chance to work on a new DKC was like coming home". In 2017 he scored Sumo Digital's *Snake Pass*, using bamboo marimbas and shakers for its jungle setting, and worked with Kirkhope again on *Yooka-Laylee*, made by former Rare staff.
