Tetsuya Takahashi's games are remembered for being too big for their budgets. *Edge* called the second disc of his *[Xenogears](/vault/games/xenogears)* "essentially a series of placeholder cutscenes", because the money ran out, and his *Xenosaga* series stopped "after just three of the six intended instalments". In 2007, looking back on *Xenogears*, the magazine described him as an auteur "whose imagination and ambition outstripped even the gargantuan budget SquareSoft allowed him". What makes his career unusual is what came next. For *Xenoblade Chronicles* on the Wii he reined in the cut-scenes and let the world tell the story, and *Edge* hailed "a return to creative form".

## Fast facts

- **Early career:** graphics at [Nihon Falcom](/vault/companies/falcom), then [Square](/vault/companies/square), where he was one of the art directors of *Final Fantasy VI*.
- **Directed:** *[Xenogears](/vault/games/xenogears)* (Square, 1998), *Xenosaga Episode I* (Namco, 2002), *Xenoblade Chronicles* (Nintendo, 2010).
- **Company:** co-founded [Monolith Soft](/vault/companies/monolith-soft) with Hirohide Sugiura in 1999; [Nintendo](/vault/companies/nintendo) bought it in 2007. He is one of its directors.

## Falcom and Square

Monolith Soft's own biography of Takahashi gives his path in one line: Nihon Falcom, then Square, then Monolith Soft's founding in 1999. At Square he worked on the 16-bit games. *Edge*'s review of *Xenoblade Chronicles* listed "*Final Fantasy V*, *[Chrono Trigger](/vault/games/chrono-trigger)* and *[Secret Of Mana](/vault/games/secret-of-mana)*" among his credits. On *Final Fantasy VI* he was one of four art directors: in *Edge*'s account he "designed the imperial Magitek Armors seen in the game's iconic introductory sequence" and was "responsible for the world map".

## *Xenogears*

*Xenogears* came out in Japan in February 1998, with Takahashi as writer and director. Kaori Tanaka, who writes as Soraya Saga, created it with him. She later recalled that they "originally submitted it as a script idea for *Final Fantasy VII*". It was turned down as too dark and complicated for a fantasy, and Takahashi was allowed to make it as a new project instead.

The game is a war story told by giant robots, a hero with a split personality and a god that turns out to be a crashed weapon (the [Xenogears](/vault/games/xenogears) entry has the full story). It sold half a million copies in Japan within months, and in 2001 IGN's magazine *Unplugged* thought it had "probably drawn a more rabid fanbase than any other PlayStation RPG". The same magazine noted that the game's ending called it "Episode V", part of an imagined six-part epic. It also showed what happened when a story outgrew its schedule. *Edge* found the second disc "relies ever more heavily on non-interactive cutscenes" after "time or budget restraints forced them to rush the game to completion". It called *Xenogears* "a grand demonstration of what can go wrong, and right, when a single-minded development team is given free rein and financing to do as it pleases".

## Monolith Soft and *Xenosaga*

Takahashi left Square in 1999, and "a great many of Xenogears' staff" went with him, *Unplugged* reported. Monolith Soft was set up on 1 October 1999 with Hirohide Sugiura, a Square producer, as its head, and "under the wing of [Namco](/vault/companies/namco)", which put up the money. Square kept the rights to *Xenogears*, so the team started again. Its first game was codenamed Project X until the Tokyo Game Show in spring 2001 revealed its name: *Xenosaga Episode I: Der Wille zur Macht*, German for "The Will to Power".

*Xenosaga Episode I* came out in Japan in February 2002 on the PlayStation 2. Like the two episodes after it, it took its title from Nietzsche, and it pushed further towards film than *Xenogears* had. Reviewing *Episode II* in 2005, *Edge* called the series "the natural endgame for the FMV-driven RPG gaming that SquareSoft's PS1 output prophesied", with "cut-scenes the new king, gameplay elements little more than lines to link the drama". It credited Takahashi with "a tide of closely developed characters", but also with references "ranging from Paul McCartney to the eponymous work of Nietzsche and on through to the Old Testament". The planned six episodes became three. *Episode III* came out in 2006, and the series ended there.

## Nintendo and *Xenoblade Chronicles*

By 2007 Namco Bandai owned 96 per cent of Monolith Soft. In April that year Nintendo bought 80 per cent from it, leaving Namco Bandai with 16. Takahashi's next game began, he told Nintendo's president Satoru Iwata, with an idea on a train: "it would be interesting to have people living on the enormous bodies of some kind of gods". A colleague, Yasuyuki Honne, turned his sketch into a diorama of two giant figures, and that model was all Monolith Soft had to show Nintendo's producer at first. Takahashi was executive director, "involved in every aspect of the game, from coming up with the original concept and writing the script, right through to the debugging stage".

*Xenoblade Chronicles* came out in Japan on 10 June 2010, the first major game from Monolith Soft since Nintendo bought it. Its world is the giants themselves: two colossi, locked still after an ancient battle, with the player's people living on one of them. When *Edge* reviewed it in October 2011, Takahashi was reported to have called the cut-scene-heavy approach of his earlier games a "dead end". The magazine agreed. It found a world that "tells a more engaging story in shape and contour than could have been delivered in mere words", and called the game "a potent return to form for Takahashi" and "a glowing comeback for the Japanese RPG".

## Later games

Monolith Soft stayed with the series. In 2013 *Edge* reported that Takahashi was working on *X* for the Wii U, released as *Xenoblade Chronicles X* in 2015. *Xenoblade Chronicles 2* came out on the Switch on 1 December 2017 and *Xenoblade Chronicles 3* on 29 July 2022. Nintendo now owns all of Monolith Soft, and the company lists Takahashi as one of its directors.
