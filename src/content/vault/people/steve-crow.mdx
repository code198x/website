In the spring of 1986 Steve Crow went up to collect the Golden Joystick for Programmer of the Year from Jools Holland, on a boat on the Thames, ahead of [Jeff Minter](/vault/people/jeff-minter). He had written two games. Both were for a small publisher in Tonbridge, [Bubble Bus](/vault/companies/bubble-bus), and both were Spectrum arcade adventures that *CRASH* had made Smashes: *Wizard's Lair* at 94 per cent and *Starquake* at 96. When he moved to [Hewson](/vault/companies/hewson-consultants) later that year, Hewson sold his next game on his name: "Another fast-moving, richly animated scorcher from 1985's Programmer of the Year, Steve Crow — author of 'Wizard's Lair' and 'Starquake'."

## Fast facts

- **Name:** credited as Steve, Steven and Stephen Crow.
- **Games:** *Wizard's Lair* (1985) and *Starquake* (1985) for [Bubble Bus](/vault/companies/bubble-bus); *Firelord* (1986) for [Hewson](/vault/companies/hewson-consultants).
- **Award:** Programmer of the Year in *Computer & Video Games*' [Golden Joystick Awards](/vault/events/golden-joystick-awards) for 1985.
- **As an artist:** graphics for Hewson's *[Zynaps](/vault/games/zynaps)* (1987); Commodore 64 graphics for [Probe](/vault/companies/probe-software), including *Turbo OutRun* (1989) and *Golden Axe* (1990); lead artist on Shiny Entertainment's *Earthworm Jim* (1994).

## Starting out

*CRASH* met Crow at a trade show at Olympia early in 1985 and described him as "a 19 year old freelance programmer". He had started programming at school, writing his first game about four years earlier on an RM 380Z, the school's computer, and then a few on a ZX81. "It wasn't until I got a Spectrum that I started taking it more seriously." His first published game came out about eighteen months before the interview, from Poppysoft; *CRASH* reminded its readers that Crow had already written one Smash, Poppysoft's *Factory Breakout*.

He planned his games around the memory before he wrote any code. "Before I start I make sure I have everything sorted out so that I know it'll fit in the memory," he told the magazine. "When you're writing 45K of code it's got to fit." He allocated a budget to each part of the game: for the map, 7K; for the graphics, 20K.

## Wizard's Lair

*Wizard's Lair* (1985), at £6.99, was openly modelled on Ultimate's *[Atic Atac](/vault/games/atic-atac)*, and *CRASH*'s reviewers said so: accusations of copying were "bound to fly", but there was "nothing wrong with taking a good idea and developing it, if the result is as good or better". Pot Hole Pete explores 256 locations on seven levels, linked by trapdoors and lifts, collecting the pieces of the Golden Lion while monsters burst through the doors. Gold could be spent on spells for more weapons, energy, keys or rings. One reviewer thought it "much faster" than Ultimate's game. *CRASH* made it a Smash at 94 per cent, and Crow wrote a high-score validating program so that the magazine could catch cheats in its *Wizard's Lair* competition.

## Starquake

*Starquake*, which *CRASH* reviewed in November 1985, was bigger: 512 flick screens of caverns under the surface of an unstable planet that will destroy the universe unless its core is rebuilt. The hero, BLOB (a "Bio-Logically Operated Being"), can walk left and right and fall, but climbs only by laying temporary platforms that crumble away, and a network of six teleports, each with its own password, links the caves for anyone who finds the codes. *CRASH* gave it 96 per cent. One reviewer rated it "an all time great", "one of the best Spectrum games to date, both from a gameplay and technical point of view", and the review praised its colour, which "avoids attribute problems really well".

The readers agreed. In *CRASH*'s poll for 1985 *Starquake* won Best Sound FX, just beating Mirrorsoft's *Dynamite Dan*, and came third for Best Game Overall, behind *Elite* and one percentage point behind *The Way of the Exploding Fist*: "You don't have to be big to be beautiful in the eyes of CRASH readers." The Commodore 64 version was converted by Crow's partner, Nick Strange, who added touches of his own such as BLOB's blinking eyelids. When Crow won the Golden Joystick, *Commodore User* reported, there were no champagne corks at Bubble Bus. "We just went to Macdonalds," said Strange. "We were pretty busy working on the 64 conversion at the time."

## Firelord

Crow's third arcade adventure, *Firelord*, came from Hewson at the end of 1986 for the Spectrum, Amstrad and Commodore 64. Its hero, Sir Galaheart, roams the land of Torot to win back the Firestone from a vain and wicked queen by bringing her the ingredients of a spell of eternal youth. The game added a society to the formula: the villagers hide in their houses, and Sir Galaheart has to go in and trade with them through a screen of icons, or steal from them when they turn away, at the risk of being judged by the Reeve. *CRASH* gave it 91 per cent and counted 500 screens. "Steve Crow does it again!" ran its verdict, though one reviewer noticed how much its layout and graphics resembled *Robin of the Wood*.

## Drawing for other programmers

From 1987 Crow worked mostly as an artist. He drew the graphics for Hewson's *[Zynaps](/vault/games/zynaps)* with Dominic Robinson, and *CRASH* thought his graphics might make Hewson's *Eliminator*, a shoot-'em-up on a road through space, a rival to *LED Storm* as the top racing game of 1989. At [Probe Software](/vault/companies/probe-software) he teamed up with the programmer Mark Kelly on Commodore 64 arcade conversions. *ZZAP!64* wrote in 1989 that the pair "must now be counted true stars of the C64 scene" after *Mr Heli* and *Turbo OutRun*, and in 1990 it found him, "programmer of numerous Spectrum smashes and now one of the C64's top graphic artists", drawing *Golden Axe* on an Atari ST with Rainbird's *Art Studio* and a Genius mouse. Kelly explained why the partnership worked: "With Steve the graphics are usually so good I can't help but try and get them all in."

By 1994 Crow was in California at Shiny Entertainment, the studio [David Perry](/vault/people/david-perry) had founded, and *Edge* listed him as senior artist on *Earthworm Jim*, one of several former Probe staff on the team. In 1997 the Italian magazine *Zeta* described him as working with the team behind *The Neverhood*.

## How he is remembered

*Starquake* outlived its publisher. *PC Zone* wrote in 1999 that "Steve Crow was the first programming superstar as far as I'm concerned", and that *Starquake* was "his pièce de résistance, and an all-time classic". *Retro Gamer* still introduced him in 2013 as the "Starquake creator" when praising his C64 *Turbo OutRun* graphics.
