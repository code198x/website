When Ed Boon sent out the only résumé he ever wrote, at the age of 21, it had a line at the bottom mentioning an "interest in video games". Williams, the Chicago pinball maker, offered him a job programming its tables. "I distinctly remember saying, 'People program pinball machines?'" he told *Game Informer* in 2022. A few years later he and the artist [John Tobias](/vault/people/john-tobias) made *[Mortal Kombat](/vault/games/mortal-kombat)*, the digitised fighting game whose home versions were shown to the US Senate in 1993. More than thirty years on, Boon is still making *Mortal Kombat* games, and it is still his voice that shouts "Get over here!" when Scorpion throws his spear.

## Fast facts

- **Born:** 1964, Chicago.
- **Employers:** [Williams](/vault/companies/williams-electronics) and [Midway](/vault/companies/midway), from the late 1980s until Midway's bankruptcy in 2009; [NetherRealm Studios](/vault/companies/netherrealm-studios), owned by Warner Bros., since then.
- **Pinball:** programmer at Williams for about three years, including *Black Knight 2000*; a voice in about 20 tables.
- **Video games:** *High Impact Football* and *Super High Impact*, then the *[Mortal Kombat](/vault/games/mortal-kombat)* series from 1992, and *Injustice: Gods Among Us* (2013).
- **Voice:** Scorpion's "Get over here!"
- **Honours:** inducted into the Academy of Interactive Arts & Sciences Hall of Fame in 2022.

## Programming pinball

Boon was born in Chicago and studied computer science at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign. At Williams he spent about three years in the pinball department. A pinball program runs the table's rules, lights, sounds and speech, and Boon took the sound seriously. Brian Schmidt, who composed the music and sound for *Black Knight 2000* (1989), later wrote that its programmer, Ed Boon, "was a stickler for detail in all aspects of the game, including audio" and put in "plenty of overtime" to keep the sound, the lights and the play tightly synchronised.

He also lent the tables his voice. "I'm a voice in about 20 pinball machines," he said, the best known being the talking puppet in *FunHouse*. Downstairs, [Eugene Jarvis](/vault/people/eugene-jarvis) and his team were making the video game *NARC*, and Boon "gravitated that way over time". His first video game work was *High Impact Football*. The follow-up, *Super High Impact Football*, was made with John Newcomer, the designer of *Joust*, who told *Retro Gamer* that it was "a very high-earning sport game in the arcades". After it, Newcomer recalled, "Ed wanted to do Mortal Kombat", while their colleague Mark Turmell took the High Impact formula to basketball in *[NBA Jam](/vault/games/nba-jam)*.

## Mortal Kombat

*Mortal Kombat* was Midway's answer to Capcom's *[Street Fighter II](/vault/games/street-fighter-ii)*, made by a team of four led by Boon, its programmer, and Tobias. "Mortal Kombat was put together in an incredibly fast time," Boon told *Retro Gamer*. Its fighters were [digitised](/vault/techniques/digitized-sprites) from video of real actors, and the loser could be killed with a [fatality](/vault/techniques/fatalities). The [Mortal Kombat](/vault/games/mortal-kombat) entry tells the story of the game, its home versions and the [1993 Senate hearings](/vault/culture/congressional-hearings-1993) that led to the [ESRB](/vault/culture/esrb).

Boon's voice is on the soundtrack. Describing how Scorpion's spear move came about, he said: "Something comes to mind, you say it out loud, walk through it, and put it into the game." The move, with its "Get over here!", became perhaps the series' best known, "and it's weird hearing my voice say that line." The creators wrote themselves into the games in other ways too. The hidden fighter Noob Saibot, introduced in *Mortal Kombat II*, takes his name from their surnames spelt backwards, as *Nintendo Power* pointed out in 1995, and *Nintendo Official Magazine* told readers to look closely at the trees in *Mortal Kombat II* to find Boon's face grinning out at them.

## Talking to the players

The sequels made Boon a public figure. In late 1994 he began writing a "Kombat Kolumn" for the British magazine *GamesMaster*, and in 1995 he invited its reviewer to Chicago to play *Mortal Kombat 3* before anyone else. Asked where the fatalities came from, he explained that ideas were never the problem: players sent in letters and drawings full of them. Memory was. *Mortal Kombat 3* had more than 30 fatalities to fit into 18 megabytes of image memory, and a fatality with entirely new graphics for every character would have used up the lot. "The challenge isn't to think of them, I could think of one off of the top of my head, it's to make them feasible." He admitted he was never satisfied with a finished game: "I'm very hard on myself in that respect."

He was also selling the new consoles. *Mortal Kombat 3* was converted for Sony's PlayStation alongside the coin-op, and Boon, "the man behind the arcade game", told *Edge* that "the PlayStation version will be the first one to be released and it's going to be awesome".

## After Tobias

When *Mortal Kombat 4* moved the fighters into 3D in 1997, Boon was wary. He thought that the complex animation in Capcom's 3D *Street Fighter EX* got in the way of the play, so he kept the new game's fighting close to its 2D predecessors, running on a new arcade board called Zeus. Tobias left Midway in 1999 to found Studio Gigante with other members of the team, according to *Retro Gamer*, leaving Boon in charge of the series. The spin-off *Mortal Kombat: Special Forces* flopped. Boon's answer, *Mortal Kombat: Deadly Alliance* (2002), gave each fighter two fighting styles and a weapon, and brought the series back to good reviews. *Deception* (2004) and *Armageddon* (2006) followed. "I think the main reason we've managed to sell big numbers this far has been because we are not afraid to make dramatic changes to Mortal Kombat," he told *Retro Gamer* in 2007.

He also had a sense of humour about the series. On his own website he once announced *Mortal Kombat Trilogy: Nitro Edition*, a game that did not exist, for machines such as the Atari 2600 and the Apple IIc. *Digital Press* included it in a round-up of video game hoaxes.

## NetherRealm

Midway went bankrupt in 2009. Warner Bros. bought *Mortal Kombat* and most of the company, and the Chicago studio Boon worked in became [NetherRealm Studios](/vault/companies/netherrealm-studios), with Boon as its creative director. Its *Mortal Kombat* of 2011 went back to fighting on a flat 2D plane. "We thought it was time to reset the clock," Boon told *Edge*. NetherRealm then made *Injustice: Gods Among Us* (2013), a fighting game with DC Comics' superheroes. *Edge* suspected that it "was the game NetherRealm wanted to make all along". The studio carried on with *Mortal Kombat 11* in 2019 and *Mortal Kombat 1*, which restarted the series' story, in 2023. In 2022 the Academy of Interactive Arts & Sciences inducted Boon into its Hall of Fame.
