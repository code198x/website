Manfred Trenz came into games as an artist. He had trained as a structural draughtsman, and in the mid-1980s he entered a graphics competition in the German Commodore magazine *64'er*. Rainbow Arts saw his work and hired him to draw. "Then I started doing some coding," he told *ZZAP!* in 1991: "this was quite a surprise to them because they didn't know I could do this!" His first game, *Katakis*, was so close to Irem's *R-Type* that Activision, which held the licence, had it pulled from British shops. His third, *[Turrican](/vault/games/turrican)*, won a *ZZAP!* Gold Medal at 97%. He designed it, drew it and programmed it himself; only the music was by someone else.

## Fast facts

- **Born:** 1965, in Saarbrücken.
- **Rainbow Arts:** graphic artist from 1987, then programmer.
- **Commodore 64 games:** *[The Great Giana Sisters](/vault/companies/rainbow-arts#the-great-giana-sisters)* (graphics, 1987), *Katakis* (1988, reissued as *Denaris*), the C64 *[R-Type](/vault/games/r-type)* (with others), *[Turrican](/vault/games/turrican)* (1990), *Turrican II* (1991), *Enforcer* (1992).
- **Later:** *Super Turrican* for the NES, *Rendering Ranger* for the Super Famicom (Japan only, 1995), and the cancelled *Turrican 3D*.
- **Partner:** the graphic artist and designer Andreas Escher worked with him on most of these games.

## From the competition to Rainbow Arts

Trenz first used a Commodore VIC-20 in 1984, bought a Commodore 64, taught himself BASIC and then assembly language when BASIC proved too slow. He told *Retro Gamer* in 2005 that he came third in *64'er*'s graphics competition in 1986; *ZZAP!* had reported in 1991 that he won it. Either way, [Rainbow Arts](/vault/companies/rainbow-arts) wrote asking to see more, and took him on. "At first I did animation graphics, title screens and so on."

His first credit was the graphics for the C64 version of *The Great Giana Sisters* (1987), programmed by Armin Gessert with music by [Chris Hülsbeck](/vault/people/chris-huelsbeck). It was withdrawn in 1988 after Nintendo objected to its likeness to *Super Mario Bros.*

## Katakis

Trenz asked Rainbow Arts' boss, Marc Ullrich, whether he could write a game, "and he said go ahead". He started with the scrolling, "because this was the most important thing", and then took it to Andreas Escher, who designed the game and drew half the graphics. Hülsbeck wrote the music. The programming took seven months, "then I had a vacation, four weeks at home with my parents, because if I spend too long on computers I go crazy!"

The result was *Katakis*, a horizontally scrolling shoot-'em-up with twelve levels. Escher said later that the pair had based it on their favourite arcade games, *R-Type* and *Darius*. *ZZAP!* gave it 93% in October 1988, called it "the closest you can get to R-Type on a humble 8-bit", and summed it up as "at present, the best R-Type variant on the 64".

That was the trouble. [Activision](/vault/companies/activision) held the home licence for Irem's *R-Type*, and the same month *CRASH* reported that *Katakis* "will never see the light of day" because Activision thought it looked "a bit too much like R-Type". *ZZAP!* had to replace a *Katakis* demo on its cover cassette. Rainbow Arts settled by doing Activision's work: Trenz helped write the official C64 conversion of *R-Type*, which Escher said took only six weeks, and the programmers who became [Factor 5](/vault/companies/factor-5) wrote the Amiga one. In Julian Eggebrecht's account to *Edge*, Activision had been impressed enough by the Amiga *Katakis* to let Rainbow Arts keep selling the game if they coded an Amiga *R-Type* for it within three months. *Katakis* came back in 1989 as *Denaris*, with a new second level, and *ZZAP!* ruled that it still deserved its original marks.

## Turrican

Trenz had enjoyed Thalamus's *Armalyte* for a few days, he told *ZZAP!*, then got bored: "same style, same attack waves all the way through." For his next game he wanted "another action game but away from spaceships". He started on the ideas for what became *Turrican* in January 1989 and, once more, wrote the scrolling first. It was the hardest part, "because it was eight-way and very fast". The first two versions did not work once sprites were added; the third did. *The One* asked where the name came from. "Oh, just picked it from the telephone book," Trenz said. "There are loads of good material out there, you don't have to bother making it up."

He drew the levels on paper first and built them with his own level and object editors. The bosses were the hard part, he told *Retro Gamer*: built from many multiplexed sprites, they were difficult to show without flicker. The giant metal fist came from the Beatles film *Yellow Submarine*, where the Blue Meanies attack Pepperland with a huge blue fist. For monsters he drew on "strange books", H. R. Giger's *Necronomicon* among them, along with Japanese games magazines and books about plants and animals.

*ZZAP!* gave the C64 *Turrican* 97% and a Gold Medal in May 1990. The [Turrican entry](/vault/games/turrican) tells the story of the game, its four-month Amiga rewrite by Factor 5 and its conversions. Trenz said he worked only on the C64, Amiga and NES versions; the Super Nintendo and Mega Drive games "had nothing to do with me".

*Turrican II* (1991) went further. *ZZAP!* described its "huge superbaddies: several screens high, beautifully detailed, and yet moving around so smoothly", and its readers voted Trenz the second-best programmer of 1991 while giving *Turrican II* three awards. At Rainbow Arts in Düsseldorf he was working "nine-to-nine" and reckoned "90% of the ideas are mine".

## The ninth sprite

The Commodore 64 can show eight hardware sprites on any one line, and every programmer knew it. In April 2000 the German magazine *GO64!* printed an April-fool interview in which Escher claimed Trenz had managed nine. When Trenz heard about the joke he got in touch to say it could be done:

> All you need to do is reset the X position in a grid line with a grid line interrupt and with exact timing.

As the raster beam passes one sprite, the program moves that sprite further along the same line, so it is drawn twice. The trick "eats computing time like hell", Trenz admitted.

## After the Commodore

Trenz wrote *Super Turrican* for the NES (1992) on his own, the German magazine *Video Games* reported. He told *Retro Gamer* he was "far from satisfied" with it, because a short schedule had cost it much of the earlier games' gameplay and difficulty. His last C64 game was *Enforcer* (1992), a shoot-'em-up in the style of *Katakis* published by the German CP Verlag, which *GO64!* called "another action game classic".

On the Super Famicom he made *Rendering Ranger: R2*, released in 1995 only in Japan, which *Retro Gamer* credited to him as sole creator. It began as *Targa* with hand-drawn sprites; after *Donkey Kong Country* there was, *Retro Gamer* said, "a perceived demand for CG sprites", and the originals were scrapped. A *Turrican 3D* for the PC, announced under the Rainbow Arts name for 1999, was stopped by THQ and never released. Trenz said in 2005 that the *Turrican* rights were "now in the wrong hands".

Public reference works list his later games as handheld and console titles for other publishers, among them *Micro Machines V3* for the Game Boy Color (2000), *CT Special Forces* for the PlayStation (2003) and several Nintendo DS games, and say he co-founded Denaris Entertainment Software in 2004 to work on a *Katakis 2* that has not been released.
