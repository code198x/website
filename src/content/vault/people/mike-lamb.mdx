In May 1989 a *CRASH* reader, Richard Dobbs of Humberside, sent the magazine his own chart: the top fifteen people behind the games Ocean and Imagine had published since 1987. At number one he put a programmer most buyers would not have known by name:

> 1 MIKE LAMB – programmer of RoboCop, Combat School, Renegade, Target Renegade.

In his first three years at Ocean's Manchester offices Lamb had written the Spectrum versions of some of the company's biggest games, arcade conversions and film licences alike, on schedules of a few months each. *Renegade* went to number one; *RoboCop* was at the top of *CRASH*'s own readers' chart in the same issue that printed the letter.

## Fast facts

- **Before Ocean:** *Pool* for CDS Microsystems (1983), and by his own account *Steve Davis Snooker*, also for CDS.
- **Ocean and Imagine, Spectrum:** *Top Gun* (1986), *Arkanoid* (1987), *[Renegade](/vault/games/renegade)* (1987), *Combat School* (1987, with [Andrew Deakin](/vault/people/andrew-deakin)), *Arkanoid: Revenge of Doh* (1988), *Target Renegade* (1988), *RoboCop* (1988), *[Batman: The Movie](/vault/games/batman-the-movie)* (1989).
- **Artists he worked with:** Ronnie Fowles, then Dawn Drake from *Target Renegade* on.
- **Later:** Game Boy work for Ocean; then the United States, where he co-founded Left Field Productions in 1994.

## Pool, and a job at Ocean

Lamb taught himself to program on a ZX Spectrum he had nearly given up waiting for. A friend's success with two published games, he recalled, made him think he could do better. His *Pool* for CDS Microsystems, a 16K machine-code game at £5.95, came out in the middle of 1983. When *CRASH* caught up with it in July 1984 it had been "remaining in the charts all year", and CDS had just repackaged it. The royalties from it and from *Steve Davis Snooker* kept him going for a couple of years, he said, "but I needed more steady work".

He joined Ocean in 1986, aged 21. Ocean's co-founder David Ward remembered him as part of the "first intake" of an in-house team, "just an incredible talent", and Gary Bracey, the development manager, had been told, as Lamb put it, "to get some good people in to turn things around". Lamb admitted he had thought Ocean had "great licences, good marketing" and poor games. "When I first joined Ocean back in 1986 I was 21 and for me it was a bit like going to university," he said. "Ocean had hired a bunch of developers all about the same age, and most of us were away from home for the first time." There was, he told *Retro Gamer*, "a fair bit of competition amongst the programmers to do one better than the next guy and I think it really helped the games."

## Top Gun and Arkanoid

His first Ocean game was *Top Gun*, the licence of the Tom Cruise film, which he set out to make "a 3D combat game on 8-bit machines in three months". It was a wire-frame dogfight, and Lamb split the screen to show the cockpits of both aircraft at once, so that two players could fight head to head. *CRASH* made it a Smash at 90% in February 1987; one reviewer "was expecting some old licensing trash". Lamb himself thought it "had potential" but needed six months, not three or four.

*[Arkanoid](/vault/games/arkanoid)* (1987) followed for the Imagine label. The arcade game was played with a knob, and the Spectrum had none, so Lamb made the bat accelerate towards the ball. *CRASH* gave it 59%, complaining of speed changes "at the most illogical moments". Lamb's view, decades later: "possibly because I tried to make the paddle accelerate towards the ball in order to compensate for not having analogue control. But I think if you played the game a while it was better my way." He also wrote the Spectrum version of its sequel, *Revenge of Doh* (1988).

## Renegade and Combat School

*Renegade* came next, because, he said, it "just happened to be the next thing in the pipeline". His description of the conversion for *Sinclair User* is covered in the [Renegade](/vault/games/renegade) entry: fixed screens instead of scrolling, shared legs for all the fighters, and a game that loaded in one go on a 48K Spectrum. He was proud, too, of making it a one-button game where the arcade machine had three. *CRASH* gave it 89% in September 1987, and Robin Candy praised him for escaping "the one-opponent-at-a-time format typical of this genre". It reached number one in the Gallup Spectrum chart.

*Combat School*, Konami's arcade game of army training events, was a shared job: *CRASH* credited the Spectrum version to Andrew Deakin and Mike Lamb, and gave it 93% in its Christmas 1987 issue. Ocean paid its staff in cash then, and Lamb remembered "counting a couple of thousand pounds in ten-pound notes in the bathroom after we finished *Combat School*". One *Sinclair User* reader thought "only Mike Lamb and his gang" could have made it.

Ocean had written the right to a sequel into the *Renegade* licence, and *Target Renegade* (1988) was designed in-house. Ronnie Fowles had drawn *Renegade*; halfway through the sequel he left, and Dawn Drake took over the graphics. *CRASH* gave it 90% in May 1988, and *Your Sinclair* reported that Lamb had "done a job and three quarters in coding it".

## RoboCop and Batman

Lamb and Drake went on to *RoboCop*, Ocean's licence of the 1987 film. According to Bracey, "the main driving force behind the game was Mike Lamb": it took elements from Data East's arcade game, but Lamb "translated and adapted it for home computers", as a run of different sections instead of one kind of game. *CRASH* gave the Spectrum version 92% in its Christmas 1988 issue. That Christmas Ocean's *Operation Wolf* and *RoboCop* were first and second in the Gallup charts, and by May 1989 *RoboCop* led *CRASH*'s readers' chart. Lamb was characteristically modest about it: "*RoboCop* was OK – nice graphics by Dawn Drake and the final game fitted the licence really well. It would have sold whoever worked on it at Ocean."

The pair then made the Spectrum *Batman: The Movie* (1989), which took 93% in *CRASH*; Lamb also programmed on the Amiga and Atari ST versions, which he thought "an average game... one warehouse section too many in my opinion". He had also written the road routine, running at 25 frames a second, for the Spectrum *WEC Le Mans*. Ocean moved him to *RoboCop* before it was finished, and *CRASH* credited the finished game to the outside team Sentient Software. Lamb still named it as the Ocean game he was proudest of.

Ocean's staff turned up in its games. *The Vindicator* hid their names as anagrams, and readers of *CRASH* and *Your Sinclair* worked out that "Emile Bakma" was Mike Lamb.

The magazine's reply called the list "a timely reminder of the work of the programmers, graphic artists and musicians who wrote the games for Ocean. However much money a software company puts into a game the ultimate responsibility for a game's presentation and playability must largely lie with the programming team involved." The ranking still caused "a bit of controversy in the office": *CRASH*'s Phil King "couldn't understand how Jon Ritman wasn't in the top slot!"

Lamb's own choices had appeared in *CRASH* a few months earlier, when the magazine asked programmers for their favourite games. He picked Artic's *Galaxians*, "written by my friend's brother and... one of the first quality Spectrum games", *Enduro Racer* and *Bubble Bobble*, and named William Wray, Jonathan Smith and the Ultimate team as the programmers he most admired.

## Game Boy, and America

By late 1990 Lamb had moved on to Nintendo's handheld. Andrew Deakin told *Your Sinclair* that Lamb was "working on projects on the Gameboy console for Ocean", and Bracey later confirmed that "we did the Game Boy" version of *RoboCop*: "in fact, Mike Lamb did it". Dawn Drake recalled working with him again on the Game Boy *Darkman*.

By the time he finished *Darkman*, Lamb said, he had "already decided I wanted to work in the US". There he programmed and designed the boxing game *Greatest Heavyweights* (1993) for Sega's Mega Drive, and in 1994 he co-founded Left Field Productions in California with two other former Malibu Interactive staff, John Brandwood and Jeff Godfrey. Left Field made the *NBA Courtside* games and *Excitebike 64* for Nintendo; *Nintendo Power*'s preview of *Excitebike 64* in 1999 quoted "Left Fielder Mike Lamb".
