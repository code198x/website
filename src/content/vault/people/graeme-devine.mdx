In 1990 the talks about CD-ROM at industry conferences were all about encyclopedias. "They were talking about how fast text search was," Graeme Devine remembered, "and all Rob and I could see was the potential to use megabytes of space." He and Rob Landeros filled two discs with a haunted house, and *[The 7th Guest](/vault/games/7th-guest)* became the game people bought CD-ROM drives to play. Devine had got there the long way round: from Spectrum shoot-'em-ups written as a schoolboy, through conversions for Atari and Activision, to a six-month trip to America that he never came back from.

## Fast facts

- **Born:** 1966, in Glasgow.
- **First games:** *Firebirds* for the ZX Spectrum (Softek, 1983), then the Spectrum *[Pole Position](/vault/games/pole-position)* for Atari and *Ballblazer* for Activision.
- **Studios:** Program Techniques (*Xcel*, 1985); Trilobyte, founded with Rob Landeros, in Medford, Oregon.
- **Best known for:** *[The 7th Guest](/vault/games/7th-guest)* (1993) and *The 11th Hour* (1995), and the GROOVIE video engine behind them.
- **Later:** designer at [id Software](/vault/companies/id-software) on *Quake III Arena*; [Ensemble Studios](/vault/companies/ensemble-studios) (*Halo Wars*); Apple; Magic Leap.

## A Spectrum programmer

Devine's name was in the British press by 1983, in Softek's advertisements for *Firebirds*, a 16K Spectrum shoot-'em-up: "Realisation: Graham Devine." *CRASH* called it "a classic phoenix" with "beautiful graphics", and gave the fire rate and movement as "the best we've seen".

At 16, by *Wireframe*'s account, he converted *Pole Position* to home computers for Atari, and was expelled from school for taking time off to do it. Atarisoft's Spectrum version was shown at the Earls Court computer fair in September 1984, withdrawn almost at once after criticism, and reviewed by *CRASH* in January 1985, which found it looked "very like the arcade original" but did not play "anything like as well". Devine then got a job at Atari, *Wireframe* says, and was laid off with most of the staff when Jack Tramiel took the company over.

With a friend he set up Program Techniques, whose Spectrum shooter *Xcel* Activision distributed in Britain in late 1985. *Computer Gamer*, putting his game *Xebec* on a cover flexidisc that December, introduced him as the author of the Spectrum *Pole Position*, a member of Program Techniques "who wrote *Xcel*", and the converter of Lucasfilm's *Ballblazer* for the Spectrum. *Wireframe* tells what happened to the *Xcel* money: a royalty cheque for £28,000, of which, he later found out, his friend's cousin had kept £25,000.

## Mastertronic and America

His next studio, Industrial Concepts & Design, made games for [Mastertronic](/vault/companies/mastertronic). In 1988 Mastertronic's co-founder [Martin Alper](/vault/people/martin-alper) asked him to help start the company's new office in the United States. "He said no one there knew how to turn on a computer," Devine recalled. "He asked me to fly over for six months and help get it up and running." He stayed. At what became Virgin Mastertronic he designed *SPOT: The Computer Game* (1990), a variation on *Othello* for up to four players.

## Trilobyte and The 7th Guest

At Virgin Mastertronic Devine met Rob Landeros, a former [Cinemaware](/vault/companies/cinemaware) art director. They sketched their first idea on a napkin. The aim, as Devine put it to *ACE* in 1992, was "a David Lynch version of Cluedo". Alper liked the plan but did not want a special division inside Virgin, so the pair set up their own studio, Trilobyte, in Oregon, and Virgin published what it made. Devine was president and lead programmer; Landeros ran the puzzles and art.

The hard part was the video. A CD-ROM drive delivered 150K a second, and Devine wrote GROOVIE, the engine that played the compressed footage over the rendered rooms. He also wrote the code to capture the actors, shot on Super VHS against a blue screen from a party shop, frame by frame through a serial port. *The 7th Guest* came out in 1993, and Devine told *Wireframe* that 60,000 copies sold on the first day. By August 1994 *Edge* reported the millionth sale, and Trilobyte had grown from four people to 25.

Devine had no time for the criticism that there was little game between the films. "We didn't aim *7th Guest* at the obsessive gamesplayers — we don't make games for that small and largely sad section of the market," he told *Edge* in 1994. "We aimed it at a mass audience and they loved it."

*The 11th Hour* was harder. Devine said he had asked its director to keep the camera still, and found the footage full of camera movement and "impossible to compress". He rewrote the engine around a wavelet-based codec to fit 65 minutes of video on two discs. The game slipped from spring 1994 to December 1995, the team grew past 60 people, and Devine and Landeros stopped talking. Landeros left to finish *Tender Loving Care* at his own company, Devine's children's puzzler *Clandestiny* sold poorly, and Trilobyte's next projects, *Extreme Warfare* and a racing game, were cancelled. "You could throw a hundred rocks at what caused Trilobyte to implode," Devine said in 2020. He later sold Landeros the company, Landeros said, "for a dollar".

## id, Ensemble and after

In 1999 Devine joined [id Software](/vault/companies/id-software). He had known [John Carmack](/vault/people/john-carmack) since the early 1990s from trade shows: "He was doing *Doom* when I was doing *7th Guest*; *Quake* when I was doing *11th Hour*. He teased that he was catching up with me in real time." When *Edge* visited that year he was looking after *Quake III Arena*, and argued that its bots made it "a classic singleplayer game". In 2000 *PC Gamer* reported him at QuakeCon describing a massively multiplayer game id had considered before choosing *Doom 3*.

He moved to Microsoft's [Ensemble Studios](/vault/companies/ensemble-studios), where he worked on *Age of Empires III* and was a designer on *Halo Wars*. In 2009 he joined Apple to look after games on the iPhone and iPad, and left at the end of 2010: "I am ultimately a game designer that wants to make games." He later ran the games studio at the augmented-reality company Magic Leap, as its "chief games wizard". By 2020 his steadiest income came from his own *Full Deck Solitaire*. "My gravestone will say, 'he made a pretty good Solitaire game'," he told *Wireframe*.
