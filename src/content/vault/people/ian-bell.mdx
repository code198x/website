Ian Bell had already had a game published before *[Elite](/vault/games/elite)*. *Freefall* had come out from Acornsoft the year before, and he wanted to follow it with a 3D simulation game. "It seemed logical to set it in space," he told *Acorn Programs* in December 1984. His friend [David Braben](/vault/people/david-braben) added the trading, and the two Cambridge undergraduates spent the next year writing the game that Acornsoft launched in September 1984. Bell kept working on *Elite* for years afterwards, converting it to other machines, and has since made the original version, its manual and much of its source code available from his own website.

## Fast facts

- **Born:** 1962, in England.
- **Education:** Jesus College, Cambridge: a first in mathematics (1985) and a Diploma in Computer Science (1986).
- **Before Elite:** a version of Reversi for the BBC Model A (Program Power) and *Freefall* (Acornsoft, 1983).
- **Elite:** written with Braben for the [BBC Micro](/vault/systems/bbc-micro); published by [Acornsoft](/vault/companies/acornsoft), 1984.
- **Conversions:** the Commodore 64 version with Braben, and the Nintendo Entertainment System version with Braben.

## Before Elite

Bell went to Jesus College, Cambridge, to read mathematics. He had owned a Tandy TRS-80 since his last year at school and, apart from one term of "computer lessons" in the lower fourth, taught himself. For machine code he recommended "poring over other people's listings". His first professional game was Reversi for the BBC Model A, sold by Program Power: "I do not think it was a great seller," he said. Then came *Freefall* for Acornsoft, a game he describes on his website as the first beat-'em-up. "It was rather an effort to finish *Freefall*," he said in 1984, "but *Elite* was sufficiently interesting to keep us hacking away happily."

## Writing Elite

Bell and Braben met at dinner in Hall at Jesus. They wrote *Elite* in their spare time, in 6502 assembly language, starting with the BBC BASIC assembler. When they disagreed, Bell said, "we would blame the assembler, not each other." They shared the work and agreed to share the proceeds equally.

Bell has published a table of who wrote what. His side includes the sun and planet drawing, 3D movement and ship rotation, the star dust and explosions, the hyperspace and weapon effects, the graphics routines, the scanner code, enemy tactics, docking, missiles, the secret missions and the Trumbles. Braben's side includes the ship drawing, the trading and equipment screens, the charts, the sound effects and the galaxy generation. Bell designed most of the ships, among them the Adder, Anaconda, Asp, Boa, Fer-de-Lance, Krait and Moray, and the dodecahedral space station.

Memory was the limit. "If the BBC had more memory, we would probably still be writing it," Bell told *Acorn Programs*. "As it is, there is not a single free byte in the program." Asked whether all the bugs were gone, he said: "We think we have got rid of all of them, but you can never be sure." Bell said he would save his share of the money. He did not recommend writing games as an easy option: "games will become increasingly difficult to write, with the market becoming more competitive and machines becoming more sophisticated."

## The other versions

Bell and Braben kept the rights to machines other than the BBC Micro and sold them to [Firebird](/vault/companies/firebird). *ZZAP!64* reported that they converted *Elite* to the Commodore 64 themselves while studying for their finals, adding the Trumbles. Braben told *Amiga Power* in 1995 that the pair also wrote a version for Nintendo. Bell regards that NES version, and the Archimedes one, as the best conversions. On his website he explains that the NES was "the most technically challenging port". The BBC Micro version splits its screen with a hardware timer, and the NES had no timer that could do the same job. Busy-waiting for the right scanline would waste time the game could not spare, so Bell wrote "self-timing code" that did useful work, such as clearing character maps, while it counted down.

He also began *Elite II* for the BBC Micro, an attempt to improve the game on the 6502, and abandoned it as the market moved to 16-bit machines.

## Frontier and the dispute with Braben

Braben wrote *Frontier: Elite II* (1993) largely alone. He told *Amiga Power* that he started it "on my own"; Bell's account is that Braben developed it "with some assistance from me", and that he had no part in the 1995 sequel, *Frontier: First Encounters*. Bell's website asks readers not to email him about either game.

The two fell out publicly. Bell criticised Braben's handling of the *Frontier* games in an interview in 1995. By Bell's account, Braben's solicitors objected to parts of it, and a libel writ followed in November 1995. In 1999 the internet service provider hosting Bell's *Elite* pages took them down after a legal threat from Braben over the distribution of the *Elite* files. Bell says Braben stated publicly a month later that he did not mind the files being freely downloadable, and Bell restored the site in November 1999. *Retro Gamer*, looking back in 2005, traced the row to royalties from *First Encounters* and said it "kicked off royally in 1999", with press releases from both sides. Most of this detail comes from Bell's own pages, which give one side of the dispute.

## Later work

Bell says *Elite* "sold well enough to finance many years of hobbyist programming and study of martial arts". His own list of later work includes a voxel-based character animation system for Revolution Software, water, fire and smoke effects for the Sony PlayStation for Pterodactyl Software, and a political and economic adventure game for Oxfam about US policy in Central America, which collapsed. His website still offers emulator-ready copies of the published versions of *Elite*, the flight training manual, Robert Holdstock's novella *The Dark Wheel*, and *Text Elite*, the game's trading engine rewritten in C. On 20 September 2024, which he marked as the game's 40th anniversary, he added the sources of the Apple II, Atari, Commodore 64, NES and unfinished SNES versions, and of his unfinished BBC *Elite II*.
