Jez San's first commercial game was, in his own words, "a Defender clone with nice graphics". His second was *[Starglider](/vault/games/starglider)*, a 3D combat game that made the Atari ST sing and ran on every demonstration machine in Dixons. By 1990 his team had 3D graphics running on Nintendo's black-and-white Game Boy, and within a few years his company, [Argonaut](/vault/companies/argonaut), had designed the chip that put polygons on the Super Nintendo. San was not the only programmer behind any of it, and by the 1990s he was running the company more than writing code, but he was the one who kept asking whether a machine could do 3D.

## Fast facts

- **Born:** 1966.
- **Argonaut:** began trading under the name in 1982, at sixteen; a real company once *Starglider*'s royalties arrived.
- **Games:** *Skyline Attack* (Commodore 64, 1984), *Starglider* (1986), *Starglider 2* (1988), *Birds of Prey* (1992); then as head of Argonaut, *Star Fox* with Nintendo (1993) and *Croc* (1997).
- **Hardware:** proposed the 3D chip that became the [Super FX](/vault/techniques/super-fx-chip).
- **Honours:** OBE in the 2002 New Year Honours, "for services to the Computer Games Industry".
- **After Argonaut:** founder of the online poker company PKR; revived Argonaut Games as a publisher in 2024.

## A BBC Micro kid

San got his first computer, a Tandy TRS-80 Model 1, at twelve and a half, and in 1981 was one of the first people to buy a BBC Micro, *The One for ST Games* reported in 1991. A Saturday job at Microage Electronics, which the magazine called "the first BBC Micro shop", led him and his colleagues to writing books, games and manuals. With his school friend Fouad Katan he wrote a guide to the BBC Micro's disc system, which *C&VG* said appeared in 1982, "well before the publication of the official Acorn manual". In the summer of 1982, at sixteen, he worked alongside Katan at Silversoft. That is the year the [Argonaut](/vault/companies/argonaut) entry takes as the company's beginning, though until *Starglider*'s royalties arrived it was, as San put it, effectively a one-man company trading under a name.

His first commercial game, written in 1984, was *Skyline Attack* for the Commodore 64, published by Century Hutchinson. San called it "a Defender clone with nice graphics, a bit like *Drop Zone* in some ways": a 2D scrolling shooter, with a game of Snake to play while it loaded. Century also published his book on the Sinclair QL, *Quantum Theory*.

## Starglider

San's break came from a tool, not a game. He and Katan had built a programmers' development system, and British Telecom's games business, [Telecomsoft](/vault/companies/telecomsoft), used it to convert *[Elite](/vault/games/elite)* from the BBC Micro to the Commodore 64. "I agreed to help them port *Elite*," San told *Retro Gamer*, "if they agreed to give me my own game deal – *Starglider*."

He started it on a rented Apple Macintosh, moved to the [Atari ST](/vault/systems/atari-st) and [Amiga](/vault/systems/commodore-amiga), and worked on it from 1984 to 1986, living at home on the advance. It had begun as a bid for a *Star Wars* licence that fell through. *Starglider* came out from [Rainbird](/vault/companies/rainbird) at the end of August 1986: fast wireframe vector graphics with hidden-line removal, a missile you steered from its own camera, and a sampled song on the title screen. The ST's sound chip could not play samples, so San opened one up, measured its outputs with a voltmeter and built a table of volume settings that let him play waveforms through it. "Back then this was a major technical achievement," he told *Edge* in 2000.

The game "flew off the shelves", he said, and the royalties went back into the business. Argonaut grew to six people, then ten, and made *Starglider 2* (1988) in solid, filled 3D. Its next project, the flight simulator *Birds of Prey*, took four years. "We just thought of doing a flight sim with *Starglider* technology," San told *The One*. "We kept making it bigger and more realistic… It was commercial suicide, and we're unlikely to do it again."

## 3D for Nintendo

At the end of the 1980s Argonaut turned to Nintendo's machines. According to *Retro Gamer*'s 2006 history of *Star Fox*, Argonaut's team went as far as reverse-engineering a Game Boy, and San then approached Nintendo in Japan with the idea of 3D games for its hardware. "They immediately flew me to Japan to meet with them," he said. "They hired us to do a few 3D games, starting with the Japan exclusive *Eclipse* on the Game Boy, which became *Lunar Chase*. Then we started doing *Starglider* on the NES, which was codenamed *NesGlider*." *ACE* played *Eclipse* in September 1990. "We wanted to program *Starglider* on the Gameboy," San told the magazine. "The Nintendo Gameboy and NES consoles weren't designed to run 3D games."

Nintendo then showed Argonaut its next console, the [Super NES](/vault/systems/super-nintendo). "I suggested the idea that while developing 3D games for Nintendo we might be able to design a 3D chip that would make its game console the first one capable of doing proper 3D graphics," San said. Nintendo paid for the chip. It was called the MARIO chip, for Mathematical Argonaut Rotation I/O, and was designed by Rob Macaulay and Ben Cheese before being renamed the Super FX. It sat in the game cartridge, so players did not need to buy an add-on. Argonaut's Dylan Cuthbert, Krister Wombell, Giles Goddard and later Colin Reed moved into Nintendo's offices in Kyoto to make *[Star Fox](/vault/games/star-fox)* with [Shigeru Miyamoto](/vault/people/shigeru-miyamoto), while the chip and the 3D technology were developed in England. *Edge* put *Star Fox*'s sales at four million copies in 1998, when San told the magazine that working with Nintendo "got us into hardware and I am eternally grateful to Nintendo for giving us that opportunity".

## Running Argonaut

Argonaut chose to stay independent of Nintendo and worked for everyone: *Croc* for Fox Interactive in 1997, early work on Sega's Dreamcast, and processor designs sold as ARC. San told *Edge* in 2000 that the hardware division had been spun off as a separate company, ARC, which was about to go public. The same article put him 198th in *The Times* Rich List, with £150 million. He helped set up TIGA, the British trade association for game developers, and came up with its name.

In the 2002 New Year Honours he was appointed OBE "for services to the Computer Games Industry", described in the list as founder of Argonaut Games plc. He called it "sort of surreal that the government at a moment in time decided to reward someone from the game industry with such an honour". Two years later, in October 2004, Argonaut ran out of cash and collapsed into administration; the [Argonaut](/vault/companies/argonaut) entry tells that story.

## After Argonaut

San's next company was Crunchy Frog, which became the online poker site PKR. It launched in 2006 with a 3D poker table: in 2012 San said his 3D work had carried on "even today with 3D poker". PKR was suspended in May 2017 after admitting it was in severe financial difficulty, and went into administration; PokerStars later refunded its players. By 2012 he had also founded Origin8, a developer of mobile applications, and in 2017 he launched FunFair, a gambling venture built on the Ethereum blockchain.

"I miss being hands-on," he told *Retro Gamer* in 2012. "I keep saying that one day I will start programming again." In August 2024 he and Gary Sheinweld relaunched Argonaut Games as a small publisher, beginning with a remaster of *Croc*. "I'm thrilled to see the Argonaut name back after twenty years away," San said.
