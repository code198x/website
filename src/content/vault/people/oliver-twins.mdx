In January 1984 *Computer & Video Games* printed a listing for the Dragon 32 headed "Road Runner by Philip Oliver". It was the twins' first published game, and they were paid £50 for it, which went towards a BBC Micro. Eight years later their father, Malcolm, interviewed them for *CRASH*. By then Philip and Andrew Oliver had had about 20 titles published, *Treasure Island Dizzy* had been in the charts for 120 weeks, and every Code Masters game put together drew fewer fan letters than Dizzy.

## School, a ZX81 and a TV competition

The twins went to Clarendon School in Trowbridge, Wiltshire. Philip told *Edge* in 2001 that they got their first computer in 1981, when they went with their older brother Martin to buy a second-hand ZX81. Martin soon wanted a machine with colour. The twins taught themselves by changing the type-in listings, found they needed more speed, memory and colour, and took on a newspaper round to buy a Dragon 32.

After the *C&VG* listing they moved to the BBC Micro. Their board game *Gambit*, Philip told *CRASH*, won a television competition run by *The Saturday Show* while they were in the fifth form. They then wrote for BBC Soft, Firebird, Interceptor and Players. Philip's list in *Edge* also names Argos Press and Acornsoft. In 1988 they told *Amstrad Action* they had been close to giving up when they met Richard and David Darling at the PCW Show in September 1986. Instead of going to university, as *Retro Gamer* later told it, they persuaded their parents to let them take a year out and write games.

## Code Masters

The Darlings' new label, [Code Masters](/vault/companies/codemasters), sold games at £1.99. The twins' first game for it was [*Super Robin Hood*](/vault/games/super-robin-hood). "This was a great success and many more have followed," Philip told *CRASH*. *Ghost Hunters*, *Grand Prix Simulator*, *Professional Ski Simulator*, *3D Starfighter*, *Fruit Machine Simulator* and others followed quickly. *Grand Prix Simulator*, Andrew said, "firmly established the place of the Simulator range", even though *CRASH* gave its Spectrum version 42%. By late 1987 *CRASH* reported that the twins' games made up 20% of Code Masters' sales, and the company chose their *Jet Bike Simulator* to launch its £4.99 Code Masters Plus range.

By 1988, they told *Amstrad Action*, they were using PDS, the Programmer's Development System, which Code Masters had sent them: a game that had taken five minutes to assemble now took seconds. Their accountant had also advised them to take on a mortgage, and fan mail at Code Masters was now addressed to "The Famous Oliver Twins!"

## Dizzy

*[Dizzy](/vault/games/dizzy)* came out in 1987 on the Spectrum, Amstrad CPC and Commodore 64, at £1.99. *ACE* reviewed it in its first issue and gave it an ACE rating of 788; *CRASH* gave it 78%. Philip later explained the shape to *Retro Gamer*: "What we most wanted out of Dizzy was to develop a character, which isn't easy when you're dealing with sprites. We felt the best way to achieve this was to make his head as big as possible so we ended up making him into an egg shape. It was also easy to draw."

*Retro Gamer* said the first game sold no better than an average Code Masters title, but kept selling. The twins gave the sequel its own name, *Treasure Island Dizzy* (1988), rather than calling it *Dizzy 2*, and it outsold the first. *Fantasy World Dizzy* (1989) added Dizzy's family, the Yolkfolk. Andrew called it their favourite: they finished it in a month, and it was "the last Dizzy game done by just the two of us". Big Red Software wrote the next adventures, starting with *Magicland Dizzy*. Dizzy also starred in arcade spin-offs; the first was *Fast Food* (1989), a maze game in which he eats burgers and avoids ghosts. In the United States, Camerica published *The Fantastic Adventures of Dizzy* for the NES.

The twins saw Dizzy as a change of direction. "It was just what we were aiming to achieve," Philip told *CRASH* in 1992, "to change the market from simulators to cartoon adventure games in which the player decides what happens next." Andrew was less impressed by the technology: "Dizzy wasn't amazingly clever, technically — *Ghostbusters II* was better."

## Freelance

"Neither Andrew nor I have ever drawn a salary from anyone," Philip told *Edge* in 2001. "We've remained freelance writers," he had told *CRASH* in 1992, and they did not put all their eggs in one basket: they also wrote *The Incredible Shrinking Sphere* and *Ghostbusters II* for [Activision](/vault/companies/activision) at full price. Philip argued that budget games paid just as well: "If you sell budget games for, say, a quarter of the price of a full priced product, you probably sell four times as many so you don't lose out money-wise." They also wrote *The Race Against Time* (1988) for the Sport Aid '88 charity, with Peter Gabriel's "Games Without Frontiers" as its music.

How they split the work changed over time. "Originally, we did work together on each game, dividing the project down the middle," Andrew told *CRASH* in 1992. By then Philip was concentrating on organising, project liaison and the accounts.

## Interactive Studios and Blitz Games

The twins went on to found a development studio, Interactive Studios. A recruitment advertisement in *Edge* in 1998 said it had more than 50 staff and had developed over 100 games, and claimed that the twins' games had once accounted for 7% of all UK game sales, with over 2.5 million sold. By 2000 the studio had been renamed Blitz Games, and by late 2001 it employed more than 90 people, with Philip Oliver as managing director. Blitz Games closed in 2013.
