## Overview

**Philip Oliver** and **Andrew Oliver** — the **Oliver Twins** — are identical-twin British game developers born 19 October 1969. They began programming together as schoolchildren in the early 1980s and shipped their first commercial game in 1985 at age fifteen. Through the late 1980s and the first half of the 1990s, working primarily through [Codemasters](/vault/companies/codemasters), they became one of the British budget-games scene's most consistent producers of commercial hits — most prominently the ***Dizzy*** series, an egg-shaped cartoon protagonist whose flip-screen platform-adventures across the Spectrum, [Commodore 64](/vault/systems/commodore-64), [Amstrad CPC](/vault/systems/amstrad-cpc), and NES sold in the seven-million-unit range across the franchise's lifetime.

The Oliver Twins are, in the canonical sense, the most successful **continuously-collaborating** bedroom-coder pair in British game-development history. Where the [Stamper brothers](/vault/people/stamper-brothers) had a similar arc on a different commercial scale, the Olivers' trajectory was specifically a budget-tier story — fast, high-volume, accessible — and one that produced a body of work several million British, European, and (later) American children played, owned, and remember.

## The early career

The twins began coding aged about twelve on a borrowed ZX81. Like many of their generation, they progressed quickly: ZX81 BASIC; Spectrum BASIC; Spectrum machine code; commercial submissions. Their first published games — *Gambit* and *Road Runner* — shipped in 1984-85 when the twins were fifteen, on small labels.

In 1986, aged sixteen, they signed with the newly-founded **Codemasters** label (run by the Darling brothers — see [Codemasters](/vault/companies/codemasters)) and shipped ***Super Robin Hood*** (1986). The game was a £1.99 release; it sold strongly enough to justify a follow-up. The pattern that would define the next decade was set: rapid development, budget price point, family-friendly cartoon aesthetic, Codemasters distribution.

## The Dizzy era

***Dizzy: The Ultimate Cartoon Adventure*** shipped in 1987 on Spectrum, C64, and Amstrad CPC at £1.99 through Codemasters. The protagonist — an egg with arms and legs, a cartoon face, and an animation cycle that took advantage of the simple silhouette — was an immediate visual hit. The gameplay was flip-screen adventure: explore, pick up objects, solve item-based puzzles, navigate the world.

Across 1987-1994, the mainline Dizzy series ran:

1. ***Dizzy: The Ultimate Cartoon Adventure*** (1987)
2. ***Treasure Island Dizzy*** (1988)
3. ***Fantasy World Dizzy*** (1989) — widely considered the strongest of the series
4. ***Magicland Dizzy*** (1990)
5. ***Spellbound Dizzy*** (1991)
6. ***Dizzy: Prince of the Yolkfolk*** (1991)
7. ***Crystal Kingdom Dizzy*** (1992)

Plus spin-offs: ***Fast Food*** (1989, arcade-style chase), ***Kwik Snax*** (1990, puzzle), ***Bubble Dizzy*** (1990), ***Dizzy Down the Rapids*** (1991), and ***Dizzy the Adventurer*** (1992).

Total franchise sales across all platforms are estimated at over seven million units — making Dizzy the bestselling British game character of the budget era, and the Oliver Twins among the era's most commercially successful authors of any tier.

## How the twins worked

The standing division of labour, by the twins' own accounts in subsequent interviews:

- **Andrew** focused on **graphics, sprite design, and level layout** — the visual side of each game.
- **Philip** focused on **code, engine work, and platform-specific technical implementation**.
- **Both** contributed to **design, story, puzzle structure, and play-testing**.

The division wasn't absolute — both could and did do both at need — but the consistent allocation across thirty-plus games gave the partnership a stable shape and let each twin develop deep expertise in their primary area. The dynamic is comparable to the Stamper brothers' (Tim graphics / Chris code) and to many other sibling-team partnerships in British game development.

## Beyond Dizzy

The Olivers shipped many non-Dizzy games during the same period:

- ***BMX Simulator*** (1986) — Pre-Dizzy Codemasters hit.
- ***Pro BMX Simulator***, ***Grand Prix Simulator***, ***Pro Tennis Simulator*** — the *Simulator* series, multiple titles in Codemasters' second-most-successful franchise of the era.
- ***Italy 1990*** (1990) — World Cup football tie-in.
- ***SuperTed*** — a licensed children's-cartoon adaptation.

Many were budget hits in their own right. The Olivers were not solely Dizzy authors; they were the most productive *Codemasters* authors of the period.

## Post-Codemasters: Blitz Games and Interactive Studios

In 1990, the twins formed **Interactive Studios** (later renamed **Blitz Games Studios**), retaining contractor relationships with Codemasters but moving toward fully-independent studio operation. Through the 1990s and 2000s, Blitz produced games across all major platforms — PlayStation, N64, Xbox, PC — including titles like *Glover*, *Frogger 2*, and several licensed children's properties. The studio grew to several hundred employees at its peak; it closed in 2013.

After Blitz Games closed, both twins moved into separate but related ventures (Radiant Worlds and others), continuing in the British games industry into the 2020s.

## Legacy

The Oliver Twins' specific historical position:

- **Bedroom-coder canon.** Started at twelve, shipped commercially at fifteen, defined a franchise by seventeen. The trajectory is the canonical British 1980s bedroom-coder story, scaled to the budget commercial tier rather than the full-price tier.
- **Budget-tier auteurs.** Many bedroom coders shipped one or two Mastertronic / Codemasters titles and disappeared; the Olivers ran a continuous productive partnership for nearly a decade, producing recognisable, branded, repeatable work. The combination is rare.
- **Family-game aesthetic.** Dizzy's cartoon visual style, accessible difficulty curve, and item-puzzle gameplay were specifically pitched at a younger / family audience than most of the era's commercial output. The franchise's commercial success vindicated the positioning.
- **The continuing-team example.** The Olivers stayed together as a working partnership across thirty-plus years and multiple industry transitions. The continuity is unusual; most bedroom-coder partnerships dissolved or transformed within five years.

## Why the Oliver Twins matter for Code Like It's 198x

Two reasons:

1. **They're the canonical successful trajectory.** A pair of children with a ZX81 in 1982, shipping commercial games by 1985, running a recognisable franchise by 1987. The Project's pedagogical claim — that a learner can build a complete commercial-bar Spectrum game — has the Oliver Twins as the most relatable trajectory it can point to.
2. **Their Dizzy games are the budget-tier reference for what the BASIC track sits adjacent to.** Where Shadowkeep is calibrated against full-price Hewson 1987, the BASIC track is calibrated nearer to the Olivers' production values — accessible cartoon visual design, item-puzzle gameplay loops, friendly difficulty. The Dizzy aesthetic is a useful BASIC-track reference point.
