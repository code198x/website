Yoshiki Okamoto's first game was the one he was told not to make. At [Konami](/vault/companies/konami) in the early 1980s his boss asked him for a game about a driving school. Okamoto thought it would never sell, and instead built a shooting game about aircraft in secret. When management found out, just before it went out for location testing, his boss told him: "You're fired!" Then the test results came in, players loved it, and Konami's president came to praise it. The game was *Time Pilot*, and nobody fired anyone. Okamoto told the story to the Japanese site Denfaminicogamer in 2023. "I thought I was being told to make something that sells," he explained.

## Fast facts

- **Born:** 1961, in Ehime Prefecture, Japan.
- **Konami (from 1981):** joined as a designer; *Time Pilot* and *Gyruss*.
- **Capcom:** joined in 1983 or 1984 as its ninth employee; designer of *[1942](/vault/games/1942)* (1984); producer of *[Final Fight](/vault/games/final-fight)* (1989); head of development in the years of *[Street Fighter II](/vault/games/street-fighter-ii)* and *[Resident Evil](/vault/games/resident-evil)*; later a member of the board.
- **Flagship:** a scenario-writing company he owned, working with Capcom by 1999.
- **Game Republic:** founded after he left Capcom in 2003; *Genji* (2005), *Genji: Days of the Blade* (2006), *Folklore* (2007).
- **Later:** *Monster Strike* (Mixi, 2013), a YouTube channel and a game company in Malaysia.

## Konami

Okamoto joined Konami in 1981, when it had about 80 staff, as a designer hired to draw artwork and characters, and was soon planning games instead. One of the colleagues he worked alongside was [Tokuro Fujiwara](/vault/people/tokuro-fujiwara). After *Time Pilot* he worked on *Gyruss*. *Edge* later called him "the driving force behind *Gyruss*, *Time Pilot*" and the rest of his early hits.

He left after about three years. In the 2023 interview he put it down to pay: he felt the company treated people unequally, with new recruits starting on more than he had, and peers given identical raises regardless of what they had done. *Retro Gamer* has written that Konami "jettisoned" him; in his own account, pay was what decided it.

## Capcom and 1942

Capcom's founder, Kenzo Tsujimoto, promised Okamoto that he could lead development. When he arrived, aged 22, the company had seven employees and no developers of its own; he was the ninth. Fujiwara had been invited separately, and the two of them went together.

His first big game was *[1942](/vault/games/1942)*, released in December 1984: a vertically scrolling shoot 'em up over the Pacific, in which American pilots fight the Japanese air force near Midway. Okamoto said the foreign heroes were deliberate, to give the game a chance overseas. *Edge* later wrote that it "made the company's name", and *Retro Gamer* that it "caused an instant upturn in fortunes". *Retro Gamer* also traced most of Capcom's early hits to just two people, Fujiwara and Okamoto, with "a friendly sense of competition" between them.

## Final Fight and Street Fighter II

By the end of the decade Okamoto was producing rather than designing. He produced *Final Fight* (1989), the side-scrolling brawler Capcom first showed as *Street Fighter '89*, and as Capcom's head of development he oversaw *Street Fighter II* in 1991 and *Resident Evil* in 1996. The producer of *Street Fighter II* was Noritaka Funamizu, as Funamizu himself told *Edge*, with Akira Nishitani and Akira Yasuda as its planners. The press has often given Okamoto more of the credit: *Edge* called him "the producer of *Street Fighter II*" in 1999 and its "spiritual father" in 2010.

Through the late 1990s his name sat at the top of Capcom's credits. The in-game credits of the company's Saturn and Dreamcast releases list him as executive producer of *Mega Man 8*, *Mega Man X4*, *Marvel vs. Capcom* and *Power Stone*, as general producer of *Street Fighter Alpha 3* and *Super Puzzle Fighter II Turbo*, and as supervisor of *Resident Evil 2* and *Dino Crisis*. He also owned Flagship, a company that wrote stories and scenarios for Capcom games, and in 2000 *Edge* reported that he had written the scripts for the first six chapters of *Eldorado Gate*, an episodic role-playing game for the Dreamcast. By 2001 he was a managing director of Capcom, and he announced its support for Microsoft's Xbox on stage that year. In January 2003 *Edge* described him as Capcom's chief operating officer.

## Game Republic

Okamoto left Capcom in 2003 and set up his own developer, Game Republic. Public accounts of its founding date differ, from 2003 to 2005. He explained why to *Edge* in 2010: "I was told to make sequels to our franchises like Rockman [Mega Man] or Biohazard [Resident Evil] or Street Fighter, but I did not join the games industry to spend my time making sequels." In 2005 he put the difference more simply: "Big game makers have to create titles that they are sure will sell, as they have to consider stockholders and yearly sales. However, if you start your own company you can create new titles."

The new titles had a hard time. *Genji* (2005) was a samurai action game for the PlayStation 2. Its sequel, *Genji: Days of the Blade*, was moved during development to the PlayStation 3 and became a launch game for the new console, built, *Edge* said, mostly without PS3 tools or development kits. It was not a success, and neither, beyond a cult following, was *Folklore* (2007). By 2010 Game Republic had about 300 staff in Tokyo, Nagoya and Osaka and was making *Clash of the Titans* for Bandai Namco and an original game, *Majin*. Okamoto argued that Japanese developers needed to aim at overseas markets: "My personal opinion is that we need to go overseas."

It did not work out. In 2011 Kotaku reported that Game Republic had closed its Osaka and Nagoya branches. The company stopped trading in 2013 and stayed on the books as a dormant company until the Tokyo District Court opened bankruptcy proceedings against it on 10 June 2026.

## Monster Strike and after

Okamoto's next success came on phones. *Monster Strike*, released by the Japanese social network company Mixi in 2013, was a huge hit; *Inside Games* later called Okamoto its creator, and in 2015, still president of Game Republic, he talked to *Famitsu* about it. Its success, he said, had matched the middle of three forecasts he had made before launch. He warned against copying it: "People won't spend their precious time on games that are merely imitations."

By 2023 he was running a game company from Johor Bahru in Malaysia and talking about his career on YouTube, where in 2021 he explained why *Resident Evil 0* went to the GameCube: Capcom, he said, spread its big games across rival consoles so that a single failure could not sink the company. He also runs a foundation that supports young Japanese game developers.
