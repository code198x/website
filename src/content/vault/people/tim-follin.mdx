In August 1987 *Your Sinclair* gave its readers a type-in listing that made a 48K Spectrum play three-part music through a speaker that can only be switched on or off. The magazine called it "this amazing three-channel sound routine". Its author was Tim Follin, then sixteen, "the man behind the tunes on Mastertronic's Agent X, and Firebird's spectacular Sentinel". Within a few years he was writing for the Commodore 64, the Amiga and Nintendo's consoles, and reviewers kept saying the same thing about his work: the music was better than the game it came with.

## Mike's little brother

Follin came into games through his family. He was the youngest of three brothers: Mike was a Spectrum programmer, and Geoff also wrote music. Tim told *Super Play* in 1996 how it began: "My brother Mike was already in the computer industry, writing a Spectrum game called Star Firebirds." Tim had been playing with a machine-code sound routine. Asked whether it could play tunes, "I tried, they could, and the tune went into the game." A three-channel routine followed, and with it the music for Mike's next game for Insight, *Vectron*. That was before he left school. After what he called "an enjoyable but aimless year at music college", Software Creations, the Manchester company Mike was working for, then in "a cramped office above a computer shop", offered him a job writing music.

*Vectron* already got players' attention. *CRASH* rated its sound "fab — great when amplified" in January 1986, and a reader wrote in a year later asking why every game could not have tunes like *Ping Pong* and *Vectron*.

## Chords from one bit

The *Your Sinclair* routine is the best record of how Follin's beeper music worked, because the magazine printed all of it. It is 1,340 bytes long and loads at address 40000. Its first instruction switches off the interrupts, so nothing can disturb its timing.

The routine does not mix waveforms. It keeps a countdown for each of three voices, and every pass through its loop subtracts one from each. When a voice's count reaches zero, the routine sends a single short pulse to the speaker, holding bit 4 of port `$FE` low for `C` delay loops and then high for `16−C`, so every pulse takes the same time. Then it reloads that voice's count with the voice's current pitch. A voice with a small count pulses often and sounds high; one with a large count pulses rarely and sounds low. The three streams of pulses share the one speaker, and the ear hears three notes.

The pulse width `C` gives the notes their shape. A narrow pulse sounds quieter than a wide one, and the routine widens and then narrows `C` as each note plays, so notes swell and fade. The [beeper music](/vault/techniques/beeper-music) entry works through the loop instruction by instruction.

The listing's blurb said Follin was already "working on a brilliant new routine for 6 channel sound with chorus bass, 128K snare drum, echo on/off/delay time, portamento, and full ADSR!" A reader wrote in to say the routine "was a joy to hear". In his 1996 *Super Play* profile, Follin listed a "five-channel sound routine" among his Spectrum work, used for *Agent X*, *Chronos* and *The Sentinel*.

## What the reviewers heard

Follin's beeper tunes played over title and menu screens: a beeper routine keeps the processor busy for as long as the music plays, and *Retro Gamer* later called his "processor hungry". Reviewers still noticed them, and not always for the same reasons.

- ***Agent X*** (Mastertronic, 1987, £1.99). *CRASH* gave it 85% in February 1987 and singled out the music: "The sound is the best feature. The title music is without doubt amongst the best 48K music around."
- ***The Sentinel*** (Firebird, 1987). Mike wrote the Spectrum version. *CRASH* found its "sound effects and tunettes are of a high standard, but it's a shame that there aren't a few more."
- ***Chronos*** (Mastertronic, 1987). *CRASH* was unmoved. One reviewer heard only "a strange bit of title sound (rather than music)", and the verdict box said "average 'effects' music". The game got 55%.
- ***Agent X II*** (Mastertronic, 1988). *Your Sinclair* found that, like Software Creations' *Agent X* and *Chronos* before it, the game "looks and sounds marvellous (a good tune by Tim Follin) but falls down on gameplay".

## The 128K Spectrum

The Spectrum 128 added an [AY-3-8912](/vault/hardware/ay-3-8912) sound chip, which plays three channels by itself and leaves the processor free, and Follin's music on it could play during the game. His 128K soundtracks drew the strongest reactions of his Spectrum career.

*Sinclair User* credited "Tim Follin (brother of Mike)" with the music for Software Creations' *Bubble Bobble* (Firebird, 1987) and was torn about it: "a cute soundtrack that you'll enjoy for about two minutes, and then turn off in exasperation. Very clever it is, but also incredibly infuriating." *CRASH* liked it better: "The continuous tune on the 128 version matches the frantic action of the game."

*Bionic Commando* (GO!, 1988) went further. On a 48K machine it had "just sound effects". On a 128, one *CRASH* reviewer called it "the best music I've ever heard on the Spectrum", a "psychedelic 128K tune" that was "different for every level". The same issue praised "Tim Follin's 128K music" on *Psycho Pig UXB*: it "really gets the foot tapping".

The trade-offs show in *Sinclair User*'s 1989 account of how Software Creations built Spectrum *Ghouls 'n' Ghosts*, programmed by Mike "with music and sound effects by Tim Follin and additional sounds by Geoff Follin". The game's scrolling tied up the processor so completely that music could only have been played at 25 updates a second. "Rather than have sub-standard music", the team used three-channel sound effects during play and kept "high quality 50Hz music" for the menu screens.

## Commodore 64 and Amiga

At Software Creations Follin wrote his own music drivers for the C64 and the Amiga as well as the music itself. His name did not always appear in the reviews, but the scores did. *ZZAP!64* gave the C64 *Bionic Commando* 97% for sound in July 1988: "Five superbly psychedelic in-game themes, a brilliantly funky title tune but no spot effects." *ZZAP!* gave *LED Storm* 94%, with praise for its "really brilliant soundtrack", and when the game came back on a budget label in 1991 *ZZAP!* judged the music "merely good by his standard but still brilliant for almost anyone else".

*Ghouls 'n' Ghosts* (US Gold, 1989) was the high point of this period. Reviewing the C64 version at 96%, *ZZAP!* said "the soundtrack, and FX, are simply out of this world with some beautifully produced music urging you on." On the Amiga, the same magazine thought the game less impressive than the C64 one, except for "the gorgeous soundtrack. Tim Follin has provided three excellent, atmospheric in-game tunes plus a brilliant 'folk rock' title tune." In 1990 *ZZAP!*'s readers voted *Ghouls 'n' Ghosts* to the top of their C64 music chart.

The Amiga music was not easy to write. *Amiga Action*, visiting Software Creations in late 1989, explained that the arcade game used a dedicated Yamaha sound chip with a different tune for every level, and Follin had to squeeze it into the Amiga's memory. After a couple of weeks' work it was "shaping up well", he said, "with absolutely no thanks to the Amiga". Asked in the same issue for advice to would-be game musicians, he offered: "Listen to real music and write that instead."

He later told *Super Play* that the C64 had influenced him more than the newer machines, "probably because the [sound] it made was so unrecognisable in real music terms that there was no pressure to mimic outside music".

## Nintendo consoles

Software Creations turned to Nintendo's consoles, and Follin went with it. His *Super Play* discography lists music drivers and scores for *Solstice*, *Target Renegade* and *Silver Surfer* on the NES, and *Equinox*, *Spider-Man and the X-Men* and *Plok!* on the SNES, with *Plok!* written jointly with Geoff. *Nintendo Power* thought the *Equinox* soundtrack "one of the best this reviewer has heard. Play this one in stereo," and said *Plok!* "has one of the best music tracks in video games".

None of it was composed on a keyboard. Follin told *Super Play* that he and his collaborators wrote SNES music by typing numbers for each note's pitch and length into a PC assembler, and kept to that method up to their last project. His verdict: "Never again!" He was not sentimental about the hardware either: "I actually hate all videogame machines."

## Later work

By December 1994 Follin had left Software Creations for Malibu Interactive in Warrington, where he wrote a music driver and music for *Time Trax* on the Mega Drive and was producing CD soundtracks for the Mega CD. He told [*Edge*](/vault/magazines/edge): "I never want to write chip-generated music again if I can help it." He wanted to make films. In 2000 *Edge* found him freelancing, having just scored *Ecco the Dolphin: Defender of the Future* on the Dreamcast, and still wary of how chip music is judged. "Unless you knew the limitations, which only people absorbed into the 'scene' did, the creativity really didn't mean a whole lot," he said. "You didn't — and don't — listen to chip music with objective ears."

According to public reference works, he stopped composing for games in the mid-2000s and later returned as the designer and composer of the video game *Contradiction* (2015).

Players did not forget the Spectrum work. In 1993 *Your Sinclair* reviewed a scene demo that had borrowed "a really fab echoey-whistley Tim Follin soundtrack" to play under its scrollers.
