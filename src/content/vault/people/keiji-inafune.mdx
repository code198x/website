For about twenty years Keiji Inafune was the face of *[Mega Man](/vault/games/mega-man)*. Magazines called him the character's creator, and by the late 2000s he was one of the most senior people at [Capcom](/vault/companies/capcom). He did not direct the first game: that was Akira Kitamura. Inafune was a young artist on Kitamura's team, "then fresh out of college", as *Wireframe* put it. He drew the Blue Bomber's world, redrew the hero for the Super Nintendo, and went on to produce three of Capcom's biggest new series: *Onimusha*, *Dead Rising* and *Lost Planet*. In 2009 he told the Tokyo Game Show that Japan's games industry was "finished". A year later he left Capcom, and in 2013 he asked Mega Man's fans to pay for a new game in the old style.

## Fast facts

- **Born:** 1965, in Kishiwada, Osaka, according to public reference sources.
- **Capcom:** 1987 to 2010, starting as an illustrator on *Street Fighter* and ending as its head of research and development and global production.
- **Mega Man:** artist on the first game and its sequels; designer of the hero's new look in *Mega Man X* (1993).
- **Capcom's new series:** producer of *Onimusha* and its sequels; the public face of *Dead Rising* (2006); came up with the concept for *Lost Planet* (2006).
- **After Capcom:** founded Comcept in 2010; *Mighty No. 9* (Kickstarter 2013, released 2016); Level-5 from 2017 until 2024.

## An illustrator at Capcom

Inafune joined Capcom in 1987. His first job, according to *Edge* and two *Retro Gamer* histories, was drawing the character portraits for the arcade *[Street Fighter](/vault/games/street-fighter)*. That year he moved to the small team making *[Mega Man](/vault/games/mega-man)* for the Famicom, led by Kitamura as director and planner, with Nobuyuki Matsushima as programmer. The team took its cue from Japanese animation, and from Osamu Tezuka's *Astro Boy* above all. "The [Mega Man] character is really based on Japanese animation," Inafune told *Play* magazine in 2004, as *Wireframe* quoted him: "Nintendo games really did not have a huge focus on the characters."

Inafune also drew the art that surrounded the game. In Japan he designed the covers and manuals of the first few *Mega Man* games himself. Capcom's American marketing department chose not to use his anime-style art and commissioned its own, and the result, *Wireframe* wrote, "has long since gone down in history as one of the worst ever".

He stayed with the series after Kitamura left following *[Mega Man 2](/vault/games/mega-man-2)*, which Inafune later remembered as a game the team made in its own time: "we worked really, really hard, you know, just 20-hour days to complete this, because we were making something we wanted to make." The series carried on under producer [Tokuro Fujiwara](/vault/people/tokuro-fujiwara), with Inafune as artist. For *Mega Man X* (1993) on the Super Nintendo he was planner and artist, and replaced the squat 8-bit hero with a taller, more detailed design to suit the new console's larger palette.

## Producer

By the late 1990s Inafune was producing games himself. In 1999 *Edge* described him as a veteran of the *Mega Man* series who had worked on *Resident Evil 2*, and introduced him as producer of *Onimusha*, a samurai action game for the PlayStation 2 whose 30-strong team had largely come from that game. "The purpose of Onimusha was to create a game where players can use the abilities they have built up while playing Bio-Hazard," he said. *Onimusha: Warlords* was an early hit on the PlayStation 2, and he produced the sequels too; *Retro Gamer* lists him as producer of *Onimusha 2: Samurai's Destiny* (2002). In 2002, when Capcom reorganised its developers into numbered studios, *Edge* reported that Studio 2 was run by Inafune.

In the Xbox 360 generation he became Capcom's best-known spokesman in the West. He fronted *Dead Rising*, a zombie game set in a shopping mall, where, as *Edge* reported in 2005, he wanted "practically every object on every shelf" to be a potential weapon. It came out in August 2006 and, with *Lost Planet: Extreme Condition* in December, was one of two Capcom Xbox 360 games that each sold more than a million copies. Inafune said later that he "came up with the concept for the original Lost Planet". By 2010 he was, in *Edge*'s words, "the head of R&D and global production at Capcom". For *Dead Rising 2* Capcom worked with Blue Castle Games in Vancouver, and Inafune described the culture clash to *Edge*: the Canadian team drank beer in the office on Friday evenings, and his Japanese staff "just thought it must be a trap!" He also directed *Zombrex: Dead Rising Sun*, a short tie-in film released in eight downloadable episodes.

## "Japan is over"

Inafune had, *Edge* wrote in 2009, "spearheaded the overseas strategy of Capcom since the early years of the millennium". At the Tokyo Game Show that September he gave his verdict on the show: "Japan is over. We're done. Our games industry is finished." *Edge* added that his comments "were universally supported during discussions we had with other Capcom staff".

His promotion to global head of production in 2010 was, *Edge* wrote, "short-lived": later that year he left to set up his own studio, Comcept. Several *Mega Man* projects were cancelled around the same time, and the main series did not return until *Mega Man 11* in 2018. Speaking to the Japanese site *4Gamer* as he left, in a translation reported by *Siliconera*, he said that as head of development he "couldn't go any higher", and that the job rewarded playing safe: "not doing anything new so as to avoid failing".

## Comcept and Mighty No. 9

Comcept worked with other companies on their games. By 2014 it had co-developed Sony's *Soul Sacrifice*, which *Edge* said had met "a lukewarm reception", and *Yaiba: Ninja Gaiden Z* with Team Ninja and the American studio Spark Unlimited. Inafune also ran a school for young developers, which he called Inafune Academy. "I don't want to see fewer game developers in Japan," he told *Edge*; "I want to see new game developers come up."

In August 2013 he took *Mighty No. 9* to Kickstarter. *Edge* described it as a game for fans of a series whose look was "iconic enough to survive without the name": its hero, Beck, copies his enemies' abilities as Mega Man did. It asked for $900,000, reached that in two days and raised nearly $4 million from more than 67,000 backers. The game came out in June 2016, after delays, to poor reviews: *GameSpot* called it "inoffensively average" and *IGN* "charmless". On the launch-day stream, speaking through a translator, Inafune took the blame: "I'm the key creator. I will own that responsibility." Comcept's other big project of the period, *ReCore*, an Xbox One game made with Armature Studio for Microsoft, was announced in 2015 and released in 2016.

## Level-5

In June 2017 the Japanese publisher Level-5 bought Comcept and made it a subsidiary, Level-5 Comcept, with about 15 staff in Osaka. "I had the feeling that I wanted to be like Hino since around the time I was at Capcom," Inafune said of Level-5's head, Akihiro Hino. In 2023 Level-5 named him producer of *Fantasy Life i: The Girl Who Steals Time*. He left Level-5 Comcept in 2024; the company was dissolved in May 2025, and the original Comcept in January 2026.
