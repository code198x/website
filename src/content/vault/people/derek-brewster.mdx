For nearly four years Derek Brewster was on both sides of the counter. Each month he answered *CRASH* readers stuck in text adventures, and in the same years the magazine reviewed his own Spectrum games: the 3D space battle *Codename MAT* scored 93% in the issue that launched his column. When *Your Spectrum* reviewed his adventure *Kentilla*, its reviewer decided that it was only fair to mark it the way Brewster marked everybody else's. At the end of 1987 he left *CRASH* for Newcastle to help set up [Zeppelin Games](/vault/companies/zeppelin-games), a budget publisher that later became the racing-game developer Eutechnyx.

## Fast facts

- **Born:** Durham, according to *Computer and Video Games* in January 1985.
- **Own label:** Neptune Computing, which first published his adventure *Velnor's Lair*.
- **Games:** *Velnor's Lair*, *Haunted Hedges*, *Starclash*, *Codename MAT*, *Kentilla*, *Jasper*, *Codename MAT II*, *Winter Olympiad 88*, *World Soccer*.
- **In *[CRASH](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine)*:** the Adventure Trail column, from issue 4 (May 1984) to issue 48 (January 1988), 45 issues in all.
- **Co-founded:** [Zeppelin Games](/vault/companies/zeppelin-games), Newcastle, 1987, with Brian Jobling and Martin O'Donnell. Zeppelin later became [Eutechnyx](/vault/companies/eutechnyx).

## From a mainframe to the Spectrum

*Computer and Video Games* made Brewster its "Designer of the Month" in January 1985. It said he first used a computer while studying geophysics at university, where the first game he played was *Colossal Adventure* on the university mainframe. During his three years there he wrote an adventure of his own, which "he admits wasn't earth shatteringly brilliant". He bought a Spectrum as soon as it came out and began writing in machine code, and he told the magazine how long that took to learn: "only one day", adding, "Machine code is very easy to learn. But you never stop learning." (*Amiga Format* later called him "Dr Derek Brewster".)

His first commercial adventure, *Velnor's Lair*, came out under his own label, Neptune Computing, and was then taken on by [Quicksilva](/vault/companies/quicksilva), which listed it at £6.95. By early 1984 he was writing for Micromega. *CRASH* reviewed his *Haunted Hedges* in its first issues, a 3D maze game "marketed by Micromega" with the note "Derek Brewster is Neptune Computing". Micromega also sold his shoot-'em-up *Starclash*.

## Codename MAT

Brewster's biggest game went through several names. *CRASH* reported in April 1984 that he had called it *Invasion 2000*, that Micromega had code-named it "Hank" "for the sake of calling it something", and that he had "wistfully told me that Micromega never used his titles". It came out as *Code Name MAT*, a space combat game that, *CRASH* promised, did not just look 3D but "actually works completely in three dimensional space". *CRASH* gave it 93% in May 1984, and in a July survey of 3D on the Spectrum called it "probably the most sophisticated 3D game yet". A sequel, *Codename MAT II*, came out through Domark in 1985 for the Spectrum, Commodore 64 and Amstrad CPC, advertised as "by Derek Brewster".

In November 1984 *CRASH* reviewed two new Micromega games in one issue, both by Brewster. *Kentilla* was an illustrated text adventure and a follow-up to *Velnor's Lair*. *Jasper* was something else: "an all swinging, all singing, all dancing jump, hop and collect game in the tradition of Jet Set Willy". It scored 91%. *Personal Computer Games* put a *Jasper* demo on its Christmas cover tape, and the same autumn invited Brewster to a games contest in its offices with Jeff Minter, Tony Crowther, Kevin Toms and Steve Turner.

## The Adventure Trail

*CRASH* admitted in October 1984 that it had two weak spots, adventure games and strategy games. The first had been solved when "Derek Brewster kindly agreed to write an adventure column"; strategy went to a separate wargamers' column by Angus Ryall of Games Workshop, and later to Sean Masterson and Philippa Irving. Brewster's column started in issue 4, in May 1984, with an introduction his readers would see many times:

> DEREK BREWSTER has been an ardent adventurer for some time, and not content to merely play the games, he put his programming talents to good effect and produced one of the earlier successful adventures for the Spectrum, Velnor's Lair.

The first column set out how it would work. Readers sent in their problems, and "if we cannot answer your problem we will ask other readers for suggestions". It also started a separate Adventure Trail chart, because, Brewster wrote, adventures fared badly in the magazines' ordinary charts. Brewster rated each game under five headings (*Your Spectrum* noticed this and turned it on him), and readers' pleas for help ran in a section called Signpost, later Signstumps.

He used the column for opinions as well as tips. In November 1984 he argued against judging an adventure by whether it was written in machine code. Machine code was more flexible in theory but slower to write, he said, so it was often the adventures that used some BASIC that showed "originality both in display, theme and plot", and "at a time when the Spectrum has been pushed to its limits, imagination, theme and plot are now the decisive factors". He wrote about adventures made with Gilsoft's [Quill](/vault/tools/the-quill), and in May 1987 he called Gilsoft's *[Professional Adventure Writer](/vault/tools/paw)* "a work of genius". In June 1987 the column grew into a 17-page Adventure Trail Extra of reviews, tips, maps and letters.

## Reviewed by his readers

Brewster's games kept coming out while he wrote the column. *CRASH* called *Kentilla* the work of "our very own Derek Brewster" when Mastertronic's cheap re-release reached number 10 in its chart in January 1987. Other magazines made more of the double role. *Your Spectrum* reviewed *Kentilla* in December 1984 and pointed out that "Mr Brewster writes an adventure column for a certain Spectrum games review magazine, where he gives each adventure a rating under five different headings. It seems fair, therefore, to rate his own adventure in the same way." One of its three reviewers, still stuck in a ten-location section after three hours, marked it 2 out of 5: "I'd have expected something better from someone who tells people what's wrong with their adventures." A second also gave it 2, and the third 4.

*Kentilla* had a further twist. In April 1988 *Your Computer*'s adventure column reported that Mastertronic's budget re-release, "seemingly identical in every respect to the original", had somehow acquired two fatal bugs: a silver dagger the game would not let the player pick up, and a character who would not help at the moment the player needed him.

## Zeppelin Games

*CRASH*'s January 1988 issue announced the end of the column: "sadly Derek Brewster is leaving CRASH after 45 issues as our guide on the Adventure Trail. The veteran programmer is helping set up a software house and just won't have the time to write for a while." In May the magazine reported that he had "left for Newcastle to help set up a new budget software company, Zeppelin Games". Its first Spectrum games were *Frontline* and *Sabotage* at £2.99, and its blue-striped re-release label began "with two Derek Brewster classics", *Codename Mat* and *Jasper*, at £1.99.

According to a 2013 *Retro Gamer* history of the company, Brewster had met Brian Jobling at Tynesoft, where Jobling was working as a programmer, and the two founded Zeppelin in November 1987. Brewster wrote Tynesoft's *Winter Olympiad 88*, which *CRASH* reviewed in February 1988 as the work of "former CRASH adventure reviewer Derek Brewster". *Amiga Format*'s 1992 industry A-Z names the founders as "Dr Derek Brewster, Martin O'Donnell and Brian Jobling". Zeppelin also re-released Mervyn Estcourt's Micromega games *Deathchase* and *Full Throttle*, *Retro Gamer* says "through Derek Brewster's contacts with Micromega". *Retro Gamer* also credits Brewster with *Speed King 2* for the Spectrum, published by Mastertronic.

The new company was noticed. *ACE* printed a photograph in March 1988 captioned "Derek Brewster surrounded by the Zeppelin crew", and in January 1989 *CRASH* called it "Derek Brewster's, Tyneside-based Zeppelin Games" when it signed a sales and marketing deal with Digital Inspiration. The reviews were less kind. *Your Sinclair* wrote in November 1988 that "Derek Brewster's new label has yet to make much of an impact in chart terms".

*Retro Gamer* says Brewster and O'Donnell left Zeppelin in 1989, after which Jobling's brother Darren joined. One more Brewster game came out on the label: the football management game *World Soccer*, which *Sinclair User* reviewed in March 1990, noting that it was "written by someone called Derek Brewster, who Zeppelin describe as 'former celebrity'". Zeppelin later became Eutechnyx, a racing-game developer based in Gateshead. Public sources do not record Brewster's work after 1990.
