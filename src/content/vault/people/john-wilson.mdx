## Overview

John Wilson of Rochdale wrote text adventures, argued with magazine columnists in print, and founded **[Zenobi Software](/vault/companies/zenobi-software)**, which published hundreds of independently written adventures long after the commercial industry had abandoned the form. The magazines knew him by a nickname they gave him: **the Rochdale Balrog**.

## Fast facts

- **Known as:** the Rochdale Balrog.
- **Wrote:** *Fuddo and Slam*, *An Everyday Tale of a Seeker of Gold*, and many more, most of them written with [the Quill](/vault/tools/the-quill).
- **Founded:** Balrog Software, then [Zenobi Software](/vault/companies/zenobi-software).
- ***Your Sinclair*** on *Fuddo and Slam*: "a good enough romp from the Rochdale Balrog, and it'll be snapped up by **his many fans**" — with the reviewer noting "**JW is adept at handling that utility**".

## A nickname that stuck

*Your Sinclair*'s adventure column treated him as a recurring character. One issue runs a football-terrace chant at a rival correspondent — "**One Rochdale Balrog… There's only one Rochdale Balrog**" — and another records a genuine dispute:

> The Rochdale Balrog wants to settle up with me for asking if the free ferry-ride in *Golden Eggcup* was an adventure first. No, says the Balrog, as there's one in **his game of 18 months ago, *An Everyday Tale of a Seeker of Gold***… The Balrog dares to suggest that the only reason I couldn't remember was because I didn't get that far in the game!

That is the adventure scene in one exchange: a columnist and an author, arguing in public about who did something first, with the readership as the jury. Nobody involved is a corporation.

## Why Zenobi mattered

By about 1988 text adventures had stopped being commercially interesting. The machines could do graphics, the shops wanted arcade games, and the big publishers left.

The genre did not die, because **the tools had escaped**. [The Quill](/vault/tools/the-quill) and its successors let anyone with a Spectrum write a full adventure without knowing assembly, and hundreds of people did. What that scene needed was not a publisher in the ordinary sense but somebody to duplicate tapes, take cheques and print an inlay — and Wilson did it, for years, from Rochdale.

Zenobi is therefore an unusual thing in this Vault: a publisher whose function was closer to **a distribution service for a community** than to a software house. Its catalogue is largely other people's work, sold cheaply by post, in a genre the market had written off.

## Why he matters

Wilson is the entry for **the amateur scene as a durable structure**, not a phase people passed through on their way to professional work. Most of the Vault's British story is about companies growing, being bought, and disappearing. This is the other kind of story: a genre kept alive by one person with a duplicating deck and a mailing list, outlasting nearly all of them.

He also shows something about the record. Wilson has **no credit row anywhere in this library's structured data** — his games were never reviewed in a box with an `Author:` field. Everything above comes from running prose in an adventure column, which is where this part of British computing was written down at all.
