Koji Igarashi grew up in Shirakawa, in Fukushima Prefecture, and as a boy he could see the ruins of Komine Castle from his bedroom window. As a teenager, he told *Eurogamer*, he took a cheap camcorder into the ruined grounds most mornings and filmed a little further in each time. He spent much of his working life doing something similar at Konami: *Castlevania: Symphony of the Night* (1997), the game he is known for, is one enormous castle that the player explores room by room, and its structure became the pattern for a decade of *Castlevania* games he produced. Fans called them "Igavanias". When Konami stopped making them, he left, asked players for the money to make another, and got eleven times what he asked for.

## Fast facts

- **Born:** 1968, in Fukushima Prefecture.
- **Konami:** joined straight from university at the start of the 1990s; left on 15 March 2014.
- **Early work:** programming on Konami's PC Engine games, including a *TwinBee*; the story of the dating game *Tokimeki Memorial*.
- **Symphony of the Night (1997):** assistant director, scenario writer and programmer.
- **Castlevania producer:** from the early 2000s, on the Game Boy Advance, PlayStation 2 and DS; director of *Dawn of Sorrow* (2005).
- **Bloodstained:** Kickstarter from 11 May to 13 June 2015, $5,545,991 from 64,867 backers against a $500,000 goal; *Ritual of the Night* released in 2019, and *The Scarlet Engagement* due in 2027.

## From BASIC to Konami

Igarashi's family could not afford a computer. After seeing an arcade game running on an older friend's machine, he taught himself BASIC from enthusiast magazines, writing games on the friend's computer and on a display model in a local electronics shop and leaving them there for other people to play. The first was a shoot-'em-up based on *Gundam*. When BASIC became too slow, he borrowed a book on assembly language from a friend, which, he admitted, he never gave back.

"As soon as I graduated from college, I went straight into Konami," he told *Wired*. His first project there was not a game: Konami had just set up a business-software division and gave his group of new graduates a business training simulation to write. They loved *Fire Emblem*, he said, so they "interpreted the instruction as a brief to make a business-themed Fire Emblem game". It was cancelled after a year. He moved to a team writing programming tools for the PC Engine, working on Konami's *[TwinBee](/vault/games/twinbee)* and *[Gradius](/vault/games/gradius) II* conversions for that console.

Then, although he was a programmer, he was told to write the story for *Tokimeki Memorial*, a dating game set in a high school. His girlfriend, later his wife, worked in the next office on *Castlevania: Rondo of Blood*, and she helped him with plot ideas and took him round clothes shops to research places for the game's dates. *Tokimeki Memorial* was a hit, which Igarashi described as "a social phenomenon at the time". His boss wanted a sequel. Igarashi said he had run out of romance, and was shown a list of Konami's projects and told to pick one. He pointed at *Castlevania*.

## Symphony of the Night

*[Castlevania](/vault/games/castlevania)* was made at Konami's headquarters in Kobe. *[Symphony of the Night](/vault/games/symphony-of-the-night)* was being made in Tokyo, where Igarashi worked, and so it counted as a spin-off. "Because of that we were allowed free rein to do pretty much whatever we wanted," he said. Its director was Toru Hagihara, who had directed *Rondo of Blood*. Igarashi was assistant director, and he also wrote some of the scenario and some of the program. "It could be argued that he had the most telling influence over proceedings," *Retro Gamer* wrote.

The team took *Super Metroid* as its model and gave up the stage-by-stage structure of the earlier games for one large castle. Igarashi wanted to change "the impression that Castlevania was this difficult-to-access action game", so the game borrowed from role-playing games: "we agreed that users should receive something good when beating enemies. So I thought of adding Experience Points to the game." The hero was not a whip-carrying Belmont but Alucard, Dracula's son, which worried Igarashi: "I presumed that those who had been fans for a long time would be angry." The Japanese title was *Akumajō Dracula X*; "I used 'X' to show that it would be apart from the main stream of the series," he said, "but surprisingly, it has now become the mainstream."

The game had to last. Igarashi had seen used copies of shorter *Castlevania* games piling up in a bargain bin, and one of his team suggested a way to double its length cheaply: "If we flip the castle upside down during the game then we'd have twice as much content." The work was long. He remembered being at his desk when the temple bells rang in the new year of 1996. The game came out in Japan in March 1997. It became a best-seller in America but sold badly in Europe, where *Retro Gamer* put the first PAL shipment at around 15,000 copies, many of them discounted before they sold.

*Wireframe* has noted that *Castlevania II: Simon's Quest* (1987), with its open world, inspired Igarashi's work on *Symphony*, even though he has said that game left a poor impression on Japanese players.

## Producing the series

Igarashi became the producer of the whole *Castlevania* series. Konami's first Game Boy Advance *Castlevania*, *Circle of the Moon* (2001), came from another team, but he produced the next two, *Harmony of Dissonance* (2002) and *Aria of Sorrow*, in *Symphony*'s mould. *Edge* later described them as the games "through which Igarashi has quietly continued the series' 2D bloodline".

At the same time he tried to take the series into three dimensions. Introducing *Castlevania: Lament of Innocence* for the PlayStation 2 in 2003, *Edge* reported his "modest goal": "to have a copy of Castlevania in every single household", which he thought needed 3D. The results disappointed. "We certainly got a lot of feedback from the press about what was missing in Lament," he told *Electronic Gaming Monthly* in 2005, promising "huge interconnected maps" in its sequel, *Curse of Darkness*. Looking back in 2014, he said that moving the design into 3D brought "enormous pressure" to raise the graphical detail, and camera problems that needed a film director's eye.

The 2D games moved to the Nintendo DS. Igarashi directed *Dawn of Sorrow* (2005). "Much like in *Symphony of the Night*, I wanted to give players a little bit of freedom," he told *Electronic Gaming Monthly*, and the magazine quoted him that year: "I feel like the [Nintendo] DS is the last fortress of 2D gaming." *Portrait of Ruin* followed in 2006 and *Order of Ecclesia* in 2008. In 2010 he produced *Harmony of Despair*, a 2D *Castlevania* for Xbox Live Arcade, in the same year that a 3D reboot of the series, *Lords of Shadow*, was made by the Spanish studio MercurySteam with [Hideo Kojima](/vault/people/hideo-kojima) as executive producer.

At press events he dressed the part. *Wired* described him turning up at E3 "brandishing a fedora and a whip".

## Metroidvania

Igarashi's games gave the [Metroidvania](/vault/genres/metroidvania) genre half its name, but he did not coin the word. He told *Eurogamer* in 2014 that he had first heard it the year before, when he saw a Facebook group dedicated to his game. The rival nickname "Igavania", after him, is used in the same way; *Wireframe* filed *Bloodstained* under that genre.

## Leaving Konami

Konami turned towards mobile games. Igarashi moved to the mobile side of the company to try to make console-style games for phones, and, *Wired* reported, "after years there, he didn't release a single game". "It was very hard to try and find that fit," he said. In 2023 he told a conference audience in Monaco that this was when "I heard the voice of a devil inside me telling me to quit."

He had never worked anywhere else, and he took his cue from [Keiji Inafune](/vault/people/keiji-inafune), who had left Capcom and used Kickstarter to fund a spiritual successor to *[Mega Man](/vault/games/mega-man)*. "Seeing Inafune's example showed me that fans have power," Igarashi said. His last day at Konami was 15 March 2014, and he announced his departure at the Game Developers Conference in San Francisco that month. "I'm extremely nervous," he told *Eurogamer*. "I have a family and, to be honest, lifetime employment is attractive." He became a founder of a new studio, ArtPlay. [Konami](/vault/companies/konami) lost Kojima the following year.

## Bloodstained

On 11 May 2015 Igarashi launched a Kickstarter campaign for *Bloodstained: Ritual of the Night*, which *Wired* called "essentially a classic Castlevania without Konami's trademarks". The game already had investment behind it. The $500,000 goal was meant to enlarge the budget, pay for PlayStation 4 and Xbox One discs, and build a connection between Igarashi and Western players. It was reached within four hours. When the campaign closed on 13 June 2015, 64,867 backers had pledged $5,545,991, with more coming in through PayPal.

A smaller, 8-bit-style spin-off, *Bloodstained: Curse of the Moon*, made with Inti Creates, came out first, in 2018. *Ritual of the Night*, developed by ArtPlay and published by 505 Games, followed in June 2019. *Wireframe* reviewed it under the genre heading "Igavania", called it "a Castlevania game in all but name" and gave it 74%: its castle was "a treat to explore", but it was held back by "a disappointing slew of bugs and glitches" and puzzles so vague that the reviewer wandered for an hour looking for a trigger he had missed. "There's no denying ArtPlay succeeded in creating exactly what it said it was going to," the review concluded.

In June 2025, 505 Games announced *Bloodstained: The Scarlet Engagement*, a 2.5D action role-playing game set in the 16th century and produced by Igarashi, for 2026. In August 2026 Igarashi said in a video message that it would be delayed to 2027: "Currently, the development of the game has entered its final stages."
