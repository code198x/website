In 1993, players of the PC version of *Frontier: Elite II* could find advertising hoardings in its space ports announcing "Coming soon... Chris Sawyer's Transport Game". Sawyer had written that PC version, converting [David Braben](/vault/people/david-braben)'s Amiga original, and he was using it to advertise his own next game. The game became *[Transport Tycoon](/vault/games/transport-tycoon)*, and five years later *[RollerCoaster Tycoon](/vault/games/roller-coaster-tycoon)* made him one of the best-selling game designers of his time. He wrote the program code of both himself, in x86 assembly language, long after most of the industry had moved to compiled languages.

## Fast facts

- **Born:** October 1967, according to Companies House.
- **Education:** a degree in Computer Science and Microprocessor Systems from the University of Strathclyde in Glasgow.
- **Early machines:** the Camputers Lynx, the Memotech MTX and the [Amstrad CPC](/vault/systems/amstrad-cpc).
- **Conversions to the PC, 1988–1993:** *Virus*, *Elite Plus*, *Campaign*, *Birds of Prey*, *Dino Dini's Goal* and *Frontier: Elite II*.
- **Own games:** *[Transport Tycoon](/vault/games/transport-tycoon)* (1994), *[RollerCoaster Tycoon](/vault/games/roller-coaster-tycoon)* (1999), *RollerCoaster Tycoon 2* (2002) and *Chris Sawyer's Locomotion* (2004).
- **Programming:** x86 assembly language, written with Microsoft's Macro Assembler.

## Starting out

Sawyer first programmed at school, which had a Commodore PET and a Research Machines 380Z. "I started writing little BASIC games on those during the lunch hour just for fun," he recalled, before moving on to machine code. His own first computer was a Camputers Lynx, and then came a Memotech MTX. His website dates his start in the industry to 1983, "writing games in Z80 machine code on the Memotech MTX". He later named *Missile Kommand*, "blatantly based on a similarly-named game", as probably the first of his MTX games to be published. The small publishers of those years did not always pay: after one company's first "(small) royalty cheque", he said, "I never received anything further".

At university he moved to an Amstrad CPC 6128 and kept writing games. In January 1987 *Computer Gamer* previewed *Ziggurat*, an isometric maze game for the Amstrad set in a Mesopotamian pyramid and devised by the Tigress team of Dave Bishop for [Ariolasoft](/vault/companies/ariolasoft); the next month *Amtix* credited its programming to Chris Sawyer. Sawyer remembered Ariolasoft as the company "who had published one of my CPC games", and which promised him a job once he graduated. "Ariolasoft was struggling and downsizing, and the job offer came to nothing."

He spent four years studying at Strathclyde, in the Livingstone Tower in Glasgow, whose top floors then held the computer science department. The tower later turned up among the buildings of *Transport Tycoon*.

## Converting other people's games

From 1988 to 1993 Sawyer worked on PC versions of games written for other computers, mostly the Amiga. The PC was, as he put it, "quite a limiting machine" with no graphics co-processor, so "you were really having to push the main processor". He enjoyed that: "it didn't matter that it was somebody else's game".

The work included *Elite Plus*, the PC version of *[Elite](/vault/games/elite)*. When *Computer Gaming World* reviewed it in October 1991, the credits box named Chris Sawyer as designer and MicroPlay as publisher, at $49.95; later advertisements credited "Elite Plus programming" to him. He then converted *Frontier: Elite II*. Asked by *Edge* in 1994 whether he had written the Amiga version, Braben said he had, and that "Chris Sawyer did the PC version". Sawyer later said he had added texture mapping to its 3D graphics, "before PC graphic cards had any kind of hardware to help with 3D texture mapping". His website also lists *Virus*, *Campaign*, *Birds of Prey* and [Dino Dini](/vault/people/dino-dini)'s *Goal*.

## Transport Tycoon

*[Transport Tycoon](/vault/games/transport-tycoon)* grew out of an isometric game world Sawyer had built for himself. "One day I decided it would be fun to try having little trains running around in my 3D isometric game world," he said. He worked on it alongside his conversions during 1993 and full-time from the end of that year, and paid for it himself from his conversion earnings. Simon Foster drew the graphics on the understanding that he would be paid once the game found a publisher. Asked whether anybody else worked on the code, Sawyer answered: "No, just me."

MicroProse, which had published *Elite Plus*, released the game in November 1994 and gave it the name *Transport Tycoon*, tying it to its own *Railroad Tycoon*. *Transport Tycoon Deluxe* followed at the end of 1995.

## RollerCoaster Tycoon

His next project began in 1996 as a sequel to *Transport Tycoon*. He abandoned it and changed the code "to handle roller coasters instead of transport vehicles". The game was called *White Knuckle* throughout its development, until Hasbro Interactive, which was to publish it, bought MicroProse and took the chance to make it another *Tycoon*. *[RollerCoaster Tycoon](/vault/games/roller-coaster-tycoon)* came out in the spring of 1999. Sawyer described it as "99% written in x86 assembler/machine code (yes, really!), with a small amount of C code used to interface to MS Windows and DirectX". Allister Brimble wrote its music and later accounts credit its graphics to Simon Foster.

It became the best-selling PC game in America in 1999, with 719,635 copies according to the retail tracker PC Data, and sold another 1,251,770 there in 2000. When Sawyer sued Atari in 2005, the trade press reported that the series had sold nearly nine million units worldwide by early 2005.

Sawyer wrote *RollerCoaster Tycoon 2* alone as well, and Infogrames released it in 2002. The third game, in full 3D, went to David Braben's [Frontier Developments](/vault/companies/frontier-developments), with Sawyer giving "input and advice".

## Locomotion and the dispute with Atari

The long-promised successor to *Transport Tycoon*, *Chris Sawyer's Locomotion*, came out through Atari in October 2004. It was built on the *RollerCoaster Tycoon* engine, and *Computer Gaming World* was harsh. Its reviewer, Di Luo, found it hard "to figure what's improved in the last decade", and summed it up as "a 10-year-old game with a 5-year-old coat of paint".

In November 2005 Sawyer sued Atari, the former Infogrames, for royalties. According to *Gamasutra*'s report at the time, he claimed about $4.8 million, saying forensic accountants had found improper deductions from his royalty statements. Both *Gamasutra* and the trade magazine *Develop* reported that his games had brought Atari about $180 million, of which Sawyer had received about $30 million. The case was to be heard in London after Atari failed to move it to New York. *Develop* reported in February 2008 that the two had settled out of court, on terms that were not made public.

## Return to his games

Sawyer set up a company, 31X, in 2010, at first "only as a holding company for the Transport Tycoon IP". With the developer Origin8 it turned into a studio for new versions of his games. *Transport Tycoon* came to iOS and Android in 2013, rewritten because the original assembly code could not simply be recompiled for phones. *RollerCoaster Tycoon Classic* followed in 2016 for phones and in 2017 for Windows and macOS.

On 1 November 2024 Atari announced that it had acquired *Transport Tycoon* in an agreement with Sawyer. Atari already published *Locomotion* and the *RollerCoaster Tycoon* series.
