Yuji Horii came to games as a writer. He was a freelance journalist who taught himself to program on his own computer, and a programming contest run by the publisher [Enix](/vault/companies/enix) gave him his start. His first game for Enix was a detective adventure; his second big idea was *[Dragon Quest](/vault/games/dragon-quest)* (1986), which took the dungeons and statistics of American computer role-playing games and made them something anyone could play on a Famicom. *Edge* credits it with laying out "the fundamentals of the Japanese RPG". Horii designed and wrote it, and he was still in charge of the series a quarter of a century later. His rule for every new game, he told *Edge* in 2010, has not changed: "it must be easy to play."

## Fast facts

- **Born:** 1954, in Hyōgo Prefecture, Japan.
- **Before games:** a freelance writer for newspapers and magazines.
- **Break:** a game programming contest run by Enix in the early 1980s.
- **First Enix game:** *The Portopia Serial Murder Case* (1983), for the NEC PC-6001.
- **Known for:** designer and scenario writer of the [Dragon Quest](/vault/games/dragon-quest) series from 1986.
- **Also:** *[Chrono Trigger](/vault/games/chrono-trigger)* (1995), with [Hironobu Sakaguchi](/vault/people/hironobu-sakaguchi) and the artist Akira Toriyama.
- **Company:** Armor Project, which *Edge* described in 2010 as a one-man production outfit.
- **Honours:** the Game Developers Choice Awards' Lifetime Achievement Award (2022); the Order of the Rising Sun (2025).

## A writer who taught himself to program

Horii started programming on an NEC PC-6001, *Retro Gamer* recorded, learning BASIC and then altering the code of commercial games. A *Retro Gamer* feature on famous designers' early work listed what came out of it: an address book, a hacked version of *Star Trek*, a fortune-telling program and a tennis game. He was earning his living as a freelance writer at the time, and when he won Enix's contest, in *Edge*'s words, he "got a foot in the door with the publisher with which he's remained until today". Accepting a lifetime achievement award in 2022, he put it simply: "I taught myself how to code, and started making my own games for fun."

He also told the former *Computer and Video Games* editor Paul Davies what drew him to the Famicom: its games were like seeing manga come to life.

## Portopia

His first game for Enix, *Portopia Renzoku Satsujin Jiken* (*The Portopia Serial Murder Case*), came out in 1983 for the PC-6001. *Edge* explains that Horii felt compelled to write it when he realised how popular the text adventure was in America and how rare it was in Japan. The player investigates a murder by typing commands, and in his 2022 speech Horii gave away its twist: "In *Portopia*, the person giving you the 'What will you do next?' command prompts is actually the murderer."

The game was a success, and for its Famicom version Horii worked with Koichi Nakamura, another winner of the Enix contest, who went on to found [Chunsoft](/vault/companies/chunsoft). It left a mark on at least one future designer. Asked in 2003 to choose three games to be stranded with, [Hideo Kojima](/vault/people/hideo-kojima) picked *Portopia*: "An adventure-game classic by Mr. Yuji Horii. This game taught me the potential of the game medium to deliver drama and messages."

## Dragon Quest

According to *Edge*'s history of the series, it was Nakamura who proposed to Enix that he would like to make a role-playing game, and a manga editor, Kazuhiko Torishima, who had worked with Akira Toriyama, approached Horii to join the project. Horii designed it and wrote the scenario, Toriyama drew the characters and monsters, Koichi Sugiyama wrote the music and Chunsoft programmed it. The famous first monster came from Horii's brief: he asked for a "pool of slime", and Toriyama drew a smiling blue teardrop.

Horii's aim was to make the role-playing game approachable. "At the first planning meeting we have on the main branch of the Dragon Quest games I remind myself that it must be easy to play," he told *Edge*. "There has to be warmth and humanity in the game. Anyone should be able to pick up and play them." He meant the tone as much as the controls. "If you play a really dark RPG with moody visuals, then the player himself will have a dark heart," he told *Electronic Gaming Monthly* in 2005. In the same year *Edge* asked him about "Horii-isms", puzzles so obscure that only their designer could solve them. He laughed and denied there was any such thing: the best kind of puzzle, he said, is one that makes the person who solves it think they are the only one who could have.

The stories grew more personal. *Dragon Quest V* follows its hero from childhood, through the death of his father, to fatherhood of his own. "It's emotionally moving for people playing the game because everyone at one time is a kid, and many become parents, so it's something everyone can relate to," Horii said. Asked by *Retro Gamer* to choose a favourite, he picked *Dragon Quest VI*, whose story moves between reality and dreams, while admitting that some players found it confusing.

Each new game was a national event in Japan. *Nintendo Power* reported that fans queued for *Dragon Quest VI* when it went on sale on 9 December 1995, and that Enix expected to sell three to four million cartridges in its first few months. The games also took longer to make. When Enix moved *Dragon Quest VII* from Nintendo's machines to the PlayStation, Nintendo's president, Hiroshi Yamauchi, played down the loss to *Edge* in 1997 by pointing out that "it will take four years for Yuji Horii to develop a new Dragon Quest title", and asking who could be sure Horii would not "have a slump, or an illness" in that time. *Edge* found the argument unconvincing. *Dragon Quest IX*, Horii said later, took five to six years.

## Beyond Dragon Quest

In 1995 Horii and Toriyama worked for Enix's rival, [Square](/vault/companies/square), on *[Chrono Trigger](/vault/games/chrono-trigger)*, a time-travel role-playing game made with *Final Fantasy*'s Hironobu Sakaguchi. Reviewing the American version, *Edge* credited its "packed storyline" to "the most respected RPG author in Japan, Yuji Horii". He also taught: in 1994 *Nintendo Power* listed him, with Sugiyama, among the professionals lecturing at the Enix Game School, which took 150 students a year.

## Later years

After Square and Enix merged in 2003, Horii carried on as the series' creator through Armor Project. For *Dragon Quest VIII* (2004) he worked with the developer Level-5, whose president, Akihiro Hino, told *Edge* that *Dragon Quest III* had inspired him to become a game creator. Horii said the move to 3D was what he had wanted all along: "even 20 years ago when I created the very first Dragon Quest, this is the kind of game I was trying to make. Of course, the hardware wasn't ready back then." For the game's English version he insisted on approving every naming choice and change to the text himself, *Edge* later reported, and the localisation team won his trust over time.

By 2010, when *Edge* met him at Square Enix's Tokyo offices, the nine main games had sold about 35 million copies, 53 million with the spin-offs, and Armor Project was overseeing *Dragon Quest X* for the Wii. In March 2022 he received the Lifetime Achievement Award at the Game Developers Choice Awards in San Francisco. In November 2025 the Japanese government gave him the Order of the Rising Sun; *Push Square* reported that he was the first game developer to receive it, though figures from the wider industry had been honoured before.
