"Zzap64 have asked me to keep a diary and today I have to start it. Feel like a mega-star. Decide not to let it change my life." Andrew Braybrook wrote that on Wednesday 1 May 1985, and *[ZZAP!64](/vault/magazines/zzap-64)* printed it two months later as the first instalment of "The Birth of a Paradroid". Readers followed the game for four months: the crashes, the arguments about colour schemes, the routines that worked first time and the ones that did not. The finished *[Paradroid](/vault/games/paradroid)* won a Gold Medal. Braybrook went on to write *[Uridium](/vault/games/uridium)*, then kept a second diary for *Morpheus*, and those diaries remain the closest look the 1980s press gave its readers at a programmer's working day.

## From COBOL to the Dragon

Braybrook came to games from business computing. *CRASH* introduced him in March 1984 as "23 year old Andrew Braybrook", who had worked at Marconi in Chelmsford as a trainee programmer in the computer centre, using COBOL and CICS on a suite of IBM machines. He wrote games there in his spare time, "using Cobol to throw up games rather than text on the firm's computers". He told *ZZAP!* the same story: "It began with games on the mainframe at work. In COBOL. Several of us used to stay behind at night and play games." By day he "wrote accounting packages for Marconi".

His father bought him a ZX81, and he then bought himself a Dragon 32, which he preferred for its keyboard and colours. [Steve Turner](/vault/people/steve-turner), who was writing Spectrum games for [Hewson Consultants](/vault/companies/hewson-consultants), offered him a job around the end of 1983, and Braybrook "took it up without a second thought". *CRASH* called them "the professional programmer Steve Turner and his assistant, Andrew Braybrook". Turner worked on the Spectrum and Braybrook on the Dragon; in June 1984 *CRASH* reported that he had converted Turner's *3D Space Wars* and *3D Lunattack* to the Dragon.

When the Dragon faded, the pair chose the Commodore 64 as their second machine. "It was really more of a sensible business decision than a great desire to work on the Commodore," Braybrook said. His first job was converting *Lunattack*; "the first time I was let loose was with *Gribbly's Day Out*". The partnership became the company [Graftgold](/vault/companies/graftgold).

## The Hewson games

| Game | Year | Publisher | *ZZAP!64* |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Gribbly's Day Out* | 1985 | Hewson | Sizzler, 90% (issue 3) |
| *Paradroid* | 1985 | Hewson | Gold Medal, 97% (issue 7) |
| *Uridium* | 1986 | Hewson | Sizzler, 94% (issue 11) |
| *Alleykat* | 1986 | Hewson | 89% (issue 19) |

*Gribbly's Day Out* began as a game of "scientific structures", but the character set could not stretch to the cities he had planned, so the rocks took over and he tore out the man-made scenery. The power towers are what remain of the first idea.

*Paradroid* puts the player in charge of an "Influence Device" on a freighter full of rogue robots. It can shoot them, ram them, or take one over by winning a short circuit-board duel, and each robot it controls gives it that robot's weapons and armour. The first graphics were meant to look like a blueprint, but the 64's resolution was not fine enough; "out of desperation almost", Braybrook drew the metallic, bas-relief look instead, and within a morning he could see it would work. *ZZAP!*'s reviewer, who had followed three months of build-up, wondered whether the game would be a flop, and "needn't have": it was "absolutely superb". Years later he told *Edge* the robots had no real purpose. He had laid "train-tracks" over each deck for them to follow, and the trick that made them seem alive was line of sight: a robot could see the player and react. He called it "Apparent Intelligence": "it is more important what a player thinks is happening than what is actually going on."

*Uridium* moved outside, to a fighter strafing the hulls of huge metal dreadnoughts. He built it for speed. In March 1986 he compared frame rates: Turner's Spectrum game ran at "about six cycles a second", "seventeen is the slowest on *Paradroid* and fifty on *Uridium*". He took the friction out of the controls so that the ship kept moving, and complained cheerfully that a perfect smooth scroll earns no credit: "If you do something really well people don't notice it." Dominic Robinson converted *Uridium* to the Spectrum for Hewson, and *CRASH* reviewed it in December 1986.

## The diaries

"The Birth of a Paradroid" ran in *ZZAP!64* from July to October 1985. It is funny and specific. Braybrook buys his own Commodore 64 for £139 and feels "a bit disloyal towards my old Dragon 32". A new monitor cartridge locks up and takes a day's graphics with it; Turner, remembering that mains spikes sometimes crash the machine with memory intact, leaps round the room switching the fan heater on and off. It does not work. A new robot design comes out "looking like Kenny Everett with short legs". Turner calls his grey colour scheme boring: "Debate ensues. I lose."

The diary also shows where ideas came from. The routine that hides robots until they are in view came, he wrote, from *Survive*, a game he had played "a few years ago on an IBM mainframe", in which up to six players stalked each other and "never knew what was around the next corner".

The second diary, "Mental Procreation", followed *Morpheus* from March 1987 and was more technical. On its first day he set out to show 32 sprites at once, "without glitches or airborne trousers", on a machine whose video chip shows eight, and Turner ordered two PC compatibles so that code could be assembled on them and sent down an RS-232 cable to a Commodore 128. The entries on sprite multiplexing and sprites in the border are quoted in [Sprite multiplexing](/vault/techniques/sprite-multiplexing) and [Open borders](/vault/techniques/open-borders). He kept diaries later too: *Paradroid 90* in *Amiga Action* and *Uridium 2* in *[The One](/vault/magazines/the-one)*.

## Telecomsoft

At the PCW Show in September 1987 Graftgold signed with [Telecomsoft](/vault/companies/telecomsoft), British Telecom's software business. Hewson had been preparing to publish *Morpheus* and Turner's *Magnetron*, and a temporary High Court injunction stopped it; *ACE* reported that *Magnetron* would go to [Firebird](/vault/companies/firebird) and *Morpheus* to its sister label [Rainbird](/vault/companies/rainbird). *ZZAP!* gave *Morpheus* 90% in November 1987 and found it "a complete departure from the fast blast arcade games he's previously been associated with", a game needing "an awful lot of forethought, planning and trial and error". *Intensity* followed on Firebird in 1988; *ZZAP!* gave it 75% and called it "one of his better games".

Braybrook started writing 68000 code in June 1988. From September 1988 to June 1989 Graftgold converted Taito's arcade game *[Rainbow Islands](/vault/games/rainbow-islands)* for Telecomsoft, then went back to Hewson to write *Paradroid 90* for the Amiga and Atari ST. *Amiga Format* later said Braybrook had disassembled the arcade ROMs so that the conversion would be faithful; Steve Turner later told *Edge* that the Amiga version had been made from video of the whole game. *Paradroid 90* ended badly: Braybrook told *Edge* that Graftgold produced it with no advance, learned just before release that Activision rather than Hewson would publish it, and saw none of the money.

## After the 8-bit years

His later Amiga games were published by [Renegade](/vault/companies/renegade): *Fire & Ice* (1992), a platform game starring Cool Coyote, and *Uridium 2* (1993). *Retro Gamer* later dated [Graftgold](/vault/companies/graftgold)'s closure to 1996, but the company was still at work in 1998, when *PC Zone* listed *Hard Corp* "from developers Graftgold"; Steve Turner put its life at fifteen years. Looking back in *Edge* in 2003, he said he kept in touch by going to the ECTS trade show, "but all the people I know who are still in the industry seem to say the same thing, 'It's not the industry it was, don't come back.'"
