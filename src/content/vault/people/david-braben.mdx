When *Acorn Programs* went to meet the authors of *[Elite](/vault/games/elite)* in the autumn of 1984, it found "two unassuming undergraduates at Jesus College, Cambridge". The game had sold 13,000 copies in its first two weeks. David Braben, the younger of the two, planned to spend his share on a car. He had taught himself to program from the manual of an Acorn Atom, and *Elite* was, the magazine noted, "his first professional effort and his first game for the BBC B". He kept going for the next decade. After *Elite* he wrote *Zarch*, the showpiece of Acorn's Archimedes, and then spent five years building *Frontier: Elite II* largely on his own, before setting up the company that still carries its name.

## Fast facts

- **Born:** January 1964.
- **Education:** Jesus College, Cambridge, where he met [Ian Bell](/vault/people/ian-bell).
- **Elite:** written with Bell for the [BBC Micro](/vault/systems/bbc-micro); published by [Acornsoft](/vault/companies/acornsoft), September 1984.
- **Zarch:** Acorn Archimedes, [Superior Software](/vault/companies/superior-software), 1987; converted as *Virus* for [Firebird](/vault/companies/firebird), 1988.
- **Frontier: Elite II:** Amiga, Atari ST and PC, GameTek, October 1993.
- **Frontier Developments:** incorporated in January 1994; its first game was *Frontier: First Encounters* (GameTek, 1995).

## Elite

Braben and Bell met at dinner in Hall at Jesus. "It took us some time to admit that we were both interested in computers," Braben told *Acorn Programs*. "At first we were too embarrassed." Both had owned computers since their last year at school, Braben an Acorn Atom and Bell a Tandy TRS-80, and neither had been taught to program. "They had no computers at school in my day," Braben said.

The game started with Bell, who wanted to follow his Acornsoft game *Freefall* with a 3D simulation set in space. "Then Braben added the trading ship scenario and things just developed from there," Bell said. Soon after they started, they took the program to Acornsoft, which was interested enough to lend Braben a BBC Micro. Bell's own list of who wrote what puts the ship plotting, the trading and equipment screens, the navigational charts, the sound effects and the galaxy generation on Braben's side, with Bell handling the planets, the 3D movement, the enemy tactics and docking. Braben designed the Cobra Mk III, the Python, the Sidewinder, the Viper, the Mamba, the Constrictor and the Thargoid, and the Coriolis space station.

The galaxy generator is the part most often described. *Elite* keeps no list of planets: each system comes from six bytes of "seeds", and the program derives the next system's seeds from the last with a repeated "twisting" step based on the Tribonacci series, where each number is the sum of the three before it. The eight galaxies of 256 systems therefore cost almost no memory, which mattered on a 32K machine. Braben complained to *Acorn Programs* about the BBC's "hideous shortage of memory". "The main thing is to make sure that every instruction is a useful one," he said.

They finished the game in January 1984, "then we finished it again — and several times more after that," Bell said. Acornsoft insisted on secrecy until the launch in September 1984, and in December *Acorn Programs* reported Acornsoft's forecast of Christmas sales "in the region of 100,000". The pair kept the rights to other machines and sold them to Firebird. According to *ZZAP!64*, they converted the game to the Commodore 64 themselves while studying for their finals, and in 1995 Braben told *Amiga Power* that he and Bell later "wrote a version for Nintendo". The [Elite](/vault/games/elite) entry covers the game, its launch and its many versions.

## Zarch and Virus

Braben's next big game was written for Acorn's new 32-bit computer. Owners of the [Acorn Archimedes](/vault/systems/acorn-archimedes) first met it as *Lander*, a playable demo on the machine's welcome disk. The full game, *Zarch*, came from Superior Software in 1987. The player flies a hoverplane low over a solid, shaded landscape, shooting down aliens that spread a red virus across the ground. *ACE* saw the demo in its first issue in October 1987: "every particle of a Lander/Zarch explosion casts its own shadow and falls under gravity. An Archie will set you back upwards of £800 and you don't even get a joystick port for that money, but what a game!" Its review two months later gave *Zarch* an ACE rating of 979.

In May 1988 *ACE* called it "the highest ACE-rated" game so far and reported that Braben was converting it to the Atari ST and Amiga himself. The Archimedes could show 256 colours at once, so *Zarch* shaded its landscape darker with distance. The ST could show only 16, so its version had to do without that "depth-cueing", but it already ran at "a healthy 15 frames per second or so". The conversions came out as *Virus*, published by Firebird in 1988, with the Amiga version at £19.95.

## Frontier: Elite II

*Frontier* took Braben five years. He started it in 1988, "on my own", he told *Amiga Power* in 1995, working "first with Konami and then with Gametek". He spent much of the time on astronomy and "how things work", and he later regretted not using the textured graphics he had developed early on: they were ahead of their time in 1988 but "slightly behind the latest" by 1993. Bell's account is that Braben developed the game "with some assistance from me". *Amiga Format* announced its release through GameTek "at the end of October" 1993.

*CU Amiga* gave the Amiga version 97% in November 1993, at £34.99, and called it "the single most important step forward for games this decade". GameTek put that line on its advertisements.

## Frontier Developments and First Encounters

"After Frontier, I'd resolved never to do a major project on my own," Braben told *Amiga Power*, "so I set up Frontier Developments and got a team around me." The company was incorporated in January 1994, based in Cambridge. Its first game was the sequel, *Frontier: First Encounters*, which GameTek released in 1995.

It shipped with serious bugs. *PC Zone*'s buyer's guide in August 1995 called it a "disappointing enhanced version" of *Frontier* that was "bugged to jiggery", and its letters page that month was full of readers who had returned their copies. Magazine staff wrote that they had been told the problems came from something "going wrong just before it went to duplication". Braben took legal action against GameTek over the release. In 2000 he told *Edge* that "the legal stuff really spoilt it for me, even though I finally won the case last year, which is why I haven't gone back to Elite for so long". Later reports describe the case as settled out of court in 1999.

The dispute also soured Braben's relationship with Bell. Bell has written that Braben's solicitors objected to an interview he gave in 1995, and that in 1999 Bell's website was taken down after a legal threat from Braben over the *Elite* files it offered. By Bell's account, Braben then said publicly that he did not mind the files being freely downloadable. The [Ian Bell](/vault/people/ian-bell) entry has more.

## Later

Frontier Developments grew into one of Britain's larger independent studios and in 2014 released *[Elite Dangerous](/vault/games/elite-dangerous)*, funded partly through Kickstarter. Braben was also one of the six founding trustees of the Raspberry Pi Foundation, the educational charity behind the Raspberry Pi computer, and brought what the Foundation calls "an industry perspective" from the difficulty he was having recruiting programmers.
