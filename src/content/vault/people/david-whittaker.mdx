By the late 1980s a reviewer could recognise David Whittaker from the title screen. *CRASH* wrote in May 1987 of "a standard David Whittaker title track" on Mastertronic's *Amaurote*, and in August called him "the prolific David Whittaker". He had earned it. In January 1990 the Italian magazine *K* put his count at more than four hundred soundtracks, plus thousands of sound effects, across almost every machine a British publisher sold games for. Most of them he never saw running. "Any favourite tunes? No," he told *CRASH* in 1987. "I usually end up hating them all after I've spent so much time writing them."

## From a band to Lazy Jones

Whittaker played guitar and keyboards in bands before he owned a computer, and *The One* reported in 1989 that he had been in groups since he was 16. He bought a Commodore VIC-20 in 1982. "I really started in February '82," he told *ZZAP!64*'s round-table of musicians in 1986, where he described being commissioned for a lot of small pieces for Commodore before moving to Terminal Software.

At Terminal he wrote a whole game as well as its music: *Lazy Jones* (1984) for the Commodore 64. The hero is an idle hotel worker who wanders the corridors avoiding the manager and a deadly cleaning trolley, and each room he enters holds a different mini-game with its own tune. *Commodore Horizons* printed a type-in extra screen for it in February 1985, "from David Whittaker of Terminal". Fifteen years later the German act Zombie Nation built their 1999 dance hit *Kernkraft 400* on one of the *Lazy Jones* tunes; Whittaker was later compensated for its use.

The game turned him into a composer. "After *Lazy Jones* came out, every programmer I knew who couldn't write music came to me," he told *K*. For a while he programmed and composed; as the demand for music grew, he gave up programming games. By August 1986 he was the resident musician at Binary Design in Manchester, and later he ran his own company, Musicon Design.

## Spectrum 128 and the Einstein

On the Spectrum, the 128K models' three-channel sound chip made Whittaker's name. Binary Design's *Glider Rider* (Quicksilva, 1986) scored 80% in *CRASH* on the 48K Spectrum and 92% on the 128K, and a *CRASH* reviewer called it "the first 128 game with any sort of enhancement", made "much more atmospheric by music". By November 1987 *CRASH* reported that Musicon Design wrote "most of the tunes that emit from the 128K music chip these days", including *The Living Daylights*, *Glider Rider* and *The Tube*.

The same *CRASH* feature explained how he worked. He wrote his Spectrum tunes with his own driver, "the piece of code that makes the music data run", on a Tatung Einstein, then sent the data down an RS232 lead into a Spectrum's Interface 1. A routine took anything from half a kilobyte for a jingle to 3K including the driver. He rarely saw the games: he might be given a general theme or a tempo, or occasionally a known tune to convert, as when Infogrames asked him for the *Chariots of Fire* theme.

Code Masters' budget games were a large part of the output. *CRASH* reviewers called the title tune of *Super Robin Hood* "a great David Whittaker piece" in June 1987, and in November gave *ATV Simulator* a "good David Whittaker tune with neat FX". Not every reader was charmed. A *CRASH* correspondent in September 1988 asked whether "Dave Whittaker [is] onto a nice little earner? He loads up old Commodore tunes off disk onto his Einstein, downloads them to his Speccy and Code Masters give him loads of money." Whittaker was open about reusing material. He told *The Story of the Commodore 64 in Pixels* that he had written some tunes in a few minutes, and that in dry spells he would "borrow ideas from others (i.e. plagiarise) and even from myself"; his *Red Max*, he admitted, was "my rip-off of Rob's *Commando* – but more of an homage really".

*CRASH*'s readers still voted for him. In February 1989 the magazine's first readers' Top 10 Music chart included his *Amaurote*. The same year Miles Gordon Technology hired him to write the music and sound-effect drivers for the SAM Coupé's six-channel Philips SAA1099 chip, and a SAM Coupé diary in *CRASH* that December called him "maybe the best computer musician in the business".

## Samples on the Amiga

The Amiga's four channels of sampled sound (see [Paula](/vault/hardware/paula)) suited him. Because he could program, he wrote his own Amiga driver, which could play real-time synthesised sounds alongside samples. Allister Brimble, a later Amiga composer, wrote that this gave him "a huge advantage over the competition". Whittaker described his method. He composed on a separate synthesiser, a Roland Jupiter 6, a Yamaha CX5M or a Korg M1, and "manually entered each note by hand, into an assembler file of my drivers". Because the note format was the same across the C64, Spectrum, ST, Amiga and PC, he could cut and paste between versions.

By May 1989 *The One* listed *Xenon*, *Speedball* and *Obliterator* among his 16-bit work. For the Bitmap Brothers' *[Xenon 2: Megablast](/vault/games/xenon-2)* he arranged Bomb the Bass's "Megablast", the track that gave the game its subtitle. For Psygnosis's *[Shadow of the Beast](/vault/games/shadow-of-the-beast)* (1989) he was given, he said, "a ton of (by the standards of the time) sample/music memory/disk-space", and he recorded the Korg M1's pan-flute sounds at 20 kHz, "instead of the usual 8–12, which is why they're so clear and breathy". When *ZZAP!*'s readers voted on Amiga music in 1990, *Xenon 2* came top of the July chart; in August it was second and *Shadow of the Beast* third, behind [Jonathan Dunn](/vault/people/jonathan-dunn)'s *Batman: The Movie*. He went on to score *Shadow of the Beast II* (1990) and Ocean's *Epic*, for which *ACE* reported in February 1991 that he had written the music and effects, "including some sampled speech".

## America and after

In June 1993 Whittaker and his wife moved to the United States, where he worked for Electronic Arts at its Redwood Shores studio in California; [Rob Hubbard](/vault/people/rob-hubbard), already at EA, had approached him about a job doing sound for its Mega Drive and SNES games. He later returned to England. Public reference works say he became head of audio at Traveller's Tales in Knutsford, Cheshire, in September 2004, and *The Story of the Amiga in Pixels* shows him there in 2005. *The Story of the Commodore 64 in Pixels* names the company's LEGO games as his recent work. Wikipedia lists his last game credits in 2007.
