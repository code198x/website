**Doug Neubauer** designed [POKEY](/vault/hardware/pokey) and wrote [Star Raiders](/vault/games/star-raiders). One was a chip handling several jobs inside Atari’s computers; the other made those computers feel like the cockpit of a spacecraft. His work offers an unusually direct connection between a hardware designer and a game built for the machine containing it.

## More than a sound chip

In Jon Pappas’s 1986 *ANALOG* interview, Neubauer describes POKEY’s keyboard, paddle and audio responsibilities, and the development of its serial facilities. Calling it a music chip misses the work shared across its registers and timers.

For a game programmer, those facilities form part of the available machine. Sound is not an isolated recording played behind the action. The program controls it while also handling the player’s inputs, updating the world and preparing the display.

*Star Raiders* had to fit its space combat, instruments and navigation into a small cartridge. Neubauer’s interview discusses the costs of individual effects, including explosions. An attractive effect still had to leave enough time for the rest of the simulation. (*ANALOG* 47, pp. 89–90.)

## Building the experience

In James Hague’s later *Halcyon Days* interview, Neubauer connects the game with his enjoyment of *Star Wars* and text-based *Star Trek*. These are his recollections of influences, rather than proof that the game follows one source in every detail.

The result asks the player to use a map, manage the ship and judge a view through the cockpit. Its surrounding instruments are part of play: the player must understand what the display means, not steer towards a visible target.

Neubauer later wrote **Solaris** for the [Atari 2600](/vault/systems/atari-2600). His account contrasts its resources with those of *Star Raiders*: more cartridge space did not remove the older console’s display constraints. The comparison is a reminder that ROM size alone does not measure how much a game can put on screen.

His chip and games belong together, but neither should swallow the other. POKEY served many kinds of software; *Star Raiders* was a particular design made by choosing how to spend a whole computer’s limited resources.
