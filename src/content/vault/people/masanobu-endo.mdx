Masanobu Endo's two famous games both hid things. In *[Xevious](/vault/games/xevious)*, bombing an empty-looking patch of ground could raise a hidden structure out of the earth. In *The Tower of Druaga*, each of the tower's 60 floors hid a treasure chest that appeared only when the player solved that floor's concealed riddle. Endo said in 2016 that *Druaga* was "clearly designed with the expectation of spreading by word of mouth among gamers" (in translation): a game no single player could solve, made for players to talk about. Both games came from [Namco](/vault/companies/namco) in the early 1980s, and Endo, unusually for a game maker of the time, was known to players by name.

## Fast facts

- **Born:** 1959.
- **Namco:** joined in 1981.
- **Games at Namco:** *[Xevious](/vault/games/xevious)* (1983), *Grobda*, and *The Tower of Druaga* (1984).
- **Game Studio:** co-founded in 1985 with Kazuo Kurosu, a fellow Namco developer; it went on to make *The Return of Ishtar* (1986) and *The Quest of Ki* (1988) for Namco.
- **Teaching:** professor in the game department of Tokyo Polytechnic University from 2014, and later a visiting professor there.

## Namco and the Dig Dug notebook

Endo joined Namco in 1981. His first job, as he told it in 2016, involved little programming: he was testing *[Dig Dug](/vault/games/dig-dug)*, and wrote down everything he noticed about how its monsters behaved, inventing his own names for what he saw. Namco's president Masaya Nakamura came across the notes and had them published as a small booklet, the *Digtionary*. "The first thing made was a booklet called the *Digtionary*, which collected strategies for *Dig Dug*," Endo recalled (in translation).

## Xevious

*Xevious* reached Japanese arcades early in 1983. According to a Japanese retrospective on its 40th anniversary, Endo wrote the program and drew all the graphics apart from the map. The game's double weapon system, a Zapper for aircraft and a Blaster for ground targets, gave the player two jobs at once, and its scrolling landscape held secrets. Dropping a bomb on an apparently empty spot could make a hidden structure, the Sol, burst out of the ground; the bomb-sight blinked over the right place, so players could hunt for them. A hidden special flag gave no such clue, and finding it unaided was, *GAME Watch* wrote, a near-impossible feat.

Endo gave the game a world as well. Its story, called *Fardraut*, came with an invented language in which the names mean something: "Solvalou", the player's ship, is "bird of the sun", and the enemy Zakato is "magic". Almost nobody playing it in an arcade would have known. In 1991 he published a novel set in that world, *Shōsetsu Xevious: Fardraut Saga*. He also did something few designers of the period did: he appeared under his own name and with his face in Japanese magazines such as *Login*, talking about *Xevious* and *Druaga*.

The secrets kept players coming back and comparing notes. A teenage [Satoshi Tajiri](/vault/people/satoshi-tajiri) wrote a fanzine issue about *Xevious* that sold 10,000 copies, and in 2016 Endo, Tajiri and [Ken Sugimori](/vault/people/ken-sugimori) sat down together to talk about it for the Japanese site *Denfaminico Gamer*. One of *Xevious*'s enemies, the Grobda tank (Atari's American advertising promised players "ZOSHI Death squads, GROBDA tanks"), got a game of its own: *Grobda*, an overhead tank battle over 99 levels, which *Edge* likened to Atari's *Combat*.

## The Tower of Druaga

Endo's next big game took a different shape. His first proposal, dated 25 March 1983, was called *Quest*; it became *The Return of Ishtar* in July 1983, *Tower of Anu* that November, and finally *The Tower of Druaga* in March 1984. The game reached arcades in July 1984. The player climbs a 60-floor tower ruled by the demon Druaga to rescue Ki, held captive at the top, and on every floor the chest holding that floor's item appears only when the player solves a hidden riddle.

Endo was frank about what that meant. Asked about it in 2016, he first joked that he did not want to name the game: "There was this game with an absurd number of mysteries scattered throughout, one that absolutely couldn't be solved alone." It was, he said, "clearly designed with the expectation of spreading by word of mouth among gamers" (both in translation). In the same conversation he accepted that the game had helped spread the arcade notebooks in which Japanese players wrote down their discoveries for others.

Outside Japan the design puzzled people. When Namco reissued *Druaga* on the PlayStation in *Namco Museum Volume 3* in 1996, *Edge* called it "a wildly tedious and snail-paced maze game", noting that it "was apparently a huge smash on the Famicom in Japan many years ago". *Edge* had already observed in 1994 that the game "proved a massive hit on the Famicom". For its 40th anniversary in 2024, a four-volume, 1,200-page collection of the original development documents was published, Endo's proposals among them.

## Game Studio

Endo left Namco in 1985 and, with Kazuo Kurosu, another veteran of Namco's arcade development, founded Game Studio, a development company aimed, in its own description, at raising the artistic quality of games. It kept working for Namco. *The Return of Ishtar* (1986), an arcade sequel to *Druaga* which *Edge* later noted was "one of the first titles to employ a password system", and *The Quest of Ki* (1988), a Famicom prequel, continued the series known as the Babylonian Castle Saga. The company has since been reorganised more than once, for a time as Mobile & Game Studio; in 2023 *GAME Watch* described Endo as an adviser to Game Studio.

## Teaching

Endo moved into academic life alongside the industry. He joined the board of the Japan Digital Game Society in 2007 and became a professor in the game department of Tokyo Polytechnic University's Faculty of Arts in 2014. In January 2026 the university described him as a visiting professor.

He also chaired the judges of PERACON, a game-planning contest, from its first year in 2011. In September 2020 the judges' written comments on that year's entries were widely criticised as personal attacks on the entrants. The organisers deleted all of the comments and apologised, and one judge was disciplined. *Netlab* reported that comments by Endo himself, telling entrants to give up on game planning, were among those criticised. Endo said on Twitter that the volume of entries had cost the judges their tolerance, and that he had taken note of suggestions for improvement.
