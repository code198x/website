Andrew Deakin converted arcade machines to the Spectrum for [Ocean](/vault/companies/ocean-software) during the years when that was the most commercially important programming job in Britain. A *CRASH* reader's ranking of Ocean and Imagine's staff places him second:

> **2 ANDREW DEAKIN — programmer of *Operation Wolf*, *Combat School* and *Rambo 3*.**

He is named in **37 issues** of the magazines held here, and appears in **no structured credit field** in this library at all.

## Fast facts

- **Programmed:** *Operation Wolf*, *Combat School*, *Rambo III*.
- **Worked with:** [Ivan Horn](/vault/people/ivan-horn) on graphics and [Jonathan Dunn](/vault/people/jonathan-dunn) on music — the same Ocean team recurs across these titles.
- **At:** Ocean, Manchester.

## What Operation Wolf had to be

*Operation Wolf* is a good measure of the job, because the arcade original is defined by hardware the Spectrum does not have.

The cabinet has a mounted, recoiling gun; sprite hardware throwing dozens of enemies at once; and a scrolling background that moves independently of them. The Spectrum has a rubber keyboard, no sprites, no hardware scrolling, and one colour per 8×8 cell.

The conversion cannot reproduce any of that, so the work is deciding **what the game consists of** and rebuilding that from different parts. The answer here was: a target you move, a stream of enemies with a rhythm to learn, ammunition and hostages to manage, and enough pace that the missing hardware stops mattering. It sold enormously.

That is a design act as much as a technical one, and it is what separates the conversions people still play from the ones that only shipped on time.

## The team behind the licence

Deakin's entry is also the clearest illustration of how Ocean's Spectrum work was organised. The same names recur across *Operation Wolf*, *Rambo III* and *Combat School*: Deakin on code, [Ivan Horn](/vault/people/ivan-horn) on graphics, [Jonathan Dunn](/vault/people/jonathan-dunn) on music.

That is a **team with a division of labour**, in 1988, on 48K games — the arrangement the industry would later formalise everywhere, arriving early because the deadlines left no alternative. One person cannot write, draw and score a film licence between the film's announcement and its release.

The magazines noticed the shape before the credits did. A reader could name that trio; the review boxes could not.

## Why he matters

Deakin's years at Ocean are **the licence machine at its peak** — Ocean turning arcade cabinets and film properties into Spectrum cassettes at a rate and a standard nobody else matched.

He also carries the same warning as the others in this group. The evidence for a programmer with three of the decade's biggest sellers to his name is a **reader's letter**, because the industry's own records of who did what were thrown away and the magazines' credit boxes were never designed to hold them.
