In March 1984, when he had been writing Spectrum games full time for only a few months, Steve Turner told *CRASH* where he thought games were going. Adventures, he said, rarely had more than still pictures, and he saw no reason why they could not have far better graphics; he expected "the welding of the graphical and word type adventure game", and programs "more like novels than sketches". Eight months later his *Avalon* came in a video-style box that called it "the first in a new world of computer games, the Adventure Movie". Turner wrote *Avalon*, *Dragontorc*, *Quazatron* and *Ranarama* for [Hewson Consultants](/vault/companies/hewson-consultants), and the one-man business he started in 1983 became [Graftgold](/vault/companies/graftgold), with [Andrew Braybrook](/vault/people/andrew-braybrook) as its first recruit.

## Fast facts

- **Spectrum games for Hewson:** *3D Space Wars*, *3D Seiddab Attack* and *3D Lunattack* (1983–84), *Avalon* (1984), *Dragontorc* (1985), *Astroclone* (1985), *Quazatron* (1986), *Ranarama* (1987).
- ***CRASH* scores:** *Avalon* 91%, *Dragontorc* 92%, *Quazatron* 94%, *Ranarama* 90%, *Astroclone* 88%.
- **Company:** ST Software from 1983, then Graftgold, which he ran as managing director until it stopped trading in the late 1990s.
- **Later:** programming insurance systems again, with Braybrook as a colleague, by 2004.

## From insurance to the Spectrum

Turner learned to program at school, on a desk-sized machine with 512 bytes of core memory, fed by paper tape, which printed its answers on a Teletype. His working career began, *CRASH* reported, on one of the government's TOPS retraining courses; he joined the Civil Service as a clerical assistant, became a programmer there, and then moved to one of the world's largest insurance brokers, where he spent four or five years on ICL and IBM mainframes and became a team leader. At home he owned a [ZX80](/vault/systems/sinclair-zx80), decided that writing games on it was "a dead loss", skipped the [ZX81](/vault/systems/sinclair-zx81), and bought one of the first [ZX Spectrums](/vault/systems/sinclair-zx-spectrum).

His first game, *3D Space Wars*, took five months, most of it spent on the graphics. He sent it to four software houses and three wanted it. He chose Hewson; the company's founder, [Andrew Hewson](/vault/people/andrew-hewson), had been at the same school. By the time Hewson profiled him in *CRASH* he had finished *3D Seiddab Attack* and was three months into *Lunar Attack*, the first of his games to use the whole of a 48K Spectrum instead of fitting in 16K. It came out as *3D Lunattack*, and *CRASH* gave it 90%. Hewson was blunter about the three space games in 1985: "as games, they were not overly successful or particularly good", he told *Sinclair User*, but the programming behind them led to *Avalon*.

He worked alone at first, in his dining room with a Spectrum and a black-and-white television, keeping office hours from 9am to 5pm. He had saved about £10,000 before leaving his job, and when he took on his friend Braybrook, then a programmer at Marconi, their games had to earn royalties within the year. Turner could not find a Spectrum assembler that handled large programs, so he built his own development system and added a disassembler to it, and kept each game on a rotating cycle of five cassettes because tapes so often failed to load.

## Avalon and Dragontorc

*Avalon* (1984, £7.95) cast the player as the astral projection of Maroc the Mage, sent down through the dungeons to destroy the Lord of Chaos. Movement is itself a spell, and the spells Maroc collects scroll past at the foot of the screen, to be picked with a pointer. Rooms are drawn in 3D, and the game remembers what the player has done: open chests stay open, and goblins, wraiths and guardians arrive in growing numbers after each failure. *CRASH* reviewed it in November 1984 as an arcade game, not an adventure, gave it 91%, and predicted that readers would soon be hearing the words "adventure movie" a lot: "History will dictate who is first, but Hewson have certainly made a head start". "Avalon is a game that never ends," one reviewer wrote: sink to the lowest level and "you are forced to live with the effects of what you have done on previous attempts."

*Dragontorc* (1985), "the second adventure movie", sent Maroc across ancient Britain to recover five crowns before Morag the Shape-shifter could reunite them with the Dragontorc. Its new trick, *CRASH* explained, was "sensory animation": the creatures had characters of their own, some hostile and some willing to help, and Maroc could only win by enlisting allies. *CRASH* made it a Smash at 92% in May 1985, and one reviewer called it Turner's "best game yet".

It did not sell like *Avalon*, Turner wrote later, and he put off the third game he had planned. *Astroclone* (1985), a space game that mixed strategy, shooting and adventure sequences, was the design he was proudest of, but it "did not fit into a neat category" and sold badly.

## Quazatron

*Quazatron* started as a graphics routine without a game. Turner wanted a better look than his last two games, so he wrote code to build a 3D scene from a 2D map, a city of stepped levels that he nearly called *Ziggurat*. Hewson wanted a Spectrum version of Braybrook's Commodore 64 hit *[Paradroid](/vault/games/paradroid)*, and Turner, who needed a quick game after *Astroclone*, put *Paradroid*'s gameplay on his backdrop. Some routines were converted directly from Braybrook's 64 code. The idea at the heart of both games, taking over an enemy robot by winning a duel on a circuit board, had partly been his: he had been playing with a circuit-diagram program that came free with a Spectrum, and Braybrook simplified their early design into the grapple game. The whole of *Quazatron* took six months.

In the game the player is KLP2, "Klepto", a droid expelled from droid school for dismantling his teacher, sent to clear the underground city of Quazatron. He rams enemy droids to start a grapple, and when he wins he can strip their drive units, weapons and power units to upgrade himself. *CRASH* gave it 94% in June 1986. Two reviewers noted how much it owed to *Paradroid*, one complained that the scrolling was slow, and another called it "one of the best games ever to come out on the Spectrum". Readers agreed: in *CRASH*'s poll of 1986 it came fourth for Best Game Overall. In *[ZX Computing](/vault/magazines/zx-computing)* that summer Turner published the dictionary look-up routine that let the game hold a large library of droid text in little memory, a dictionary of up to 254 words, each called up by a single number. From July the same magazine ran his monthly series of advice for would-be games writers, from getting past "the machine code barrier" to getting a game published.

## Ranarama

*Ranarama* (1987) reused ideas from *Paradroid* in a magical setting. Mervyn, a sorcerer's apprentice, has turned himself into a frog with a spell meant to make him irresistible to women, and survives the wizards who invade his dungeon because they take him for a harmless frog. He has to kill all 96 of them, twelve on each of eight levels; bumping into a wizard starts a duel in which the player unscrambles the letters of RANARAMA against the clock. Rooms light up as Mervyn enters them and stay lit. *CRASH* made it a Smash at 90% in March 1987, although one reviewer had "grimaced" at yet another *Gauntlet* variant. Turner remembered that several top-down dungeon games were on sale at once, and that *Ranarama* did better in Spain than in Britain.

## Graftgold

When the royalties began to rise Turner formed a limited company, Graftgold, to keep the money for future games and pay the programmers a steady wage. Hewson never paid advances or offered a contract, which left Graftgold free to write what it liked. Every Spectrum game, though, "seemed to sell less than the previous one", while taking longer to write.

In 1987 Turner was working on *Magnetron*, a follow-up to *Quazatron*, when two Hewson programmers, Dominic Robinson and John Cumming, rang to ask if he would employ them, and Hewson's second-in-command, who had moved to [Telecomsoft](/vault/companies/telecomsoft), introduced Graftgold there. Graftgold signed with Telecomsoft at the PCW Show that September and moved into its first offices, above a greengrocer's in Witham. Hewson challenged its right to take *Magnetron* and Braybrook's *Morpheus* elsewhere, and a High Court injunction held them up until the publishers settled out of court. *Magnetron* came out on Firebird in 1988.

For Telecomsoft Turner converted *Flying Shark* from Robinson's Spectrum version to the Amstrad CPC in 14 days. When Robinson burned out on the 3D game *Simulcra*, Turner finished it himself, redesigning the gameplay around Robinson's engine. Graftgold's conversion of Taito's *[Rainbow Islands](/vault/games/rainbow-islands)*, which Braybrook led, established it as a conversion house. Through the 1990s, with Turner as managing director, the company moved between publishers, several of which collapsed or were sold, and it stopped work in the late 1990s when funding for its last game, the PlayStation tank game *Hard Corp*, ran out. "So 15 years of developing games came to an end," Turner wrote. The [Graftgold](/vault/companies/graftgold) entry tells that story in full.

## After games

Turner went back to the kind of work he had left in 1983. Asked in 2004 whether he and Braybrook still kept in touch, he told *Retro Gamer* that Braybrook worked with him "at a firm that programs insurance systems, so it's turned full circle for us". Wikipedia records that he stayed in IT and appeared in *From Bedrooms to Billions* (2014), a documentary on the British games industry. *Quazatron* was never converted to another machine; the 64 version would have been too close to *Paradroid*, he said, and he and Braybrook lacked the capacity for many conversions. If he returned to it, he told *Retro Gamer* in 2005, he would develop KLP2's character, whose expressions came from Braybrook's *Gribbly's Day Out*, into "a cheeky little droid that would be quick to hide whenever danger threatened".
