**R. J. Mical** helped give the [Amiga](/vault/systems/commodore-amiga) a usable face. Its custom chips could move pictures and produce sound, but applications also needed windows, menus and a consistent way to receive a user’s actions. His **Intuition** work belonged to that system-software layer.

The 1986 *Amiga Intuition Reference Manual* names **Robert J. Mical and Susan Deyl** as its authors. It describes an interface that application programmers can use, rather than treating the desktop as a demonstration that only its creators know how to operate. The distinction matters: a graphics demonstration shows what a machine can do; an interface library lets many programs offer familiar controls.

## One part of the Amiga

Intuition sits within a larger body of work. **Carl Sassenrath’s** Exec supplies the operating system’s task and message machinery. [Dale Luck](/vault/people/dale-luck) worked on graphics software. Mical’s interface work depended on those surrounding services and on hardware designed by a team that included [Jay Miner](/vault/people/jay-miner).

The manual is useful evidence because it exposes the work after the celebrated demonstrations: the structures, calls and events through which another programmer makes an application. A window must do more than appear. The program needs to learn what happened to it and respond while other activity continues.

## From desktop to handheld

Mical later worked with **Dave Needle** on the handheld that became the [Atari Lynx](/vault/systems/atari-lynx). Their interview in *The Games Machine* in January 1990 describes the collaboration and the use of Amigas as development machines. The two machines were different products, but the tool-building concern continued: custom hardware needs a practical way to write and test software for it.

Mical’s own résumé also records his work on **3DO**. That is a participant’s career account, so it should be read alongside the documentation of each product, rather than as a claim that one person designed every part of it.

His place in these machines is clearest when the interface and the tools are considered alongside the chips. Those layers determined how readily other people could turn the hardware into software of their own.
