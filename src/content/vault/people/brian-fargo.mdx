In 1981 a teenager in Newport Beach, California, wrote an adventure game for the Apple II, copied the disks, designed the adverts and sold about 5,000 copies himself. Two years later Brian Fargo founded Interplay Productions. For most of the next two decades Interplay made or published role-playing games that others measured their own against: *The Bard's Tale*, *[Wasteland](/vault/games/wasteland)*, *[Fallout](/vault/games/fallout)*, *[Baldur's Gate](/vault/games/baldurs-gate)* and *[Planescape: Torment](/vault/games/planescape-torment)*. After he lost the company, Fargo started again, and in 2012 he went to players on Kickstarter for the *Wasteland* sequel that publishers had turned down.

## Fast facts

- **Born:** late 1962 or early 1963. Period magazines gave his age as 24 in December 1987, 25 in February 1989 and 36 in August 1999; Wikidata gives 15 December 1962.
- **First games:** *Labyrinths of Mordagon*, written with Michael Cranford when he was 18, and *The Demon's Forge* (Saber Software, 1981).
- **Companies:** founded [Interplay](/vault/companies/interplay) (1983) and inXile Entertainment (2002).
- **As designer:** credited with Michael Cranford for *The Bard's Tale II* and with Alan Pavlish, Michael Stackpole and Ken St. Andre for *Wasteland* (1988), where he was also Interplay's executive producer.
- **As producer and head of Interplay:** *Neuromancer*, *Battle Chess*, *Descent*, *Fallout*, and the [Black Isle Studios](/vault/companies/black-isle-studios) games.

## The Demon's Forge

"I was still in high school when my father brought home the first computer," Fargo told *Commodore Magazine* in 1987. "I'd wake up in the morning and sit at the thing until after midnight. All I know about them is self-taught." His first game, as he told the German magazine *PC Player* in 1999, was *Labyrinths of Mordagon*, a multiple-choice adventure written at 18 with the future author of *The Bard's Tale*, Michael Cranford. Fargo had an Apple II and Cranford did not, so they took turns: Fargo wrote a section in BASIC, Cranford came round to test it, then took the computer home and wrote the next part. It sold about 300 copies.

While he worked in a ComputerLand store repairing computers, Fargo set up Saber Software, "where I did everything — programmed, manufactured, drew the artwork and distributed the software." Its game was *The Demon's Forge*, a 48K graphic adventure for the Apple II at $29.95. The player is a gladiator cast into the Forge, "an infamous dungeon network" from which "no one in past centuries has escaped alive". *Computer Gaming World* printed Fargo's own scene-setting story next to Saber's advert, and its review, signed by Michael Cranford, found the game "a step ahead of conventional computer adventuring". He sold, he recalled, around 5,000 copies.

## Interplay

*The One* reported that Fargo founded Interplay Productions in November 1983. It began by writing games for bigger publishers. The first to go out through a large one was the graphic adventure *Mindshadow*, for [Activision](/vault/companies/activision). Then came *The Bard's Tale* (1985) for [Electronic Arts](/vault/companies/electronic-arts), which EA advertised in 1986 under Fargo's name as "the best new dungeon fantasy of 1986". It almost had no publisher: one turned it down, Fargo remembered, because it came on two disks and the publisher thought that too expensive. At *Computer Gaming World*'s 1987 awards, Michael Cranford and Brian Fargo were the credited designers of *The Bard's Tale II*, a nominee for Adventure Game of the Year.

By late 1987, at 24, Fargo had stopped programming. "I'm really sort of a director here at Interplay," he told *Commodore Magazine*. "We have a graphic artist here, a guy who is an expert with sound, we have a programmer who is the main guy. We have a designer who adds the real meat to our products... I sort of collaborate all of these individual talents into the final product." Everyone at the company, he added, was under 30.

That was how he worked on *[Wasteland](/vault/games/wasteland)* (1988). *Computer Gaming World* credited its design to Alan Pavlish, Michael Stackpole, Fargo and Ken St. Andre, with Fargo as executive producer for Interplay, and on 3 September 1988 he and Pavlish collected the magazine's Adventure Game of the Year award for it. The same year Interplay began publishing under its own name, starting with *Battle Chess*, and Fargo produced *Neuromancer*, from William Gibson's novel. *Battle Chess* was the game he remembered most fondly: the team, he said in 1999, was great and everyone had a fabulous attitude.

## The Fallout years

In the 1990s Fargo's job was choosing and backing other people's games. Interplay published Parallax Software's *Descent*, [Blizzard](/vault/companies/blizzard)'s early *The Lost Vikings* and Delphine's *Out of This World*, and it made *[Fallout](/vault/games/fallout)* itself. *Fallout* was a near thing. According to *Edge*, it took from 1994 to 1997, survived two near-cancellations, and its designers argued with marketing and executives over everything from the setting to the name. Fargo took an early build home for a weekend and then told the lead programmer, [Tim Cain](/vault/people/tim-cain): "You should call this Fallout." Its advertisements called it "the unofficial sequel to Wasteland", because Electronic Arts owned the *Wasteland* name. In 2010 Fargo told *Edge*, "Fallout, of course, exists as a franchise because we didn't have the rights to Wasteland."

Under Interplay's role-playing label, [Black Isle Studios](/vault/companies/black-isle-studios), came [BioWare](/vault/companies/bioware)'s *Baldur's Gate*, *Planescape: Torment* and *Icewind Dale*. In November 1996 *Computer Gaming World* placed Fargo third in its list of the most influential people in the industry, saying he had "shown both brilliant product vision and great business instincts" and naming *The Bard's Tale*, *Wasteland* and *Dragon Wars*.

The role-playing games won praise, but Interplay lost money. It went public in 1998, and in 2001 the French publisher Titus Interactive took control (the [Interplay](/vault/companies/interplay) entry tells that story). Fargo left in 2002. "When you run a company like that, you become one and the same," he told *Edge* in 2013. "It was a shock for some time when it wasn't part of me."

## inXile and Kickstarter

Fargo founded inXile Entertainment in 2002. Its first big game was a new, lighter *The Bard's Tale* (2004), led on consoles, made, he told *Edge*, to be friendlier than his old games: "I would never recommend my own RPGs to people who had just got a system — it's just too much for them to figure out." inXile spent the next years mostly on smaller games for websites and phones, and in 2011 released *Hunted: The Demon's Forge*, whose title reused the name of his first commercial game.

He also wanted to make a sequel to *Wasteland*, and publishers were not interested. Fargo told *Edge* of one meeting, with *Fallout* co-creator Jason Anderson beside him, at which the executive sent text messages all the way through the presentation. On 13 March 2012 inXile asked players on Kickstarter for $900,000, with Fargo offering $100,000 of his own money if they got that far. The campaign closed on $2,933,252, one of the successes that restarted the old-style computer role-playing game (see [CRPG renaissance](/vault/phenomena/crpg-renaissance)). *Wasteland 2* came out in 2014. In 2013 *Torment: Tides of Numenera*, a successor to *Planescape: Torment*, asked for the same $900,000 and raised $4,188,927.

For *Wasteland 3*, inXile raised money through Fig, a crowdfunding platform Fargo had set up himself. In March 2017 he said he would retire once *Wasteland 3* was finished: "I've been at this since 1981... It seems like I should relax for a little bit." Then, in November 2018, [Microsoft](/vault/companies/microsoft) bought inXile, along with [Obsidian Entertainment](/vault/companies/obsidian-entertainment). "This will be the first time in my career that I will get to spend almost all of my time in development, something I've always wanted," Fargo told *Wireframe*, and he dropped his plans to retire. *Wasteland 3* came out in 2020.
