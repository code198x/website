As a boy in Machida, on the western edge of Tokyo, Satoshi Tajiri hunted insects so keenly that, his father later told *TIME*, the other children called him "Dr Bug". "Every time I found a new insect, it was mysterious to me. And the more I searched for insects, the more I found," he said. By the time he was a teenager the fields were being built over and he had moved on to the arcades, where he started a hand-written magazine about games. That magazine, *Game Freak*, became the name of his company, and the collector's pleasure of finding, keeping and swapping became *Pocket Monsters*, the Game Boy game known in English as *[Pokémon](/vault/games/pokemon-red-blue)*.

## Fast facts

- **Born:** 1965, and grew up in Machida, a suburb of Tokyo.
- **Fanzine:** *Game Freak*, from 1983.
- **Company:** [Game Freak](/vault/companies/game-freak), founded on 26 April 1989, with Tajiri as president.
- **Best known for:** *Pocket Monsters Red* and *Green* (1996), released in English as *[Pokémon Red & Blue](/vault/games/pokemon-red-blue)*.
- **Earlier games:** *Quinty* (1989, called *Mendel Palace* in America) and *Yoshi* (1991).

## Dr Bug

Tajiri's childhood hobby is the best-known part of his story, and he explained it himself in a rare interview with *TIME* in 1999. "They fascinated me. For one thing, they kind of moved funny. They were odd." He worked out his own methods: other children caught beetles with honey smeared on bark, but he put a stone under a tree, because beetles slept under stones during the day, and lifted it in the morning. "None of my friends would do what I did. I got more insects than anyone." When *TIME* asked whether Pokémon came from this, he agreed, and pointed at what had changed: "Places to catch insects are rare because of urbanization. Kids play inside their homes now."

## Game Freak, the magazine

"I was really into Space Invaders in about 1978," Tajiri said. "There wasn't any media to get information about games, so I came up with Game Freak magazine. It was handwritten. I stapled the pages together." *Wireframe* dates the first issue to 1983, when he was 17, and describes it as a guide to improving scores on arcade games such as [Namco](/vault/companies/namco)'s *[Xevious](/vault/games/xevious)* and *Mappy*; *Edge* adds that it published tips for [Shigeru Miyamoto](/vault/people/shigeru-miyamoto)'s games, such as *Donkey Kong*. Tajiri's best seller was a special issue on *Xevious*: "We sold 10,000 copies." The magazine brought him the artist [Ken Sugimori](/vault/people/ken-sugimori) and the designer and composer [Junichi Masuda](/vault/people/junichi-masuda), who would build the company with him.

He was making games as well as writing about them. "When I was 16, I won a contest Sega was sponsoring for a game idea," he told *TIME*. He learned to program by taking a Nintendo console apart "to see how it worked": "It took me two years to learn the programming, and a year to make my first game."

## Game Freak, the company

That first game was *Quinty*, a single-screen action game in which the player flips floor tiles to knock enemies over. Namco published it on the Famicom in 1989, the year Tajiri turned the magazine into a company; *Wireframe* suggests that his years of writing about Namco's arcade games helped get him through the door. [Hudson Soft](/vault/companies/hudson-soft) released it in America as *Mendel Palace*. Game Freak then made games for whoever would publish them, among them *Jerry Boy* for Sony, *Pulseman* for Sega and *Yoshi*, a falling-block puzzle game for [Nintendo](/vault/companies/nintendo) that *Wireframe* says Tajiri programmed in six months and that "paved the way for Pokémon".

## Pocket Monsters

Tajiri had the idea for Pokémon in 1990, according to *TIME*. Two things set it off. In a 2004 interview on the Japanese television programme *Game Center CX*, quoted by *Wireframe*, he said that *[Dragon Quest](/vault/games/dragon-quest) II* had frustrated him: Sugimori's copy had two of an item Tajiri needed, and there was no way to pass one across. The other was the [Game Boy](/vault/systems/game-boy)'s [link cable](/vault/techniques/link-cable). "In Tetris — its first game — the cable transmitted information about moving blocks," he told *TIME*. "I thought of actual living organisms moving back and forth across the cable. But everyone was using it to compete. The idea I had was for information to go back and forth. It wasn't about competition."

Nintendo was unsure. "I was told they couldn't really understand the concept of the game," Tajiri said, and by the time it was finished, "Game Boy's popularity was declining." Miyamoto advised him throughout. "I really look up to Miyamoto-san," Tajiri told *TIME*. "I'd memorize each piece of advice he gave." In the Japanese game the player's character is named Satoshi, after Tajiri, and his rival Shigeru, after Miyamoto; *Edge* read that as the younger designer following in the older one's footsteps.

The work took about six years. *Pocket Monsters Red* and *Green* came out in Japan on 27 February 1996, each with a slightly different set of monsters, so that no one could collect them all without trading with a friend over the cable. *Retro Gamer* put their sales in Japan at 10.23 million, almost four million more than the previous record holder, *[Super Mario Bros.](/vault/games/super-mario-bros)* The games, the television series and the trading cards that followed made Pokémon a worldwide craze from 1998 (see [the Pokémon phenomenon](/vault/phenomena/pokemon-phenomenon)); the Pokémon comic that *Nintendo Power* serialised in 1999 carried the credit "Based on a story by Satoshi Tajiri".

## After Red and Green

Tajiri has stayed at the head of Game Freak, which has made every main-series Pokémon game since, and has given very few interviews. In July 2005 *Electronic Gaming Monthly* placed him ninth in its list of the most influential people in the games industry: "It was 1996, and Nintendo's Game Boy was starting to run out of steam. Along came Satoshi Tajiri and 151 of his closest friends."
