## Overview

*CRASH* puts it plainly: "**In 1982, Kevin Toms wrote *Football Manager*, six years (and half a million copies sold) later, Kevin went one better and produced *Football Manager 2*.**" Addictive Games' own advertising bills him as "**inventor of the world's best selling football game**".

## Fast facts

- **Wrote:** *Football Manager* (1982) and *Football Manager 2* (1988), for Addictive Games.
- **Sales:** **half a million copies** of the first game, per *CRASH*.
- **Credit line:** "Designed by Kevin Toms", printed on the packaging and the advertising.
- **Named in 54 issues** of the British magazines held here.

## A game with no game in it

*Football Manager* has almost nothing on screen. You pick a team, buy and sell players, choose a side, and watch results arrive. The famous "match highlights" are a handful of stick figures moving between two goals for a few seconds.

By the standards of 1982 arcade software it is barely a computer game at all — and it sold half a million copies, because it identified something the arcade tradition had missed entirely: **a great many people want to run the team, not be the striker**.

Everything the genre still does was there at the start. A squad with attributes. A transfer market where the interesting decision is what to sell. A league that grinds on whether you are ready or not. Injuries that ruin a plan. The pleasure is in **the spreadsheet you cannot see**, and in the fact that the world keeps score.

It also fits the hardware perfectly, which is not a coincidence. A management game needs numbers, text and a little logic — no scrolling, no sprites, no attribute clash. On a 16K machine that is the difference between a game that can exist and one that cannot.

## The face on the box

*Football Manager*'s packaging carried a **photograph of Kevin Toms**, bearded, looking at the camera. Not a logo, not an illustration — the author.

That is a remarkable thing to find on a mass-market product in 1982, and it says something about how software was sold before it had a marketing profession. The proposition was: **this is a thing a person made, and here he is.** It is closer to a book jacket than to a games box, and it worked well enough that the photograph became as recognisable as the game.

Compare it with [Steve Crow](/vault/people/steve-crow) billed in Bubble Bus's advertising, or *ZZAP!* telling readers a game was [John Twiddy](/vault/people/john-twiddy)'s. The author as the selling point runs through the whole early British industry, and Toms took it furthest.

## Still at it

Toms did not stop. He returned to the game decades later with a new version for
modern machines — released under **a slightly different title**, because the
name *Football Manager* had long since passed to Sports Interactive and become
one of the largest franchises in the industry.

There is an irony worth stating in that. The genre he invented in 1982 grew
into something enormous; the name he gave it went with the genre and not
with him; and when its author came back to it he had to call his own game
something else.

## Why he matters

**The management game** starts here: a genre invented in Britain, on the cheapest available hardware, by identifying an audience the rest of the industry could not see. Its descendants outsell most of what this Vault covers.

He is also the clearest case of a British programmer whose reputation rests on **understanding players, not hardware**. Nothing in *Football Manager* is technically difficult. Everything in it is a correct guess about what someone wants to do with an evening.
