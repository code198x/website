Charles Cecil's first games were text adventures for the ZX81, sold by mail order from Hull while he was still a student. Ten years later his own company, [Revolution Software](/vault/companies/revolution-software), was making point-and-click adventures in which the villagers kept to their own daily routines whether or not the player was watching. Its *[Broken Sword](/vault/games/broken-sword)* (1996) sent an American tourist after the Knights Templar years before *The Da Vinci Code* did the same. Cecil went on making adventures after most publishers had stopped commissioning them, and when the money ran out he found it again, first through Apple and then through Kickstarter.

## Fast facts

- **Born:** August 1962. He spent his early childhood in the Congo and went to Bedales School in Hampshire.
- **First games:** *Inca Curse*, *Ship of Doom* and *Espionage Island*, text adventures for Artic Computing.
- **Before Revolution:** Paragon Programming, then head of development at [US Gold](/vault/companies/us-gold), then Activision's European development studio.
- **Company:** co-founder and managing director of [Revolution Software](/vault/companies/revolution-software), founded in 1990.
- **Best-known games:** *[Lure of the Temptress](/vault/games/lure-of-the-temptress)* (1992), *[Beneath a Steel Sky](/vault/games/beneath-a-steel-sky)* (1994), the *[Broken Sword](/vault/games/broken-sword)* series (1996 onwards).
- **Honour:** MBE, in the Queen's Birthday Honours of June 2011, for services to the computer games industry.

## Congo, Bedales and Ford

Cecil told *Edge* in 2012 that his father had taken a job in the newly independent Republic of the Congo, and that when Charles was 18 months old a Congolese soldier pointed a gun at his head after the boy hit him with a stone; the family's gardener talked the soldier down. His pregnant mother fled the country with him, and the family lived for a time in Nigeria before settling in Britain. *Edge* suggested the experience lay behind the globe-trotting of his later games. "I'm sure some of that time affected me," Cecil said, though he traced the Paris of *Broken Sword* to visits in the late 1960s to an uncle, a Portuguese communist who had fought against Franco.

He met computers at Bedales School. When he left school in 1980, he told *Edge* in 1996, Ford sponsored him to study engineering; *Retro Gamer* names the course as mechanical engineering at the University of Manchester. Through Ford he met Richard Turner, who had just set up Artic Computing.

## Artic Computing

Turner persuaded Cecil to write games for Artic, and Cecil wrote three of its early text adventures. An Artic advertisement in *Your Computer* in October 1982, giving the company's address in James Reckitt Avenue, Hull, sold them by letter: *Planet of Death* (Adventure A) at £5, *Inca Curse* (Adventure B) at £6, *Ship of Doom* (Adventure C) at £7 and *Espionage Island* (Adventure D) at £5. Cecil later admitted to *Wireframe* that *Inca Curse* borrowed "perhaps too liberally" from *Raiders of the Lost Ark*.

In 1996 he remembered the early business as effortless. "We used to ring up WH Smiths and say, 'How many do you want to buy?' and then we'd call up the duplicators and tell them how many we had sold, and they'd make that many. No overheads." The Spectrum brought bigger sales, but a television advertising campaign, he said, "was a complete disaster", and larger publishers such as Ocean and US Gold arrived "with a lot of money and a lot of professional marketing". "In 1986 we decided to call it a day."

Artic had one last deal. [US Gold](/vault/companies/us-gold) held the licence for the 1986 World Cup and had no game, so its Tim Chaney and Geoff Brown went, in Chaney's words, "to Charles Cecil at Artic" to reuse Artic's two-year-old *World Cup Football*. It came out as *World Cup Carnival* and was savaged by the press. "I was really uneasy about this, but we were effectively bankrupt," Cecil told *Wireframe*. "So I'm quite ashamed to say we took the money."

## Paragon, US Gold and Activision

After Artic, Cecil set up Paragon Programming, based in Wandsworth in London, which converted games for US Gold and other publishers; he recalled its first job as *Infiltrator* in 1986, taken from Chris Gray's Commodore 64 original to the Spectrum and Atari. Two Artic programmers, Donald Campbell and John Prince, joined him, but as northerners they "didn't really feel part of Paragon", Campbell said, and in 1987 they left to form [Tiertex](/vault/companies/tiertex). Then Chaney invited him to join US Gold as head of development. "I imagined I was going to rule over a massive great department and it turned out it was me and Steve Fitton, and Dave Partington part time," he said.

Development, he told *Edge*, "wasn't seen as particularly important then. Sales and marketing were of far greater concern." [Activision](/vault/companies/activision) offered him a job running development in Europe, in the studio that also produced [Electric Dreams](/vault/companies/electric-dreams) games; among its projects was *Hunter*, "a really nice 3D game" for the ST and Amiga. At Activision he also tried to license *Watchmen* for a game, and met its artist, Dave Gibbons. The licence came to nothing, but the contact did not. Activision's American parent, then called Mediagenic, had lost nearly $20 million the previous year, *ACE* reported in October 1990. In Cecil's account, the failure of the American office "gave me the chance to set up Revolution, with our own money".

## Revolution Software

Cecil founded Revolution in 1990 with the programmers Tony Warriner and David Sykes, and later Noirin Carmody. The company's story is told in the [Revolution Software](/vault/companies/revolution-software) entry. Cecil's part in it was as designer and managing director: he designed and wrote, or co-wrote, its adventures, and he chose their settings from places he had visited. For *Broken Sword* he read *The Holy Blood and the Holy Grail* and walked the Paris catacombs. "It's important to get references like this and I've done it with all of the games I have written," he told *Retro Gamer*.

His aims were plain in a 1996 interview with *Edge*: "To write games that play really well, and support them with high production values. Completely the opposite of the interactive movie." He and his lead writer, Dave Cummins, went on a film-writing course, and the *Broken Sword* script was read by a senior BBC scriptwriter. He had little patience with puzzles that broke their own logic: "I've never forgiven *Monkey Island* for using the compass on the key."

By the end of the 1990s he could see the 2D adventure fading. In a *PC Zone* feature at Christmas 1998 he said that "the point-and-click adventure evolved to best exploit the technology that existed at the time", that other genres had since advanced faster, and that adventure fans, though "fiercely loyal", were "declining in numbers and this has made publishers nervous about commissioning this type of game". Announcing the 3D *Broken Sword: The Sleeping Dragon* in 2002, he put it more briefly: "The point-and-click adventure game is dead, long live the adventure game!" He later explained that he had meant the genre in its old form, not the interface, and he brought pointing and clicking back for *Broken Sword: The Angel of Death* (2006). He also watched the rest of the industry with interest: in April 2000 he told *Edge* that the acting in Sega's *[Shenmue](/vault/games/shenmue)* was of high quality, "but it cost $40m dollars to produce".

## Going it alone, and coming back

After *The Sleeping Dragon* Revolution closed its studio and made its staff redundant. Cecil described the result to *Edge* in April 2006 as a Hollywood-style arrangement: he owned the *Broken Sword* rights, contracted the publisher, THQ, and worked with the Sheffield studio Sumo Digital and freelancers, spending about half his time at Sumo's offices. He had worked in the same way on the early design of *The Da Vinci Code* game, and he was a director of Game Republic, a network of game companies in Yorkshire.

He told *Wireframe* in 2019 that Revolution came close to being wound up after *The Angel of Death*: "We were insolvent. I remember telling my wife, 'We're going to have to bankrupt the company.'" A call from Apple, asking whether Revolution would bring its games to the iPhone, changed that; by 2012 the iPhone editions of the first two *Broken Sword* games had been downloaded more than five million times, according to *Edge*. He also helped create the BBC's *Doctor Who* adventure games. In 2012 Revolution raised the money for *Broken Sword 5: The Serpent's Curse* on Kickstarter. "Kickstarter is crucial for developers like us," Cecil told *Retro Gamer*. "Until a couple of years ago, the publishing model was totally broken for independent developers and we could not afford to operate."

In June 2011 he was appointed MBE for services to the computer games industry. "I have had the privilege, and good fortune, to have been creating computer games since the inception of this extraordinary industry," he said. He went on to make *Beyond a Steel Sky* (2020) with Dave Gibbons, a sequel to *Beneath a Steel Sky*, and *Broken Sword – Shadow of the Templars: Reforged* (2024), a remastered version of the first *Broken Sword*.
