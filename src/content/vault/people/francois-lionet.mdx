Two of the best-known ways into programming a 16-bit home computer carry François Lionet's name. [STOS](/vault/tools/stos), on the Atari ST, went to number one in the Gallup ST chart in its first week on sale in 1988, ahead of every game. [AMOS](/vault/tools/amos), which he wrote for the Amiga, was where many Amiga owners wrote their first program. Neither asked its users to learn machine code first: sprites, scrolling, music and screens came as BASIC commands. And by his own account, the man who made them is "not really a gamer".

## Beginnings

Lionet told *Wireframe* in 2021 that his first computer was an Ohio Scientific Superboard II, in 1981: a 6502 machine with 4K of memory and Microsoft BASIC, and documentation only in English. "I turned it on having never touched a computer before, typed PRINT "Hello World" and done! I had a program."

*Amiga Shopper* named his first game as *Chickin Chase*, for the Commodore 64: a rooster keeps his hens laying while fighting off the rats, snakes and hedgehogs that come for the eggs. *Amiga Shopper* said Rainbird published it, but period advertisements show the C64 game on sale in Britain in late 1985 at £3.95 in the Super Silver range of [Firebird](/vault/companies/firebird); Rainbird was Firebird's premium label. *Commodore User*'s reviewer noted that it had been "written by a Frenchman" and called it "the first software I've ever seen with naughty bits in it".

## STOS and Jawx

STOS came from Jawx International, a Paris software house. In 1988 Jawx's directors, Frederique Pinelet and Jacques Fleurance, flew over from France to sign a joint venture with Mandarin Software, and Mandarin released STOS that autumn at £29.95. Magazines at the time credited Jawx rather than any one programmer. The program's own title screen credits "F Lionet & C Sotiropoulos", and in 1991 *Amiga Format* described Lionet as "one of the Jawx team".

STOS sold better than anyone expected of a programming language. *ST Amiga Format* reported in November 1988 that it had topped the Gallup ST chart after one week in the shops, pushing Firebird's *Virus* off the top spot.

## AMOS

Jawx had no plans for an Amiga version, according to *Amiga Format*'s 1991 history of AMOS. Mandarin, encouraged by STOS's sales, wanted one, and Lionet was "set to tinkering". He "became a convert to the Amiga almost overnight". He started in 1989, and *Amiga Shopper* later recorded that he kept working on it after he was called up for military service in France. AMOS came out on 12 June 1990. His service ended in March 1991, the same month the French version of AMOS was launched.

AMOS went further than STOS. It had several screens at once with their own resolutions and palettes, hardware sprites and blitter objects, access to the [copper](/vault/hardware/copper), and AMAL, a small animation language of its own. A compiler, AMOS 3D, Easy AMOS and AMOS Professional followed between 1991 and 1993. Mandarin's advertisements reported UK sales past 25,000 by December 1990 and "over 40,000 users" by November 1991; these are the publisher's figures, and no later audited total has been published.

AMOS programs ranged from public domain games to commercial releases. *Scorched Tanks*, Michael Welch's $10 [shareware](/vault/distribution/shareware) artillery game based on the PC's *Scorched Earth*, was one of *Amiga Shopper*'s AMOS recommendations in 1994, alongside the full-price *Jetstrike*. *Wireframe* lists *Flight of the Amazon Queen* and Vulcan's *Valhalla* games among the notable commercial titles.

Lionet told *Wireframe* that when he wrote AMOS he "wasn't aware of how widely used it was". Demo coders sneered at it: "some would call it 'Lame-os'". Decades later he began receiving letters from people who said they owed their careers in IT to it.

## Klik & Play and Clickteam

By early 1994 Europress, as Mandarin was now known, told *Amiga Shopper* that Lionet had stopped working on AMOS. That August the magazine reported what he was doing instead: a games-creating program for the PC that needed no programming, where "even a novice will be able to create a complete game in a couple of hours just by pointing and clicking". It was *Klik & Play*, published by Europress for Windows later in 1994. Lionet wrote it with Yves Lamoureux, and together they founded Clickteam.

Clickteam followed *Klik & Play* with *The Games Factory* in 1996 and *Multimedia Fusion* in 1998, according to a 2004 *Retro Gamer* feature, and with *Jamagic*, a 2D and 3D game-creation system with its own scripting language, in 2001. Lionet later said he felt "a bit guilty" about *Klik & Play*, "a script-free programming tool", because he now argues that tools which teach only logic do not teach coding: "The vision of AOZ is to bring back the joy of actual, intricate coding."

## Back to BASIC

The AMOS source code was released to the public in 2012. In 2019 Lionet announced AMOS 2, a new version that would produce JavaScript and HTML5 programs for modern computers and phones. It became AOZ Studio, which he set up with Laurent Weill, a co-founder of the French publisher Loriciel. AOZ is again a dialect of BASIC and can load AMOS programs. "BASIC is the perfect language," he told *Wireframe*. "The names of the instructions reflect the actions they have."
