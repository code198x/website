The Spectrum's own BASIC had no `ELSE`, no named procedures and only one kind of loop. Beta BASIC added all three without taking anything away. It loaded from tape, and after that the machine looked almost the same: *CRASH* noticed that "even the copyright message looks like the Sinclair one". The difference showed up as you typed. The new keywords went in with single keypresses like the old ones, from graphics mode, and every line was still checked as it was entered.

Its author was Andy Wright, and Betasoft sold it by mail order from 92 Oxford Road, Moseley, Birmingham. It grew from release 1.0 in 1983 to version 4.0 in 1987, and *CRASH*'s technical columnist Simon N Goodwin came to call it "peerless". When the SAM Coupé was designed at the end of the decade, Wright wrote the BASIC in its ROM.

## Fast facts

- **Author:** Andy Wright.
- **Publisher:** Betasoft, Birmingham.
- **First advertised:** *Sinclair User*, November 1983 (release 1.0, 16K and 48K Spectrum).
- **Versions:** 1.0 (1983), 1.8 (1984, 48K), 3.0 (1985), 4.0 (1987, Spectrum 128 and +2).
- **Prices:** £11.00 for releases 1.0 and 1.8; £9.95 for 3.0 in early 1986; £15.95 for 4.0, or £8.95 to upgrade from 3.0.
- **Memory used:** about 18K on a 48K Spectrum for version 3.0; 25K on the 128 for version 4.0.

## From 26 keywords to over 100

Betasoft's first advertisements, in the autumn of 1983, offered "26 new keywords" and "10 new functions" for the 16K and 48K Spectrum. By July 1984 release 1.8, for the 48K machine only, cost £11.00, with a £4.00 upgrade for owners of release 1.0. The advertisement listed the features that stayed at its heart: "Commands are single entry keywords in graphics mode. Syntax is checked on entry. Fully compatible with Spectrum Basic. Microdrive compatible."

The New Zealand magazine *Bits & Bytes* reviewed 1.8 in November 1984. Its reviewer counted "more than 50 new commands and functions" and thought the result was "a language that puts the BASIC implementation of most micros to shame". He liked the way Betasoft had placed the new keywords: "LOOP is on the L key, and CLOCK on the C key". By then Beta BASIC was also being made under licence in New Zealand.

Version 3.0 appeared in the summer of 1985, in a video-style box with a 90-page manual printed on bright red paper. By version 4.0 in 1987, Goodwin counted "more than 100 new commands and functions".

## What it added

Structured programming was the main attraction. Goodwin, reviewing version 3.0 for *CRASH* in February 1986, called it "a computer scientist's dream":

- **Procedures.** `DEF PROC` names a procedure and `PROC` calls it, with parameters that can be local or passed by reference, and default values for any left out. `PROC check answer` replaces `GO SUB 1000`. Recursion works.
- **`ELSE`**, including a multi-line `IF...THEN...ELSE`.
- **`DO...LOOP`**, with `WHILE` and `UNTIL` conditions and exits from any point. These structures can be nested freely.
- **`ON ERROR`**, to send errors (including BREAK) to a subroutine.

There were also features for the screen. Output could be confined to a window, in character sizes down to 64 columns, and areas of the screen could be scrolled or rolled pixel by pixel in any direction. `ALTER` changed colour attributes across the screen in one statement, and `FILL` coloured in a bounded shape. Printing could be placed with pixel accuracy, and `USING` formatted numbers for tables.

A toolkit made editing easier. Beta BASIC could number lines automatically, renumber, move, save or delete groups of lines, and search and replace text in a program. It could `JOIN` and `SPLIT` lines, edit a line by number without listing it first, and indent loops and tests in listings to show their structure. A `CLOCK` command kept time and could call a subroutine at a set moment.

It even made some things faster. Sinclair BASIC's `GO TO`, `GO SUB`, `RETURN` and `FOR` loops slow down as a program grows, and Beta BASIC made them "up to 20 times faster in long programs". Ordinary statements ran at the same "slothful rate" as before, and there was little machine-code support beyond two-byte `PEEK` and `POKE`.

## Typing it in

Beta BASIC let you keep the Spectrum's keyword keys, switch to typing words letter by letter, or mix the two. In the mixed mode a keyword at the start of a statement came from one keypress, but typing a space first let you spell it out. Goodwin used the mixed mode "most of the time", since it kept `LET`, `PRINT` and `GO TO` to one key without making you hunt for the rarer keywords.

## Battle of the BASICs

In February 1986 *CRASH* set Beta BASIC 3.0 against two rivals: Mega BASIC, sold by the publishers of *Your Spectrum*, and Ocean IQ's Laser BASIC, a sprite system for games. Goodwin wrote that there was "no clear winner", but "I preferred Beta BASIC, mainly because of its elegance and comprehensive support for software engineering". The Spectrum, he argued, had been designed "as a programmer's machine rather than a games one", and "Beta BASIC takes the Spectrum a long way onward as a programmer's machine". Betasoft also produced a Beta BASIC newsletter, which he described as "fourteen typed pages crammed with neat routines and tips".

## Beta BASIC 4.0 and the 128

The Spectrum 128 arrived with only two new BASIC commands, `PLAY` and `SPECTRUM`. Goodwin's August 1987 review opened: "AT LAST it's worth programming the Spectrum 128 in BASIC". Version 4.0 gave BASIC control of the new hardware. A new `BEEP` drove the three-channel sound chip in the background, with hundreds of sounds queued, so a program kept running while it played. Arrays could be kept on the 128's RAM disk, giving about 73K of extra variable space, and sorted or searched there. `FILL` used the RAM disk as a work area and could fill "almost any shape" in under a second, `DRAW` ran about two and a half times faster and `CIRCLE` 12 times faster.

Goodwin's verdict: "If you intend spending a few hours programming the 128 in BASIC, Beta BASIC 4.0 is the best utility you could buy. It's not cheap, but you get a heck of a lot for your money." He also noted what it did not do: there were "no masked sprite commands for games programmers".

## SAM BASIC

Wright's next BASIC was built into a computer: the SAM Coupé, a Spectrum-compatible machine from Miles Gordon Technology (MGT) with 256K of RAM and a 32K ROM. *CRASH* reported in February 1989 that the ROM was "written by Dr Andy Wright, the author of Spectrum Beta Basic", and would use letter-by-letter entry "rather than the 48K Spectrum keyword system". *ACE* repeated the credit in November 1989.
