**Inform** lets an author write a new [text adventure](/vault/genres/text-adventure) and turn it into a story file for an interpreter. **Graham Nelson** first released it in 1993, extending the creative life of the [Z-machine](/vault/tools/z-machine) beyond [Infocom](/vault/companies/infocom)’s original commercial games.

Nelson’s *Designer’s Manual* explains a distinction at the centre of the system. The **compiler** translates the author’s program. The **library** supplies a parser, which interprets typed commands, and a world model, which represents places, objects and their usual behaviour.

## Describe a world, then change its rules

An author can define rooms, put objects inside them and give those objects properties. A container might be openable; an object might provide light. Library rules handle many ordinary interactions, while the author adds behaviour for the particular game.

That makes a command more than a text match. Taking a key changes the model of where it is; later descriptions and actions can respond to that changed state. The manual separates the player’s words, the action they request and the rules which decide what follows.

## The language changed

Nelson’s announcement of **Inform 7**, dated 30 April 2006, looks back to Inform 1’s release on the same date in 1993 and Inform 6’s in 1996. It presents Inform 7 as a redesigned application using a notation closer to written English, rather than a small syntax revision to Inform 6.

The older language continued to matter. When the **Interactive Fiction Technology Foundation** took over hosting the Inform 6 site in 2018, Andrew Plotkin explained that people still wrote directly in it and that it remained part of Inform 7’s toolchain. The preserved site is historical documentation, not a current download catalogue.

Inform’s importance lies in both parts: a language for authors’ particular ideas, and shared machinery for the many responses a player might try. [TADS](/vault/tools/tads) provides another extensive adventure-writing system; [DAAD](/vault/tools/daad) approaches portability through its own adventure databases and machine-specific interpreters.
