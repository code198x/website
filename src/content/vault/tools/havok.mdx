Havok is a physics engine: a library that works out how objects in a game move, fall, collide and tumble, so that the programmers do not have to. It came out of Dublin at the end of the 1990s. Its first customers were racing games, where vehicle dynamics were the point. Havok 2.0, in 2003, turned it towards characters and ragdolls, and *[Half-Life 2](/vault/games/half-life-2)* put objects driven by it into players' hands. By 2005 *[Edge](/vault/magazines/edge)* called it "the standard by which the others are judged". Intel bought the company in 2007, and Microsoft bought it from Intel in 2015.

## Fast facts

- **Origin:** founded in Dublin in 1998 by Steve Collins and Hugh Reynolds, "spun out of research from Dublin University", meaning Trinity College.
- **First releases:** beta code to developers in 1999; the complete package was first shown in 2000.
- **Merger:** with the German physics company Ipion, reported in March 2001, leading to version 1.5.
- **Havok 2.0:** due at GDC 2003; "up to ten ragdoll models in realtime".
- **Named users:** [Valve](/vault/companies/valve) (from 2000), [Remedy](/vault/companies/remedy-entertainment) (*Max Payne 2*), [Ion Storm](/vault/companies/ion-storm) (*Deus Ex: Invisible War*, *Thief III*), Nihilistic (*StarCraft: Ghost*), [Bungie](/vault/companies/bungie) (*Halo 2*), EA.
- **Owners:** independent until Intel bought it in 2007; [Microsoft](/vault/companies/microsoft) from 2015.

## Arrival

*Edge*'s November 2000 Codeshop column found the company "in the middle of a licensing frenzy". A year after the beta, and six months after the complete package was first shown, the team had "already grown from eight to 35". Its business development officer, Alan Murphy, was already saying "We are working with Valve". By March 2001 Havok had merged with Ipion and was combining the two companies' code into version 1.5 of what its communications director, Paul Hayes, called "our Uber-engine". A cheaper, cut-down edition covering hard bodies and constraints was planned for studios that did not need soft bodies. In June 2001 came Reactor, a physics plug-in for the 3DS Max modelling package, with rope, cloth and fluid dynamics, at £745 on its own.

Havok's main rival was the Oxford company MathEngine, whose Paul Topping put the pitch for the whole category in one line: "It's not about better physics, it's about a faster implementation of physics while making games."

## Havok 2.0 and characters

Havok's chief technical officer, Steve Collins, explained the first wave to *Edge* in March 2003. Middleware had made its "initial impact not by providing developers with a set of new features but because it offered existing features cheaper and faster". So the first titles "were concentrated within realistic genres such as vehicle-based racers": "With a car game, the vehicle dynamics are a core part of the gameplay, so developers were happy to spend processing resources simulating high-fidelity dynamics." But "now, 70 per cent of top ten games are about characters", and "gamers aren't happy seeing characters sliding along with their shoulder or arm embedded in the wall anymore. It removes the illusion of reality."

Havok 2.0, due at that year's Game Developers Conference, was the answer. Collins said its core had been re-engineered because "when you start off building a physics engine, you concentrate on the maths and tend to end up with something which is too processor expensive". The new version could run the dynamics of up to ten ragdolls where the old one had struggled with one at a time. The ten beta partners included Remedy, whose Markus Stein reported collision detection and character controllers implemented "in a short timeframe" for the *Max Payne* sequel; Nihilistic, on Blizzard's *StarCraft: Ghost*; Ion Storm, on *Deus Ex: Invisible War* and *Thief III*; Activision; and Valve.

## In games

*Edge*'s review of *Max Payne 2* in January 2004 noted that "most objects within the game are animated with the Havok physics engine" while the light sources stayed static. Its May 2004 review of *Astro Boy* called it "the first Japanese title to use the Havok physics engine", used "tentatively rather than with the abandon seen elsewhere". Its *Half-Life 2* review in December 2004 announced a change in kind: "Finally we have a game in which physics are more than just an excuse for ragdoll deaths... This time the world is there to be grasped." The gravity gun was "the core tool", used "to drag objects, pick them up and carry them about" and, in its second mode, "to drop, throw or smash them away".

## The standard

By May 2005 *Edge*'s survey of physics vendors put Havok first: "the standard by which the others are judged — not least in terms of the games it powers, which include Half-Life 2, Halo 2, Full Spectrum Warrior and Medal Of Honor: Pacific Assault." It listed Havok's platforms as GameCube, PC, PlayStation 2 and Xbox, with the next generation of consoles to come. The product line had grown to include Havok Animation, and Collins had turned to tools and to the new machines: "We can't wait for Cell." His summary of what the company now sold was: "Physics engines aren't about lines of code any more... Tools are what we spend most of our time on."

## Intel and Microsoft

In 2007 Intel bought Havok. *Edge* counted "over 150 games" that had used it by then, including *Halo 3*, *Test Drive Unlimited* and *Crackdown*, and expected Intel to optimise it for Larrabee, the graphics processor it was developing. Commentators worried that the engine might run less well on chips from Intel's rivals, AMD and Nvidia, and that developers might turn to Ageia's competing PhysX instead. Havok kept working as a separate business. When *Edge* visited the Dublin office in 2011, the company had seven products, from physics and cloth to AI and animation, updated its tools four times a year, and had offered its physics engine free for PC developers since 2008, which *Edge* credited to Intel's ownership. Its founders had moved on. Collins and Reynolds started a new company, New Game Technology, which became Kore Virtual Machines in 2008, sold its console scripting system back to Havok and was rebranded again as Swrve, a Dublin firm supplying player data to game developers. Collins had also helped set up Trinity College's master's course in interactive entertainment technology.

Microsoft bought Havok from Intel in October 2015. By then the engine had been used in more than 600 games, and Microsoft said it would keep licensing it to the rest of the industry, including to Sony and Nintendo.
