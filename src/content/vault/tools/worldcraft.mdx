**Worldcraft** is the level editor created by **Ben Morris**, first associated with [Quake](/vault/games/quake) and later adopted by Valve. Its development continued under the **Hammer** name. It lets a designer arrange a place before the game’s engine runs it.

In March 1997, **Marc Laidlaw** wrote in *WIRED* about using the shareware editor to build his own Quake map. His account describes starting with basic solid forms and combining them into a space with tunnels, platforms and secrets. Valve announced its acquisition of Worldcraft and Morris joining the company that July.

## Work with solid pieces

Worldcraft calls its solid building pieces *brushes*. Walls, floors and ceilings can be assembled from them. Plan and side views help position those pieces, while a textured 3D view shows what the room looks like from within.

The original Forge tutorial builds two rooms joined by a corridor. It hollows the rooms, ungroups the resulting walls and resizes individual brushes to make an opening. Copying and grouping help the designer manage a structure made from several parts.

That example also reveals an important habit: the visible room is the empty space between solid pieces. Building a convincing interior means thinking about both the space a player occupies and the geometry around it.

## From a map to a playable place

The editor belongs to a larger workflow. Shapes describe the level; game entities give it things that respond, such as doors, lights and opponents. Compiling prepares the map for the engine that will use it.

[GoldSrc](/vault/tools/goldsrc) is the runtime family behind [Half-Life](/vault/games/half-life), while Worldcraft provides its map-making tools. Keeping those roles distinct helps explain how a community can share an engine yet make different places to play.

The [Build editor](/vault/tools/build-engine) offers a useful comparison. Both connect a plan to a view from inside, but they construct their worlds from different geometric ingredients.
