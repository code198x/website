In 2011 two students, Matthew Thomas Woolven and Marco Marino, told *Edge* how their game *GLiD* had started: "a little experiment over the summer holidays with Unity, because I found out it was free." Neither of them, Marino said, "really had any coding knowledge before Unity, so it's taught us, basically". The game won a prize at the Independent Games Festival, and the pair dropped out of university to make games.

That was the change Unity made. Before it, a small team that wanted a 3D engine had to write one or pay for one. Unity's founders had faced the same choice. "We actually started out as a game company," its chief executive, David Helgason, told *Edge*, "and we just remembered how we couldn't afford anything on the market apart from GarageGames, which we didn't really like. So we had to build something ourselves!" They turned the engine into the product, and in 2009 they began giving it away.

## Fast facts

- **Made by:** Unity Technologies, founded in Copenhagen by David Helgason, Joachim Ante and Nicholas Francis.
- **First release:** June 2005, at Apple's Worldwide Developers Conference; it could publish games only for Mac OS X.
- **Free version:** from October 2009, with version 2.6.
- **Scripting:** C#, and in its early years also UnityScript, a language close to JavaScript.
- **Users:** 13,000 before the free version; 250,000 by late 2010; 1.9 million registered developers, 400,000 of them active each month, by 2013.
- **Games made with it include:** *Thomas Was Alone*, *Inside*, *Cuphead* and *Gris*.

## From the Mac to the iPhone

The founders met online and settled in Copenhagen. Work on the engine began in the early 2000s, and Helgason dated the company's first business plan to 2004. Their own game, *GooBall*, made little impression, but the engine they had built for it went on stage at Apple's developer conference in June 2005. For its first year it could make games only for Mac OS X. Windows and web browsers followed, and *Edge* described the company in 2013 as having grown "from a focused OS X game creation tool to one of the most widely used engines in existence today".

The iPhone made it. Unity released a version for Apple's phone in 2008, soon after the App Store opened, and by early 2010 Helgason was calling it the most popular engine for iPhone games, used in more than 350 of them. It cost $399 for a basic licence and $1,499 for an advanced one, per seat. "The business opportunity turned out to be better than anyone had expected," he told *Edge*, "and not only did the iPhone's PowerVR graphics chip take really well to Unity, but the iPhone has overall become a truly great gaming device." The company had just raised $5.5 million in its first round of venture funding.

## Giving it away

Until 2009 the cheapest version cost $200. "It seemed like a really low price point for people that are really committed," Helgason said, "but there are a lot of people in the world for whom $200 isn't a small amount at all, and a lot of students that can't really afford $200 out of their budget." In October 2009, with version 2.6, Unity made its basic version free. The only condition was that the user's company had earned less than $100,000 in the previous year, and games made with it paid no royalties.

The users came quickly. Unity had about 13,000 users before the free version, over 100,000 by the spring of 2010 and 250,000 by the end of that year. By mid-2011 Helgason counted 450,000 developers. In 2013 the company claimed 1.9 million registered developers, 400,000 of them active each month, and announced that basic publishing to mobile phones would be free too. Its development director, Erik Juhl, described how it spread inside big studios: programmers at "EA or Activision or whatever" went home and tinkered with Unity, and at the next project meeting asked, "Hey, why aren't we using Unity?"

Unity 3, released in 2010, could export one project to the web, iOS, Android, PC, Mac and the three big consoles. The paid Pro version, at €1,100, added features such as real-time shadows and deferred rendering. "We've always wanted it so that you can bootstrap a game company or career using the free version," said Nicholas Francis, one of the founders. Late in 2010 Unity opened its Asset Store, where developers sold each other models, tools and plug-ins, with Unity taking 30 per cent. By 2011 the National Film and Television School was planning to teach it, and Helgason called it "almost a common language in the production groups".

## How it works

A Unity game is built in an editor. In a beginner's tutorial for *Wireframe* in 2018, the starting point is a scene with a camera and a light. The developer adds objects, here a capsule from the GameObject menu to stand in for the player, and gives each one behaviour by attaching components to it in a panel called the Inspector. A script is one more component: a short program, usually written in C#, attached to the object it controls. Pressing the play button runs the game inside the editor, so the developer can try a change at once. "Being able to just hit the play button and try your game, that's insanely helpful," said Woolven.

The approach suited people learning as they went. Mike Bithell taught himself to program while making *Thomas Was Alone*, a platform game about coloured rectangles, in his evenings after work; its folder on his computer, he told *Edge*, was still called "Teaching Myself Unity". The game looks two-dimensional, but Bithell built it in 3D "due to the way Unity worked at the time". Playdead's *Inside* was developed with the help of PlayMaker, a visual scripting add-on for Unity, and *Wireframe* described *Gris* as a platform game that, "like *Cuphead*", used Unity "to striking effect".

## The runtime fee

On 12 September 2023 Unity announced that it would charge developers a fee for each installation of their games once those games passed certain thresholds. Developers were furious, and some well-known studios said they would stop using the engine. Unity's chief executive, John Riccitiello, retired with immediate effect on 9 October 2023. On 12 September 2024 his successor, Matt Bromberg, cancelled the fee "effective immediately". Unity went back to charging by subscription: the free Personal version became available to developers earning up to $200,000 a year, double the old limit, and Unity Pro rose by 8 per cent to $2,200 a seat.
