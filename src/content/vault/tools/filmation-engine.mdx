On Friday 9 November 1984 the first copies of Ultimate's *[Knight Lore](/vault/games/knight-lore)* reached the *CRASH* offices in Ludlow. A rare Ultimate press release had promised "an entirely new three-dimensional concept with superb animation called Filmation", and "anxious readers ran riot with their letters". What they got was a castle drawn in solid-looking forced perspective, in which Sabreman could walk behind a pillar and disappear from view, climb onto a block and jump across a gap, all without the flicker that usually came with moving graphics on a [ZX Spectrum](/vault/systems/sinclair-zx-spectrum). *CRASH* gave it 94%, with 97% for graphics, and one reviewer called it "probably the best game yet produced for the Spectrum".

Filmation was a name, not a product. It covered the way [Ultimate Play the Game](/vault/companies/ultimate), the trading name of the Stamper brothers' Ashby Computers and Graphics, drew and moved things in *Knight Lore* and *Alien 8*. A second version, Filmation II, made the view scroll for *Nightshade* and *Gunfright*. The trick was copied so widely that by 1988 *CRASH* could look back on "unimaginative clones which sometimes threatened to swamp all the review pages with their identical, and by then extremely boring, isometric screens".

## Fast facts

- **Made by:** [Tim and Chris Stamper](/vault/people/stamper-brothers)'s Ashby Computers and Graphics, trading as [Ultimate Play the Game](/vault/companies/ultimate).
- **First game:** *[Knight Lore](/vault/games/knight-lore)* (ZX Spectrum, November 1984).
- **Filmation II:** the scrolling version, first used in *Nightshade* (1985).
- **Other machines:** *Knight Lore* was converted to the Amstrad CPC, BBC Micro and MSX; *Nightshade* reached the BBC Micro and the Commodore 64.
- **Source code:** never published by Ultimate; a full annotated disassembly of the Spectrum *Knight Lore* is public.

## What was new

Isometric views were not new on the Spectrum. *CRASH*'s own history of the form starts with Quicksilva's *[Ant Attack](/vault/games/ant-attack)* (1983), "the first game with truly three-dimensional views", followed by [Vortex](/vault/companies/vortex-software)'s *Android 2* and [Hewson](/vault/companies/hewson-consultants)'s *The Legend of Avalon* in 1984. What *Knight Lore* added was a room full of solid objects that could overlap and be moved about. *CRASH*'s reviewers put it down to "the new technique of masking, so that the moving characters do not flicker at all when they pass in front of other objects", and to a choice about colour: "only one colour is used per screen which avoids any attribute problems", with a different colour for each room.

The same reviewers noticed that being able to hide things was a weapon as well as a spectacle. "Ultimate have used the hidden view idea very cruelly, so that a hint may be given of something nasty crouching behind a stairway — but you're never sure until it's too late." Their box of special features listed simply: "filmation which allows you to do almost anything with the objects in the game".

## How Knight Lore draws a room

Ultimate never explained Filmation, but the Spectrum game has since been taken apart. The annotated disassembly by the programmer known as tcdev, published in 2017, shows how each piece works.

**Every object lives in 3D.** A room holds up to forty objects: Sabreman, the special objects he collects, and the blocks, arches and hazards of the scenery. Each has an x, y and z position and a width, depth and height, which together make a box in the world. Collisions, jumps and falls are worked out with those boxes, not with pictures.

**Projection is two additions and a halving.** The screen position comes from the world position by adding x and y for the distance across, and subtracting them, halving the result and adding the height for the distance down. There is no multiplication and no perspective, which is why the view slopes two pixels across for every pixel down. The [isometric projection](/vault/techniques/isometric-projection) entry explains why games on a pixel grid chose that slope.

**Order comes from comparing pairs.** To decide what to draw first, the game compares objects two at a time, checking whether their boxes overlap along each of the three axes, and uses the combination to decide which of the pair must go behind. This handles long and short objects side by side, which a single sorting number such as the screen height cannot. [Y-depth sorting](/vault/techniques/y-depth-sorting) is the simpler method it avoids.

**Drawing is masked, into a buffer.** Each sprite is stored as pairs of bytes, picture and mask. The mask clears the pixels the object covers and the picture is then combined in, so the scenery behind shows through the gaps without being overwritten. Everything is drawn into a 6,144-byte copy of the Spectrum's bitmap and then copied to the display, so the player never sees a half-drawn frame. When something moves, the game wipes only the area flagged as changed and redraws the objects there, rather than the whole room.

**Tables replace work.** At start-up the game builds lookup tables of bytes shifted by one to seven pixels, for drawing at any horizontal position, and of bytes with their bits reversed, so one picture can face either way. Rooms are described as short lists of scenery pieces and positions: the table describing all of the castle's rooms takes under 2.5K, where a single stored Spectrum screen needs 6.75K.

That last point is the one magazines abroad singled out when they explained the technique to their own readers in 1987. In Brazil, *Micro Sistemas* pointed out that a hi-res screen took about 6K, so a 48K machine could hold "only eight screens" if each were stored as a picture; its answer was "modularidade", building every room from reusable parts. In Spain, *Amstrad Semanal* printed a routine that added two new commands to the Amstrad CPC's BASIC so readers could move masked graphics "con el método Filmation" in their own programs.

Filmation II was the one real development. *CRASH*'s Will Brooker described it in 1988 as "an Ultimate invention" that "allows graphics of Knight Lore's quality to be scrolled smoothly over a large playing area". *Nightshade* traded something for it: there were, the *CRASH* reviewer noted, "no objects which you can shunt about and use whilst playing". On the Commodore 64 the cost was speed. *ZZAP!64* found the conversion "intolerably sluggish" and gave it 54%; the tape carried a colour version and a slightly faster black-and-white one.

By *Bubbler* in 1987 *CRASH* was writing Ultimate's epitaph: "Once Ultimate was on a pedestal above all other software companies... Sadly those glorious times are long since gone." The Stampers had by then moved on to [Rare](/vault/companies/rare) and Nintendo's consoles.

## Followers

Other programmers took the idea further within a year. The Edge's *[Fairlight](/vault/games/fairlight)*, by [Bo Jangeborg](/vault/people/bo-jangeborg), scored 95% in *CRASH* in November 1985, and one reviewer wrote that it "knocks Filmation and Filmation 2 into a cocked hat. And there's a game behind the graphics too". Another put the complaint plainly: "Ultimate first introduced us to realistic 3D graphics but sadly not a lot of game was bolted on to them." [Jon Ritman](/vault/people/jon-ritman) and [Bernie Drummond](/vault/people/bernie-drummond) made *[Batman](/vault/games/batman-1986)* (93%) and then *[Head Over Heels](/vault/games/head-over-heels)* (97%) for [Ocean](/vault/companies/ocean-software), and by then *CRASH* was noting that "there have been quite a few games of this style lately — and pretty as they are, many have been severely lacking in gameplay". By 1988 CRL's *3D Game Maker*, in Brooker's words, enabled "everyone to rewrite Knight Lore".

The idea also reached consoles. Software Creations in Manchester built *Solstice* for the NES, published in America by CSG Imagesoft in 1990. Its designer, Mark Wilson, told *Edge* in 2007 that the programmer Mike Webb "came into work one day with a method for doing a Knightlore-style isometric game on the NES", and that "Solstice would not have existed if not for the examples set by Knight Lore". A Super NES sequel, *Equinox*, was announced by Sony Imagesoft in 1993. Much later, the magazine *Fusion* greeted *Lumo* (2016), a modern isometric puzzle game, as "a clear nod to the 3D isometric games of the 80s such as Head over Heels and Knight Lore".
