**Pucrunch** puts the hard work on the computer preparing a program. [Pasi Ojala](/vault/people/pasi-ojala), also known as Albert, wrote its compressor in portable C and its small-machine decompressor in assembly. A host can spend memory and time searching for savings; a [C64](/vault/systems/commodore-64) need only restore the result.

Ojala’s design page dates the first version to March 1997, with a rewrite that December and a major update in October 1998. He also explained the work in [C=Hacking](/vault/magazines/c-hacking).

## Two kinds of repetition

Pucrunch combines **LZ77-style copies** with [run-length encoding](/vault/techniques/run-length-encoding). A copy refers to an earlier sequence in the output. A run describes several repetitions of one byte. Both save space when their descriptions are shorter than the data they replace.

The distinction matters. A repeated phrase and a long area filled with one value are different opportunities. Ojala explains that a dedicated run representation can describe longer runs than the copy lengths allowed by his LZ encoding. Lengths use variable-length codes, so small and large values need not occupy equal amounts of space.

## The decoder shapes the format

Ojala’s *Compression Basics* explains why a small computer’s needs change the design. The decompressor must be short, use little working storage and avoid arithmetic that is expensive on an 8-bit processor. A format that compresses well on a large machine is not automatically cheap to decode on a small one.

His implementation also allows packed and expanded data to share part of memory, provided unread input remains safe. That is a loading arrangement with constraints, rather than a promise that any two addresses will work.

The same decisions recur in [Exomizer](/vault/tools/exomizer) and [ByteBoozer](/vault/tools/byteboozer). File size is one part of the comparison; decoder bytes, workspace and unpacking time also belong in the budget.
