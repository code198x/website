"Have you ever wondered how games writers get the atmospheric tunes in them written?" *Amiga Computing* asked in June 1990. "Or where all those demo tracks come from? Well, 99.999 per cent of them, and I think I'm being quite conservative here, are written with a utility called SoundTracker, or one of its many derivatives or lookalikes." The program was Karsten Obarski's *Ultimate Soundtracker*, a German music editor for the [Amiga](/vault/systems/commodore-amiga). It was a commercial product, yet most of the people who used it had never bought it. They used versions that crackers had copied, patched and renamed, and those versions, with *NoiseTracker* and *[ProTracker](/vault/tools/protracker)* after them, set the way music was made on the Amiga and gave the [tracker scene](/vault/culture/tracker-music) its file format.

## Fast facts

- **Author:** Karsten Obarski, Germany.
- **First sold:** end of 1987, published by EAS, according to later accounts; promoted in North America by MichTron/MicroDeal in December 1988.
- **Price:** £29.50 by mail order in Britain in 1989.
- **Channels:** four, one for each of [Paula](/vault/hardware/paula)'s sound channels.
- **Instruments:** up to 15 samples per song in the original.
- **Output:** a "module", the song and its samples in one file, the start of the [MOD format](/vault/techniques/mod-format).
- **Descendants:** the hacked Soundtrackers, *NoiseTracker*, *StarTrekker* and *[ProTracker](/vault/tools/protracker)*.

## Obarski and the first version

Obarski was a musician as well as a programmer. In February 1989 the American *Commodore Magazine* quoted George Johnson of the publisher DigiTek: "If you listen to *Amegas*, you hear what we think is excellent sound from a man named Karsten Obarski, who is also working on a music program for us." In later accounts, *Amegas*, a game by a friend, is the reason the tracker exists: Obarski wrote *Soundtracker* to make its music. He also said he had used [Chris Hülsbeck](/vault/people/chris-huelsbeck)'s *Soundmonitor* on the Commodore 64, and public accounts describe its vertical layout as the model for his own.

## How a tracker works

A tracker shows music as a table that scrolls up the screen. Each of the four columns is one channel, and each row is a moment in time. A row in a column can hold three things: a note, the number of the sample to play it with, and an effect. *Amiga Computing* described the layout in 1990: "a musical note (C#, D, and so on), a hex number representing a sample in memory", followed by hexadecimal digits "used for various effects like vibrato and arpeggio". A block of rows is a pattern; a list of pattern numbers, played in order, is the song.

The design follows the hardware. [Paula](/vault/hardware/paula), the Amiga's sound chip, has four channels, and each one plays 8-bit samples from memory at a rate set by a period register. A tracker stores that period value for each note and writes it, with the sample's address, length and volume, straight into the chip's registers as the song plays. Nothing is synthesised, so any recorded sound can become an instrument, and a whole song can be carried in one file with its samples. The [MOD format entry](/vault/techniques/mod-format) sets out the file byte by byte.

The first version was not easy to use, and *Amiga Computing* did not pretend otherwise: "trackers make no concession to the non-programmer", and they were "really assembly hackers' tools". The original *Soundtracker* accepted only raw sample data, not IFF files, and its instruments had to be declared in a separate "Preset List" editor before they could be used. *Amiga Format*'s review of *Games Music Creator* in November 1989 counted that among "the complaints that surrounded the original Sound Tracker". Samples came on disks named ST-01, ST-02 and so on, and trackers went on looking for disks with those names: *CU Amiga* told readers in 1991 that *ProTracker* "automatically detects samples stored on disks named ST-00, ST-01, ST-02 etc."

## Commercial product, pirated tool

*Ultimate Soundtracker* was on sale. MichTron/MicroDeal showed it beside their A.M.A.S. sampler in Toronto in December 1988, and British mail-order firms listed it at around £29.50 through 1989. But most copies in use were not the real one. "In the hands of these generally hateful people," wrote *Amiga Computing*'s public-domain columnist of the crackers, "it has slowly mutated to become something slightly more useful. The name has mutated, too... the list gets longer daily as more crackers insert their names in the code and then claim to have written it." He counted at least 20 variants.

Nobody was sure what was legal. In March 1990 a reader told *Amiga Format* that he had bought the original but had since "got hold of many other hackers' versions that are much better", and asked whether he could give them to friends. The editors replied that however much the hackers claimed to have rewritten the code, "Sound Tracker (in all its incarnations) still remains on dodgy ground. Legally, Sound Tracker is still a commercial product." In November 1990 *Amiga Computing* said the program had "become famous among both crackers and demo writers", that "chances are that even the games you buy contain tunes produced using Sound Tracker", and that because "nobody seems to know whether Sound Tracker is now PD or not" it had become "almost impossible to obtain".

## The descendants

The later versions changed the format as well as the program. One raised the limit from 15 samples to 31 and marked its files with the letters `M.K.`, whose origin is still disputed (see [MOD format](/vault/techniques/mod-format)). Obarski himself released a second version, *The Ultimate Soundtracker 2*, in November 1988, public reference works say. The best-known successor was *NoiseTracker*, written in 1989, those works add, by two Swedish programmers, Pex "Mahoney" Tufvesson and Anders "Kaktus" Berkeman.

By December 1990 *Amiga Format*'s public-domain column found three on one disk: *NoiseTracker*; *StarTrekker*, which turned out to be "Noisetracker V2.12, rewritten by a Norwegian who goes by the alias Exolon of Fairlight"; and "Protracker V1.0, which bears a distinct resemblance to the infamous SoundTracker". The columnist called the lot "a noble continuation of the Tracker saga". *[ProTracker](/vault/tools/protracker)* became the standard, and trackers on the PC later read the same modules.

Obarski did not stay in the scene. In 1993 *Amiga Format* noted that the disk magazine *AM/FM*, edited by the *MED* author Teijo Kinnunen, had interviewed "Karsten Obarski, who wrote the first soundtracker program". In a later interview he said that he now worked as an electronics specialist for an industrial company.
