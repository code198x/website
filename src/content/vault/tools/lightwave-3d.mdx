**LightWave 3D** began as bundled software with NewTek's Video Toaster in 1990 and evolved into one of the industry's most respected 3D packages. Used to create the groundbreaking CGI for **Babylon 5**, it proved that affordable software could produce broadcast and film-quality animation. LightWave demonstrated that the Amiga creative scene could birth professional tools used at the highest levels.

## Fast facts

- **Developer:** [NewTek](/vault/companies/newtek); originally Allen Hastings, under Aegis
- **First release:** 1990 (with [Video Toaster](/vault/hardware/video-toaster)) — but descended from Allen Hastings's Videoscape 3D, shown in 1986 and published by Aegis
- **Standalone:** 1994
- **Famous for:** Babylon 5 CGI
- **Architecture:** Modeller + Layout
- **Status:** Still actively developed

## The Babylon 5 story is about money, not firsts

This entry used to claim Babylon 5 was the "first TV series with all-CGI
effects". Jimmy Maher, whose account of the Amiga is the most careful one
available, declines that framing in as many words:

> Such a use of computer graphics imaging (CGI) in film was not precisely new;
> major Hollywood productions such as *Tron* and *The Last Starfighter* had
> previously made use of such techniques.

What changed was the price of doing it. Foundation Imaging's visual effects
director Ron Thornton made the comparison himself: *The Last Starfighter*'s CGI
had needed **a $15 million Cray X-MP**. Less than a decade later, Foundation
produced "equally impressive effects using a rendering farm of **twelve
Toaster-equipped Amigas** running LightWave 3D".

The budget tells the same story. Babylon 5's pilot — which premiered on
**22 February 1993** — had a total visual effects budget of **$250,000**, which
Thornton put at "about one-fourth the cost of an episode of *Star Trek: The Next
Generation*", a contemporary space show still shooting models.

What Foundation displaced was physical craft. Those shots "had traditionally
been created by filming meticulously crafted and painted model miniatures",
with only lasers, explosions and engine glow added afterwards.

## The show's effects team taught the readers

While Babylon 5 was airing, Foundation Imaging were writing a monthly 3D
tutorial series for *Amiga Shopper* — "The Three Ds of 3D". Issue 45 bills them
plainly as "the people behind the graphics for the TV program Babylon 5",
promising to show readers "how to create 3D images like this"; issue 46 names
the author as "Mojo, the man who works with Ron Thornton on the TV series
Babylon 5".

A working television effects house, publishing its methods month by month in a
consumer computer magazine, for readers with the same software on the same
machine. There is no modern equivalent.

## Legacy

LightWave 3D proved that professional 3D tools didn't require professional prices — and it had a run-up. Hastings had been building Amiga 3D software since 1985, and Videoscape reached NewTek before the Toaster made it famous. Artists trained on LightWave went on to work at major studios. The techniques developed for Babylon 5 influenced television production standards. And it all started bundled with an Amiga expansion card.
