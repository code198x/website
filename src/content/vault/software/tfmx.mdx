**TFMX** was music software made for the job of putting a soundtrack into an Amiga game. [Chris Hülsbeck](/vault/people/chris-huelsbeck) developed the system through his game work; **Demonware** then sold it to other users. A composition editor and the code that played the music belonged to the same working system.

In February 1990, *The Games Machine* connected it with **X-Out**. Later that year, reviews in Britain and the United States examined what happened when a programmer’s own music machinery became a product for other people.

## Control beyond the note list

*Amiga Computing* described pattern-based editing and controls for changing a sound over time, including pitch movement and vibrato. Those details matter because the instrument is not only a recording triggered at a particular pitch. Playback commands can continue shaping it while the music advances.

The editor prepares those decisions. A player then interprets them inside the destination program, sharing the Amiga with graphics, game logic and other sound. That relationship connects TFMX with [sound drivers](/vault/techniques/sound-drivers), while giving the composer a dedicated environment in which to prepare the data.

## Selling the working system

The British review noted an integration problem: the retail package supplied a separate player rather than replay source for the user to adapt. The distinction affected how a programmer could call the music from a game. (*Amiga Computing*, November 1990, pp. 120–121.)

Dean Friedman’s *AmigaWorld* review was considerably harsher about the product’s reliability, reporting crashes and a replacement copy. Those are a contemporary reviewer’s experiences with the reviewed release, not proof that every later TFMX player behaved identically.

## More voices came through further work

Hülsbeck’s later account of **Turrican II** describes collaboration with **Jochen Hippel** on mixing additional voices into one hardware channel. That seven-voice system should not be read as seven physical Paula channels, or as a feature automatically present in every earlier commercial edition.

TFMX’s history joins composition, playback engineering and distribution. A musician’s productive in-house system still needed documentation, reliable tools and an integration route before somebody else could use it with the same confidence.
