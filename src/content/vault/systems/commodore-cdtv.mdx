The **Commodore CDTV** put an [Amiga](/vault/systems/commodore-amiga)-derived computer into a case that could sit beside a television and audio equipment. A CD drive and infrared remote supplied the main way in. The standard experience did not begin with a keyboard, floppy disk and desktop commands.

Commodore's May 1991 service manual documents the 68000-based machine and its 1MB of RAM. Doug Barney's June 1991 *AmigaWorld* report independently described the same core arrangement as the company began rolling the system out in selected markets. He also stated that his review machine was a late prototype, with beta software and an operating-system update arriving during the review. His account records that stage of the product, rather than every detail of every shipped revision. ([Service manual](https://amiga.net.au/files/Tech_Amiga/Commodore_CDTV_Servicemanual_%28pn-400403-01%29%281991-05%29_ocr.pdf); [contemporary report, from p.21](https://amigaland.com/dataz/press_magazine/Amiga_World/PDF_TXT/Amiga_World_Vol_07_06_1991_Jun.pdf).)

## A disc is storage, not working memory

[CD-ROM](/vault/hardware/cd-rom) allowed software to carry substantially more material than an ordinary floppy disk: pictures, recorded sound and program data could share the disc. Programs still had to load and use that material within the machine's working memory and display facilities.

The CDTV retained the earlier Amiga hardware generation. Disc capacity did not supply the later AGA chipset or the [Amiga CD32](/vault/systems/amiga-cd32)'s processor. Both systems used compact discs, but they were different computers behind the drive.

## The remote shaped the software

Barney described the handheld remote's cursor controls as less precise than a mouse for intricate menus or drawing. That is a practical interface constraint: software intended for a television and remote needed choices that could be selected comfortably from a seat across the room.

The front panel also provided familiar CD-player controls. Audio-CD playback and computer-driven CD-ROM applications were related uses of the drive, rather than the same software format. An ordinary music disc did not become an interactive title by being inserted into CDTV.

## Computer connections remained

The case concealed a computer but did not eliminate its expansion possibilities. The service guide describes connections for equipment including a keyboard and external floppy drive. Those peripherals were options, not evidence that every buyer received a complete desktop Amiga setup.

The [Commodore](/vault/companies/commodore) entry places CDTV beside the company's other machines. Its distinctive subject here is the attempt to make disc-based Amiga software work through living-room controls, and the hardware and interface limits that followed from that choice.
