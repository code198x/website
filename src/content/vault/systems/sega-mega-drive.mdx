Sega's first 16-bit console sold out in Japan within two days. Tony Takoushi, writing in *ACE* in February 1989, reported about 50,000 machines gone at 21,000 yen each. His verdict on the import was "IT IS THE BUSINESS!" A year later the Mega Drive was in America as the Genesis, at about $200 with *Altered Beast* in the box, and in autumn 1990 Virgin Mastertronic brought it to Britain at £189.99.

What made it matter was the pairing inside: the 68000 that ran the Amiga and the Atari ST, beside the Z80 from Sega's own [Master System](/vault/systems/sega-master-system), with a Yamaha FM chip related to the ones in Sega's arcade boards. What made it famous was the fight with Nintendo that followed, and the slogans Sega wrote for it. Sega's own developer manual is more careful than the advertising. The numbers the machine is remembered by (80 sprites, 64 colours, 7.67 MHz) are each the better half of a pair.

## Fast facts
- **Maker:** [Sega](/vault/companies/sega).
- **Released:** 29 October 1988 (Japan); 1989 as the Genesis (North America); autumn 1990 (UK).
- **Launch prices:** 21,000 yen; about $200; £189.99.
- **CPU:** [Motorola 68000](/vault/hardware/68000), **7.67 MHz on NTSC machines and 7.60 MHz on PAL**, which the console reports about itself in a register.
- **Sound CPU:** [Zilog Z80](/vault/hardware/z80), given by Sega as 4 MHz, with 8K of its own RAM.
- **Memory:** 64K of main RAM; 64K of video RAM; up to 1 MB (8 Mbit) of cartridge ROM in Sega's overview.
- **Video:** Sega's VDP 315-5313: two scrolling playfields and a sprite plane, 320×224 or 256×224.
- **Colours:** 64 colour registers from a palette of 512, of which 60 can be drawn.
- **Sprites:** 80 on screen and 20 per line in the 40-cell mode; 64 and 16 in the 32-cell mode.
- **Sound:** [Yamaha YM2612](/vault/hardware/ym2612) FM synthesis plus a Texas Instruments 76489 PSG.

## The numbers, as Sega documented them

**Sprites depend on the mode, and the real limit is dots.** The *Genesis Software Manual* tabulates sprite capacity against horizontal resolution. In the 40-cell mode (320 pixels across) the VDP allows 80 sprites, 20 on a line and 320 dots on a line. In the 32-cell mode (256 pixels) it allows 64, 16 and 256. The familiar "80 and 20" belongs to the wide mode only.

The manual's worked examples show that the count is rarely what runs out first. The per-line budget is 320 *dots*, so sprites four cells (32 pixels) wide use it up at ten, not twenty: "10 Sprites in the H40 cell mode", in the manual's words. When the budget runs out partway through a sprite, the VDP does not drop it. It cuts it short, and one example ends with a sprite displayed "only for 16 dots". Programmers who kept their sprites narrow could fit more of them on a line.

**Sixty-four colours, four of them invisible.** Colour RAM holds "64 9-bit wide color registers", three bits each of red, green and blue, arranged as "4 palettes of 16 colors" from 512 possible. But the "0th color (of each palette) is always transparent". Four of the 64 registers therefore never show, and 60 remain to draw with. The quoted 64 is the size of the hardware table, not the number of colours on screen.

**The clock is a pair, and the machine knows which.** The version register at `$A10001` carries a `VMOD` bit: "0: NTSC CPU clock 7.67 MHz / 1: PAL CPU clock 7.60 MHz", next to a `Mode` bit reading "0: Domestic Model / 1: Overseas Model". A program can ask the console which territory it was built for and how fast it runs. Sega's own overview page rounds the figure to "68000 @8mHz".

## DMA, and who gets the bus

The VDP can copy data into video memory by itself, with direct memory access (DMA), and Sega is explicit about the cost:

> The 68K is stopped during memory to VRAM/CRAM/VSRAM DMA, but the Z80
> continues to run as long as it does not attempt access to the 68K memory space.

The main processor waits while the transfer runs. The Z80, which drives the sound chips, keeps going as long as it stays off the 68000's side of the machine. The manual does not draw the consequence, but it follows: music and sound effects can carry on through a large transfer that freezes the 68000.

The speed of that transfer depends on when it happens:

> The DMA is quite fast during VBLANK (about double the tightest possible 68K
> loop speed), but during active scan the speed is the same as a 68K loop.

Sega's overview puts a figure on the fast case: 36 scanlines of vertical blanking, and 7380 bytes moved within them. While the picture is being drawn, DMA is no quicker than a copy loop the programmer writes, so Mega Drive games plan their graphics updates around the gap between frames.

## The sound chip's arcade parentage

The manual's sound section introduces the YM2612 by naming its relative:

> The Yamaha 2612 Frequency Modulation (FM) sound synthesis IC resembles the
> Yamaha 2151 (used in SEGA's coin-operated machines) and the chips used in
> Yamaha's synthesizers.

It offers six FM channels, each built from "four dedicated channel components called 'operators'", with one LFO (low-frequency oscillator), two timers and stereo output. The Texas Instruments PSG, the same kind of simple tone generator the Master System used, adds square-wave tones and noise alongside.

One line in the list of capabilities matters to every composer on the machine: the 8-bit digitised audio channel exists "as replacement for one of the FM channels". Sampled sound is not an extra voice but a trade. A composer who wanted a sampled drum or a voice gave up one of the six FM channels for it and wrote the rest of the music for five.

## Marketing louder than the manual

In autumn 1990 Sega of America announced a "national advertising blitz" in its customer magazine, *Sega Visions*, under the line "Genesis Does What Nintendon't", built around games it said Nintendo's console could not offer. *Sonic the Hedgehog* followed in 1991, and a *Sega Visions* reader writing in 1993 listed what had changed since he bought his Genesis in autumn 1990: Sega had "lowered the price of the system by $70, packed in Sonic the Hedgehog, and now are giving away Sonic 2".

The best-known slogan, "[Blast Processing](/vault/techniques/blast-processing)", is also the hardest to pin down. It appears nowhere in Sega's *Genesis Software Manual* or its technical bulletins, and nothing in the developer documentation corresponds to it. In June 1993 *Sega Visions* told a reader that it was real, and that it "allows the Genesis to display one image while loading another into memory, something the SNES cannot do... without it, Sonic would move about as fast as Mario." The same month *Nintendo Power* reported that Sega of America's consumer service and PR firm "didn't have a well-defined answer" when asked what it was, and concluded: "In essence, Blast Processing means that Sega's programmers now know what they're doing."

## In Britain

The Mega Drive reached Britain through Virgin [Mastertronic](/vault/companies/mastertronic), which already distributed the Master System. *ACE* reported in August 1990 that the console would cost £189.99 with *Altered Beast*, with games at £29.99 to £39.99 and 20 titles at launch. By October *Computer and Video Games* was writing about "Virgin's official UK Megadrive" as "out and about".

It was not an instant success. *Edge*'s 1996 hardware guide judged that its first British Christmas "wasn't exactly what you would call successful", and that sales took off only in 1992, when the Super Nintendo arrived and the rivalry between the two machines filled shop shelves and television screens. By *Edge*'s count Sega had shipped over a million Mega Drives in the UK by the end of that year. The Mega-CD add-on followed in April 1993 at £270.
