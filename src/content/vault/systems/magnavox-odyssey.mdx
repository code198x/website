The Magnavox Odyssey was the first video game console sold for the home. Magnavox showed it to its dealers in May 1972, months before *[Pong](/vault/games/pong)* appeared in a bar, and for $100 it turned an ordinary television into a table tennis table, a hockey rink or a haunted house. It could draw only a few white squares and a line. Everything else came from sheets of coloured plastic that the players stuck to the screen, and from the playing cards, dice and play money in the box.

Inside was the Brown Box, the prototype that [Ralph Baer](/vault/people/ralph-baer) and his team had built at Sanders Associates in New Hampshire, with few changes. Magnavox kept making it until 1975 and built somewhere between 330,000 and 350,000.

## Fast facts

- **Introduced:** May 1972, to Magnavox dealers and the press; in the shops later that year.
- **Maker:** Magnavox, under licence from Sanders Associates. Model 1TL200.
- **Price:** $100 ($99.95), with six game cards. The rifle cost another $25.
- **Electronics:** discrete transistors and diodes on plug-in circuit boards; no integrated circuits.
- **Picture:** two player spots, a ball spot and a centre line, with no colour, no score and no sound.
- **Games:** twelve, chosen with six plug-in cards and played with screen overlays.
- **Controllers:** two, each with horizontal, vertical and "English" knobs.
- **Power:** six C cells giving 9 volts; a mains adaptor was sold separately.
- **Produced:** 1972 to 1975.

## From the Brown Box to Skill-O-Vision

Sanders Associates was a defence electronics company, and it looked to television makers to manufacture the Brown Box. In January 1971, after demonstrating it to several of them, it signed a preliminary agreement with Magnavox and handed over the prototype and its design data to Magnavox's engineers in Fort Wayne, Indiana. They copied almost all of its circuits; Baer later wrote that the final Odyssey design "was not much different from what we had drawn up for the Brown Box".

Two changes mattered. To "save some money", Magnavox left out the colour circuitry that had let the Brown Box fill the screen with coloured backgrounds. It also replaced the Brown Box's sixteen game-selection switches with plug-in cards, which Baer called "a stroke of genius".

During development Magnavox called the product Skill-O-Vision. It tried prototypes on the public at its own stores in San Diego in July 1971 and in Grand Rapids, Michigan, in October, handing out questionnaires that asked, among other things, whether people preferred plug-in cards or switches. The response was good enough for Magnavox to plan a launch for May 1972. Early that year the product took the name Odyssey.

## Spots, cards and overlays

The Odyssey had no integrated circuits because, Baer told *Video Games*, ICs "back then were so damn expensive". Inside, in his words, "was basically 40 transistors and a pile of diodes". They sat on one large circuit board with small "baby" boards plugged into it, each holding one part of the design, such as the generators for the spots or for the sync signals that let a television lock on to the picture. Baer rejected the common description of the Odyssey as analogue: the circuitry, he told *Retro Gamer*, was "nearly all digital and pulse circuitry".

All the console could show were square spots of light: one for each player, a third that the machine moved itself as a ball, and a vertical line for a net or a wall. Each player had a wired controller with three knobs. One moved the player's spot across the screen and one moved it up and down. The third, the "English" control, bent the ball's flight after it was hit. Two more knobs on the console set the ball's speed and the position of the centre line.

There was no score on the screen and no sound. With so few parts, Baer said, "you couldn't have anything more complex than a line down the middle to indicate the net, and paddles and balls". Generating complex backgrounds "was just out of the question pricewise", and "scoring was also too damn expensive". Translucent coloured overlays, held on the television screen, supplied the rest: a tennis court, a hockey rink, a map of the United States. The electronics did not know the overlay was there. In *Cat and Mouse* the maze existed only on the plastic, and *Retro Gamer* noted that players were on their honour to keep inside its walls. *[Electronic Games](/vault/magazines/electronic-games)* credited a Magnavox employee, Steve Laner, with designing the overlays.

The six cards plugged into a slot in the front of the console. They held no program and no memory. Each was a set of jumpers that connected the circuits already in the console in a different way, so the same spots behaved as a tennis ball, a skier or a ghost. Baer called them "the elementary forerunner of ROM": "It's the same damn thing but only in a different form." A later [cartridge](/vault/hardware/cartridge) carried the game itself; an Odyssey card only chose among games the console could already play.

The Odyssey connected to the television's aerial terminals. To keep the radio signal it fed the set within new FCC limits, Magnavox's engineers added a switch box with "Game" and "TV" positions. Baer wrote that millions of similar switch boxes were copied during the 1970s by game makers "all across the globe". The console ran on six C cells, which together gave 9 volts, and an AC adaptor was sold separately.

## The games

Magnavox's flier listed twelve games: *Table Tennis*, *Tennis*, *Hockey*, *Football*, *Ski*, *Submarine*, *Cat and Mouse*, *Haunted House*, *Analogic*, *Roulette*, *States* and *Simon Says*. Six cards played all twelve, because one card could set up the spots for several games and the overlay and the rules did the rest. Some were sports, some chases, and some only partly video games: *Roulette* came with chips, play money and a layout board, *States* was a geography quiz with study cards and a map, and *Simon Says* was a learning game for young children that matched parts of the body to printed words.

*Haunted House* was a game for two. One player moved a detective through a house drawn on the overlay, looking for clues on the clue cards, while the other played a ghost that stayed hidden until the right moment. The [horror games](/vault/genres/horror-games) entry counts it as the first horror game.

The console had an accessory port, and only one accessory was ever released for it: a pump-action plastic rifle, sold separately for $25, for the Shooting Gallery games. Its sensor saw the light from the screen. The player fired at a square of light bouncing across a target overlay. It was the first [light gun](/vault/hardware/light-gun) made for a home console.

Magnavox also sold add-on game packs, each with an overlay, instructions and sometimes a new card. David Winter counts ten extra games, six released in 1972 and four in 1973. Baer said the packs barely sold. At $5.95 each, he told *Video Games*, "they sat under the counters of the retailers and got the attention of the bubonic plague", and in his book he blamed Magnavox for never training its sales staff to push them.

## Launch and sales

Magnavox introduced the Odyssey on its "Profit Caravan", a travelling show of its 1972 product line for dealers and the press, which began in Phoenix on 3 May 1972. Baer was in the audience at the New York presentation on 22 May and thought the Odyssey "the undisputed hit of the show". On 24 May, at the Airport Marina in Burlingame, California, [Nolan Bushnell](/vault/people/nolan-bushnell) played its ping-pong game. Winter's history puts the first dealer and press shows in April.

When the console reached the shops is less certain. Baer wrote that Magnavox supplied dealers with production units "over the following months". *Retro Gamer* and Winter give August 1972 for the release or the start of production, while *Video Games* reported in 1984 that Magnavox began producing it on 27 January 1972.

Baer thought Magnavox made it a hard sell. The price was "a steep $100" for the console and six cards, and "another whopping $25" for the rifle. In both a 1983 interview and his book he said Magnavox restricted its sales to its own franchised dealers, and that its autumn television advertising left people believing the Odyssey worked only with Magnavox sets, "manifest nonsense" in his words. He credited a commercial featuring Frank Sinatra with lifting sales that autumn.

Close to 100,000 Odysseys were sold in 1972, by Baer's count. Production that year was higher: 140,100 according to Baer, over 130,000 according to Winter and 130,000 according to *Retro Gamer*. Having made more than it sold, Baer said, Magnavox hardly promoted the Odyssey in 1973, and the price fell to $79. Baer dates the cut to early 1973 and Winter to late 1973, when Winter says a buyer of a new Magnavox television could have the console for $49.95. Sales picked up in 1974 with heavy promotion, which by Baer's account moved 150,000 units. Production of the original model ran until 1975 and totalled about 350,000 by Baer's figure, or over 330,000 by Winter's and *Retro Gamer*'s.

Baer did not think it made Magnavox much money. He estimated factory costs of about $40 to $50 a console, roughly $17 million in total costs including tooling, testing and advertising, about 40,000 consoles returned, and receipts of around $20 million.

## Abroad

The Odyssey also sold outside the United States. According to Winter, the first export was the Magnavox Odisea, sold in Mexico from about October 1972 with Spanish paperwork, and fewer than 4,000 were made. In Germany it was sold as the ITT Schaub-Lorenz "Odyssee", with its own German manuals; it was announced in late 1973 and sold in small numbers from early 1974. Winter also records a Portuguese-language Odyssey sold in Brazil from late 1975 by Planil of Rio de Janeiro, under licence.

Baer listed twelve countries that took the export model: Australia, Belgium, England, France, Germany, Greece, Israel, Italy, Spain, Switzerland, the USSR and Venezuela. It dropped *Cat and Mouse*, *Football*, *Haunted House*, *Roulette* and *States*, and added *Soccer*, *Volleyball* and *Wipe Out*, which had been add-on games in the United States. *Soccer* was *Football* with new rules.

Look-alikes followed in Europe. Sweden's Kanal 34 bore little resemblance to the Odyssey but, Baer wrote, used the same circuits inside; Winter dates it to 1975. Baer considered Britain's Videomaster Home T.V. Game, released early in 1974, the first original design to follow the Odyssey.

## Pong, and the end

Bushnell went on to found [Atari](/vault/companies/atari), whose arcade *Pong* of autumn 1972 was, in Baer's words, "a derivative of Odyssey". The [Pong](/vault/games/pong) entry tells that story and the patent cases that followed.

In 1975 Magnavox replaced the original with the Odyssey 100 and Odyssey 200, dedicated ball-and-paddle consoles built on Texas Instruments chips, while Atari sold its own home *Pong* through Sears. Magnavox now belonged to Philips, which sold the Odyssey 200 in Britain in 1976 as the Philips Odyssey 200. The name passed in 1978 to the Odyssey², sold in Europe as the Philips Videopac G7000. *Video Games* in 1983 and 1984 called the 1972 machine the "Odyssey 100", a name that belongs to its successor.
