The **Sinclair QL** — Quantum Leap — was announced in **January 1984** at
**£399**, and was the **first home computer built around a 68000-family
processor**: a Motorola **68008** at 7.5 MHz, beating Apple's Macintosh to
announcement by days.

The specification was, for the money, close to unreasonable. *Your Computer*
listed it in February 1984 with something like disbelief: **128K of RAM**, a
32-bit processor, **two built-in Microdrives**, and "all the interfaces —
cartridge, RS-232, joystick and RGB — that Sinclair usually leaves out, for just
£399. Even the keyboard is almost of typewriter quality and there are both reset
and power on/off buttons."

It also came with **four Psion applications** in the box, written for it: **Quill**
(word processor), **Abacus** (planning and financial modelling), **Archive**
(filing, with a card index and an electronic cheque stub) and **Easel** (colour
charts), each with a help screen.

Almost none of it worked properly for the first six months, and the failure took
[Sinclair Research](/vault/companies/sinclair-research) with it.

## Fast facts

- **Announced:** January 1984 · **Price:** £399
- **CPU:** [Motorola](/vault/companies/motorola) **68008** at 7.5 MHz — 32-bit internally, **8-bit data bus**
- **Second processor:** Intel **8049**, handling the keyboard, sound and RS-232
- **Memory:** 128K RAM (about 90K free for programs), **48K ROM** — 32K planned,
  plus a 16K that would not fit
- **Storage:** two built-in 100K **Microdrives**, ~16K/sec transfer, average
  access around 3.5 seconds
- **Operating system:** **QDOS**, multitasking · **Language:** SuperBASIC
- **Bundled:** Psion Quill, Abacus, Archive and Easel

## "Quite Late"

The joke was being made **before a single machine shipped**. *Your Computer*, in
its February 1984 launch coverage: "even customers used to waiting 28 days and
more for Sinclair products are wondering if it really stands for **Quite Late**."

Twenty-eight days is not an arbitrary number. It is the mail-order delivery
threshold, and Sinclair Research sold by mail order — which means it had the
customer's money long before the customer had a machine.

By **May 1984**, on *Sinclair User*'s figures, the company was holding **£5 million
against 13,000 orders, of which only a few hundred had been delivered**. Machines
began arriving around June, roughly four months after announcement.

Sinclair's compensation was an **RS-232 lead**, normally £14.95, sent free with
every QL that took more than 28 days — and a £14.95 refund to anyone who had
already paid for one. As *Your Computer* pointed out in June 1984, the delay meant
Sinclair had also had the interest on the money.

## The blob

Then there is the thing on the back of the computer.

*Your Computer*, June 1984, on the first several thousand machines:

> The [operating system] does not fit within its natty dark-grey case. "We can't
> get all of the operating system in," a spokesman confided. So sticking out of
> the ROM socket at the back of the QL is a blob of metal, slightly larger than a
> matchbox, which contains the bits of the computer which Sinclair could not
> squeeze into the case.

*Sinclair User* gave the engineering reason the same month: **the code would not
fit into the planned 32K, so an extra 16K ROM was needed**, and until customers
returned their machines for an on-board upgrade the extra ROM lived in an EPROM in
the external socket — which meant **the expansion socket could not be used at
all**.

*Your Computer* is unambiguous about why it shipped that way:

> Sinclair felt it was more important to quiet the clamour over protracted
> delivery delays — even if it meant sending out cobbled together machines — than
> it was to delay the whole thing until they got it right.

And *Sinclair User*, in the same June 1984 news item, records the exchange that
tells you how much else was still moving. The QL, it wrote, "bears little
resemblance to the machine launched in January": the battery clock had been
dropped from every model, SuperBASIC had gained turtle graphics and changed
enough that the manuals then at the printer were already wrong. **Asked whether
the machine shown to journalists would be the same in six months, a Sinclair
spokesman declined to comment.**

## FB and AH

Owners learned to identify their machine by typing `PRINT ver$`. The first
production ROM set was **FB**; the revision was **AH**.

*Your Computer*'s July 1984 re-review is the fullest surviving list of what FB was
like to live with:

> The QL as released initially was not really suitable for business or pleasure as
> it had far too many faults and kept crashing.

The catalogue: slow; an unfriendly editor; **Microdrives prone to lose files and
data**; no documentation other than for the Psion packages; a network that would
not integrate Spectrums; bugs in the RS-232 interface; Microdrive files in a
well-used cartridge taking an age to load; a clattery keyboard with a sticking
Enter key.

AH fixed some of it. The magazine's verdict on the fix was measured and damning:
it "does not present a cure for all the QL's troubles and cannot make any
difference to the hardware faults". By September the Microdrive data loss was
gone, which confirmed it had been a software fault in the old ROM all along — but
the cartridges still cost £5 apiece, which *Your Computer*'s columnist called
"daylight robbery", and access was still slow.

## What it was like to use

The specification and the experience were different machines, and the gap is best
shown by the multitasking.

QDOS multitasked. Printing did not. From the July 1984 conclusions: "**despite the
fact that it is a multitasking machine the QL does not provide spooling of
printing with input operations**". Printing a document tied up the machine for
over six minutes at 1200 baud, and then returned you to the top of the file.
Saving a 750-word document through Quill took **three minutes and eighteen
seconds**. Answer one ambiguously-worded save prompt wrongly and the file was
discarded, leaving a blank page.

The same review's other conclusions: the QL is "comparatively slow other than when
undertaking mathematical calculations", and **program loading via Microdrive
appeared to be slower than on the Spectrum** — the eight-bit machine costing a
third as much.

## The case for the defence

The same page of conclusions ends with a sentence that has to sit beside all of
the above:

> In hardware terms the QL currently represents the ultimate in technical
> achievement in computers priced at £400.

That was true, and it is why the disappointment was so sharp. A 68008 with 128K,
a real operating system and four serious applications, for the price of a BBC
Micro, was not a machine anyone else was offering.

*Your Computer*'s letters page in September 1984 carried the best contemporary
defence, under the heading **"Quelling the QL bashers"**. The writer's argument is
still a good one: **QL machine code ran two to three times faster than Spectrum
code**, and judging the machine on BASIC benchmarks was "as meaningless as
comparing cars by their performance in first gear". His plea was for "less
cataloguing of bugs and more hard connector and interface layouts, interrupts, and
documentation" — a request that dates the whole argument precisely, and one this
project has some sympathy with.

He was less kind about the bundle: "the Psion software should be seen for what it
is, a plastic toy in the cornflake packet."

## The end, and the refusal to end

Amstrad bought Sinclair's computer business on 7 April 1986 and **announced within
weeks that it was not interested in the QL**. It tried to raise a quarter of a
million pounds by selling the remaining **19,000 machines** and throwing in the
manufacturing rights free. Interested parties included Timex and the QL user
group *Quanta*; none of them found the cash.

The machine carried on anyway.

- **CST's Thor** — a QL repackaged with 640K, disk drives and an AT-style
  keyboard, £600 plus VAT for a single-drive model up to £1,400 with a 20MB hard
  disk, with an £80 trade-in on your own QL. *CRASH* saw it at the Microfair in
  June 1986 and noted that "last month's tales of the QL's death were rather
  premature". It was still in production in 1988.
- The **Sandy Futura**, another clone, which was not.
- **Blackberry**, a 512K super-QL designed by "Sinclair escapees **Tony Tebby**
  and **Jan Jones**" — respectively the author of QDOS and the author of
  SuperBASIC. Its prototype failed to arrive at the 1986 Microfair, the
  non-arrival blamed on a power cut during development.

Tebby kept going for years: QPTR, a windowing package that most QDOS software
came to depend on, and later **SMS/Q**, a QDOS successor running on Atari, PC and
expanded QL hardware.

*Your Sinclair* summarised it in 1986 as "the continuing story of the computer
that mostly (completely?) refused to die".

## Why it matters here

Two reasons, and neither is the hardware.

**The QL is the clearest case in British computing of a launch date destroying a
machine.** Nothing in the specification was wrong. What was wrong was announcing
in January to beat the Macintosh, taking money in February for machines that did
not exist, and shipping in June with the operating system in a box on the back
because delivery pressure outranked correctness. Every fault the reviewers listed
was fixable and most were fixed — after the reputation had gone.

**And its software lineage outlived it.** HiSoft BASIC and Devpac — the assembler
a generation of Atari ST and Amiga programmers learned on — started life on QDOS.
The QL failed; the tools written for it went on to teach 68000 assembly to
everybody else.
