NAOMI was Sega's arcade version of the [Dreamcast](/vault/systems/sega-dreamcast), and it was built to be cheap. Sega's Model 3 board had become too expensive for many arcade operators, and NAOMI cost half as much. It used the Dreamcast's processor and graphics chip with twice the memory, and its games came on plug-in ROM boards, so an operator could change the game without changing the machine. That gave Sega's arcade games a short route to its home console, and it drew in rival arcade companies too: Capcom and SNK both put their own hardware plans aside to make games for it. The name is usually expanded as "New Arcade Operation Machine Idea".

## Fast facts

- **Manufacturer:** [Sega](/vault/companies/sega).
- **Unveiled:** JAMMA show, Tokyo, 17 September 1998.
- **CPU:** Hitachi SH-4 at 200 MHz, as in the Dreamcast.
- **Graphics:** PowerVR Second Generation, as in the Dreamcast.
- **Memory:** 32 MB main and 16 MB video, twice the Dreamcast's; sound memory four times the Dreamcast's.
- **Media:** ROM cartridge boards; GD-ROM discs were added later.
- **First game:** *[The House of the Dead](/vault/games/house-of-the-dead) 2*.
- **Successor:** NAOMI 2, shown in September 2000.

## A Dreamcast with more memory

Sega's president, Shoichiro Irimajiri, told *Edge* when the Dreamcast was announced in May 1998 that "the Naomi arcade system is related to Dreamcast, but will be more powerful." At the JAMMA amusement show that September, the board appeared with double the main memory and twice the video memory of the console. Rikiya Nakagawa, general manager of Sega's AM1 arcade division, explained the rest to *Edge*. The differences from the Dreamcast were "the ROM cartridge and the CD-ROM", and "the sound memory is four times that of Dreamcast". Otherwise it was the Dreamcast's design, as the ST-V arcade board had been the Saturn's.

Nakagawa was clear about why. Sega wanted "an arcade platform on which thirdparties could easily develop different types of games", one that let operators "change games without having to change the hardware". It cost half as much as a Model 3 board. It was not a straight replacement: Model 3 drew a million polygons whatever the effects, while on NAOMI "the number of polygons displayed is dependent on the effects you use". Converting games to the Dreamcast was the other advantage. Once Sega's libraries were finished, he said, "we'll be able to make conversions almost instantly."

## More boards, more power

NAOMI's graphics chip divided the screen into tiles, and that suggested a way to make it faster: give each of several chips part of the screen. When the Dreamcast was announced, *Edge* reported from an unnamed source that Sega's engineers could run "either two, four or sixteen PVR2 boards" in parallel, each handling "its half, quarter or sixteenth of the display", and that sixteen boards could manage 15–20 million polygons a second. At the JAMMA launch, the multiple-chip system "was nowhere to be seen — yet".

It arrived with a racing game. In early 1999 Yu Suzuki's *F355 Challenge* was announced on four NAOMI boards driving three monitors. VideoLogic's Trevor Wing told *Edge*: "We always said that our hardware is scaleable and it certainly doesn't stop with what Naomi can do." A Sega spokesperson admitted to being aware of the possibility of combining "two, four, eight or 16 boards". Four boards made *F355 Challenge* expensive to build, *Edge* later noted, and the next design took a different approach.

## NAOMI 2

Sega showed NAOMI 2 at the JAMMA show in September 2000. In July, *Edge* had reported the new graphics technology behind it: the PowerVR design with a geometry processor added to handle transform and lighting, taking that work away from the CPU, and able to drive several rendering chips from one geometry processor. *Edge* credited NAOMI 2 with ten million polygons a second, 32 MB of video memory, and games on ROM boards or GD-ROM. It also noticed the cost of the extra power: the new board "may rule out the option of easy ports for the Dreamcast". Four months later, in January 2001, Sega announced that it would stop making the Dreamcast.

## The games

*The House of the Dead 2*, from AM1, was the first NAOMI game. Sega's studios followed with *[Crazy Taxi](/vault/games/crazy-taxi)*, *Virtua Tennis*, *Zombie Revenge* and *F355 Challenge*, and *Crazy Taxi*, *Virtua Tennis* and *F355 Challenge* all came to the Dreamcast. Other companies made NAOMI games too: [Tecmo](/vault/companies/tecmo)'s *[Dead or Alive](/vault/games/dead-or-alive) 2*, and [Capcom](/vault/companies/capcom)'s *Marvel vs. Capcom 2*. At the September 2000 JAMMA show Capcom had *Rival Schools 2*, *1944*, *Giga Wing 2* and *Gun Spike* running on NAOMI, though *Edge* found that "nothing really shone".

## Later years

The board outlived the console it was based on. In March 2004 *Edge* found NAOMI still proving "an economic way of powering" new arcade games. By then it had a Dreamcast-based rival, Sammy's Atomiswave, and at the AOU show in early 2005 *Edge* wrote that "the Naomi era seems to be finally drawing to a close". That July Sega unveiled Lindbergh, the board that replaced it, and *Edge* reported the debut as "confirmation of Sega's decision to discontinue manufacture" of NAOMI, while Sega still made room at its show for new NAOMI games such as G.rev's shooter *Under Defeat*. A planned NAOMI 3 was abandoned: Sega explained that it had "simply fallen behind advances in PC technology".
