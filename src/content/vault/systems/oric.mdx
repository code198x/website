The Oric-1 was a British home computer built to beat the [ZX Spectrum](/vault/systems/sinclair-zx-spectrum) at its own price. Announced in late 1982 and launched in January 1983, it offered colour, three-channel sound, moving keys and a printer port for about the same money as a Spectrum. It sold well in its first year, but it reached the shops late, lost the price war that followed, and its maker went into receivership in February 1985. In France the story ran differently. Oric had its strongest market there, and a French company bought what was left of it.

The family has two main machines: the Oric-1 of 1983 and the Oric Atmos of 1984, which put the same circuit board in a new case.

## Fast facts

- **Introduced:** January 1983 (Oric-1); January 1984 (Atmos)
- **Designer:** Tangerine Computer Systems, for Oric Products International
- **Price at launch:** 48K £169.95; 16K planned at £99, expected in shops by July 1983 at £129.95
- **CPU:** [6502](/vault/hardware/6502); the Atmos review gives a 6502A at 1MHz
- **Memory:** 16K or 48K RAM; 16K ROM with an extended [Microsoft BASIC](/vault/languages/microsoft-basic)
- **Display:** 40 × 28 text; 240 × 200 HIRES; eight colours set by serial attributes
- **Sound:** General Instrument [AY-3-8912](/vault/hardware/ay-3-8912), three channels
- **Input and output:** a [6522 VIA](/vault/hardware/mos-6522-via) for keyboard, tape and printer
- **Ports:** Centronics printer, RGB monitor and television as standard
- **Tape:** 2,400 baud, or 300 baud for safety

## Tangerine and British Car Auctions

The Oric came from Tangerine Computer Systems, a British maker of 6502 computers. *Retro Gamer* records that Dr Paul Johnson and Barry Muncaster founded Tangerine in October 1979, and that its earlier products included the Microtan 65 kit. In January 1985 *Your Computer* said that Johnson and Muncaster had both been "heavily involved in Tangerine" and now ran Oric.

The money came from British Car Auctions. *Personal Computer Games* described the Oric as "Funded by British Car Auctions and using the considerable experience of Tangerine Computers". Tangerine did the design, and a new company, Oric Products International, sold the machine.

## Announcement and launch

*Computer & Video Games* broke the news in November 1982. The "Oric 1", designed by Tangerine and to be launched by Oric Products, would come in 16K and 48K versions. The 16K model was due in October at £99, and it was plainly aimed at the Spectrum. *Your Computer* later called it "Spectrum-bashing".

The launch came in January 1983. That month *Your Computer* reported that Orics were "streaming off the production line to start meeting the 250,000 orders already placed for the £99 colour micro". *Retro Gamer* later gave a much smaller figure, over 30,000 pre-orders. The two numbers come from different sources at different times, and neither can be checked against sales records.

The first machines were not finished. "The first few thousand do not have the final ROM chip but an EPROM instead," *Your Computer* reported. Oric's managing director, John Tullis, said that "users will not be able to tell the difference". The review machine sent to *Personal Computer Games* had two EPROMs in place of the ROM. The reviewer thought the Oric was "extremely good value for money", but called its arrival a fresh case of "the almost comical inability of British micro manufacturers to launch a new machine properly". At the launch, Oric said that prototypes of its peripherals were ready. In April *Sinclair User* reported that there were still no definite dates for them.

The 16K model was later still. In June 1983 *Personal Computer News* reported that it "should at last reach outlets by early July", at £129.95 rather than the £99 first promised. The 48K model cost £169.95, the price *Personal Computer Games* rounded to £170 and called "slightly dearer than the 48k Spectrum".

## The machine

The Oric used a [6502](/vault/hardware/6502), the processor Tangerine already knew. *Your Computer*'s review of the Atmos gives "a 6502A processor running at 1MHz". The 16K ROM held an extended Microsoft BASIC.

Sound came from a General Instrument [AY-3-8912](/vault/hardware/ay-3-8912), a chip that plays three tones and noise on its own once the processor has set its registers. The Spectrum had only a beeper. A [6522 VIA](/vault/hardware/mos-6522-via), a general-purpose input/output chip, worked with the sound chip to scan the keyboard, and handled the tape and the printer. Oric's own service manual and circuit diagram show both chips.

The keyboard had 57 keys that moved individually. *Personal Computer Games* called them "a sort of halfway house between a full keyboard and the Spectrum 'dead flesh' keys". A Centronics printer port and an RGB monitor socket were "fitted as standard".

### Serial attributes

The Oric's screen worked like teletext. The text screen was 40 characters across and 28 lines down. Colour and style were set by attributes: control codes stored in the line like characters. Bob Maunder's *The Oric-1 Companion* explained it in 1983: "An attribute character placed on a line is invisible but affects the way in which the rest of the information on that line is displayed, hence the name serial attributes." One attribute could turn the rest of a line red, or make it flash, or print it double height. There were eight colours.

The HIRES screen was 240 × 200 pixels. Each line was forty bytes, and each byte held either six pixels or an attribute. Reviewing the Atmos, *Your Computer* found that serial attributes "take some getting used to", but that they take up less memory.

### Tape and Prestel

Programs saved to cassette at 2,400 baud, with a slower 300 baud option "for extra security". From the start Oric promised a modem for Prestel, British Telecom's viewdata service, and the machine carried a teletext-style character set ready for it. In February 1983 *Your Computer* priced the modem at £79. In June 1984 Oric was still announcing a Prestel adaptor.

## The price war

The Oric's first year was a battle with Sinclair. *Retro Gamer* records that Oric cut its prices after Sinclair cut the Spectrum's in May 1983. By early 1984 a W H Smith advertisement listed the 48K Oric-1 at £139.95. *Retro Gamer* also reports that a fire at the factory in Feltham in October 1983 destroyed much of Oric's Christmas production.

Sales were still large for a newcomer. *Big K* wrote in April 1984 that the Oric 1 "sold over 160K during 1983". *Retro Gamer* gives about 160,000 as an estimate of British sales that year.

The company was short of money. *Personal Computer News* reported that Edenspring Investments had rescued Oric in October 1983, when its debts were £1.01 million, by issuing 25 million new Edenspring shares that raised £750,000. *Retro Gamer* puts Edenspring's funding at £4 million. The two accounts do not agree on the amount.

## The Atmos and the Microdisc

Oric replaced the Oric-1 with the Oric Atmos at the Which Computer? Show in January 1984. *Your Computer*'s reviewer, David Scobie, put it bluntly: the Oric-1 "is officially dead". The Atmos was the same machine inside. Its board was "identical to its predecessor and is still marked 'Oric-1'". The case was black and red, the keys were "full typewriter-style keys", and a revised ROM fixed bugs and added commands: programs could be verified after saving, a second program could be joined to one in memory, and arrays could be stored and recalled. *Big K* noted that existing Oric programs still ran. In Britain the Atmos cost about £170.

The disk drive took longer. When David Janda reviewed the Oric Microdisc for *Personal Computer News* in June 1984, he noted that it had been "coming soon" for over 14 months. It cost £270, used a 3-inch drive, and ran a disk operating system whose commands were "similar" to CP/M's. The Greek magazine *[Pixel](/vault/magazines/pixel)* reviewed it that September. Its importer in Athens was DATAKOR.

## France and further afield

The Oric did better in France than at home. Reporting from the Micro Expo in Paris in 1984, *Personal Computer News* wrote that "Oric really does have a foothold here". The French publisher Loriciels was showing Oric and Atmos games, some "equal to, if not far better than many sold in the UK". The Atmos cost 2,400 francs in France.

*Retro Gamer* reports that around 50,000 Oric-1s were sold in France in 1983. It also names *Théoric*, a French magazine devoted to the machine. [Éric Chahi](/vault/people/eric-chahi) bought an Oric-1 in 1983, at sixteen, and Loriciels published his game *Doggy*, written in 6502 assembly language. Loriciels also published the graphic adventure *L'Aigle d'Or* (1984), which *Retro Gamer* credits to Louis-Marie Rocques.

The machine travelled further too:

- **New Zealand:** Barr Bros Computers of Papakura were the sole New Zealand agents when *Bits & Bytes* reviewed the Oric 1 at the end of 1983.
- **Greece:** *Pixel* listed the Oric 1 among the twelve machines its first issue, in late 1983, printed programs for. DATAKOR of Athens imported the Microdisc.
- **Yugoslavia:** *Svet kompjutera* ran an Oric column in 1986, with a list of Oric programs available from an organisation in Ljubljana.

## The Stratos, receivership and Eureka

Oric's next machine was the Stratos. *Personal Computer News* reported in December 1984 that it would go on sale in France in January and be called the IQ164 in Britain. The paper described a machine with 64K of RAM, a 6502 and two ROM slots, and Bruce Everiss, managing director of Oric's software arm Tansoft, said it would run existing Oric and Atmos software. It was shown at the Frankfurt fair early in 1985, but Oric never sold it.

Oric called in the receiver in February 1985. *Sinclair User* estimated its debts at £5.5 million, "especially to Edenspring Investments", its financial backers. Its verdict on Oric's British career was harsh: "The Oric 1 was launched to compete with the Spectrum and failed miserably; the Atmos too did not do well in this country." The receiver, Dennis Cross, had offers from Europe, including one from a French syndicate that included Oric's French distributor, ASN. On the Continent, *Personal Computer News* said, "things have always looked rosier for the company".

The buyer was Eureka Informatique, a company that distributed British computers in France. *Your Computer* reported in July 1985 that Eureka had paid "several hundred thousand pounds" for Oric. It got the rights to the Stratos and to a 68000-based machine, plus stocks of components and finished Atmoses, and it would move Atmos assembly to its plant in Normandy. *Your Computer* later dated the purchase to June 1985 and added that "France had always been the strongest Oric market".

## The Telestrat

Under its French owners the new Oric was the Telestrat, a French machine of 1986. *Retro Gamer* describes it as designed for France's Minitel online network. In June 1986 *Svet kompjutera* told its Yugoslav readers that the new Oric Telestrat was coming soon. *Retro Gamer* says it went on sale in September 1986 at £400 and sold only about 6,000.

## Software

British software houses supported the Oric from the start. Tansoft was Oric's own publisher, and IJK, [Ocean](/vault/companies/ocean-software), [Durell](/vault/companies/durell), [Level 9](/vault/companies/level-9) and [Melbourne House](/vault/companies/melbourne-house) all released Oric games. Melbourne House's *[The Hobbit](/vault/games/the-hobbit)* kept its pictures on the Oric, where the BBC Micro version had lost them. In France, Loriciels was the leading Oric publisher.
