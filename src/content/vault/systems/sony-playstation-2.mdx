The PlayStation 2 sold more units than any games console before it, and its production run lasted almost thirteen years. Sony's own figures put its shipments at more than 160 million. It arrived in Japan on 4 March 2000 to scenes *Edge* had never seen at a console launch: police were called to Tokyo's Akihabara electronics district to control the crowds.

Inside, it was two machines in one. A new processor, the Emotion Engine, ran PlayStation 2 games. Beside it, doing the input and output, sat the processor from the original [PlayStation](/vault/systems/sony-playstation), which could take over the whole machine to run the old games. The console also played DVD films, which Sony had not promised when it first described the machine.

## Fast facts

- **Manufacturer:** [Sony Computer Entertainment](/vault/companies/sony).
- **Launch:** Japan 4 March 2000 (¥39,800); North America 26 October 2000 ($299); Europe 24 November 2000 (£299 in the UK).
- **CPU:** Emotion Engine, a "128bit RISC (MIPS IV subset)" design, at 294.912 MHz.
- **Graphics:** Graphics Synthesizer at 147.456 MHz, with 4 MB of embedded video memory.
- **RAM:** 32 MB of Direct Rambus main memory.
- **Sound:** SPU2, 48 channels plus software voices, with 2 MB of sound memory.
- **I/O processor:** a PlayStation CPU, running at the original machine's 33.8688 MHz or at 36.864 MHz, with 2 MB of its own memory.
- **Media:** DVD-ROM (4x) and CD-ROM (24x); plays DVD films and audio CDs.
- **Storage:** 8 MB memory card.
- **Production ended:** 2013.
- **Sales:** more than 160 million, by Sony's count.

## The specification, as announced

Sony revealed the new technology at an event in Tokyo in early 1999, held amid celebrations for the 50-millionth PlayStation, and *Edge* printed the specification table that April. The processor was listed at 300 MHz and the main memory as "32Mb Direct Rambus". The table went on:

| | |
|---|---|
| Floating point | 6.2 GFLOPS |
| Geometry, transform only | 66m polys/sec |
| ...plus lighting | 38m polys/sec |
| ...plus fog | 36m polys/sec |
| Bézier surface generation | 16m polys/sec |
| Image processing | 150m pixels/sec |
| Memory bus | 3.2 Gb/sec |

The geometry rows show how the headline figure was reached. Transforming polygons alone gives 66 million a second; lighting them brings that down to 38 million, and adding fog to 36 million. Those are still theoretical peaks. When *Edge* compared the three new consoles in January 2002, it put the PS2's "estimated real performance" at 20 million polygons a second.

The Graphics Synthesizer's figures are about moving data. Its video memory sits on the same chip as the drawing hardware, joined by a "DRAM Bus Width" of "2,560bits" for a "Total Memory Bandwidth" of 48 GB a second. That is fifteen times the main memory bus. Each pixel stores 24 bits of colour, 8 of alpha (transparency) and a 32-bit depth value: "RGB:Alpha:Z Buffer (24:8:32)".

By the machine's public unveiling in Tokyo on 13 September 1999, hosted by Ken Kutaragi, the clocks had been set at 294.912 MHz for the Emotion Engine and 147.456 MHz for the Graphics Synthesizer. The embedded video memory was 4 MB. Sony also showed the Dual Shock 2 joypad, whose buttons were "analogue, velocity-sensitive", and an 8 MB memory card. A development kit cost ¥2,000,000.

## The old machine is still in there

The shortest line in the April 1999 table is the most interesting. Under the I/O processor, where a specification normally names a controller chip, it says:

> CPU Core — Current PlayStation CPU

The PlayStation 2's input/output processor (IOP) looks after the controllers, memory cards and disc drive while the Emotion Engine runs the game. Its core is the original PlayStation's processor. In the September 1999 table its clock is "33.8688/36.864MHz (selectable)", and 33.8688 MHz is exactly the speed Sony's manual gives for the first PlayStation's CPU. When a PlayStation disc goes in, that processor runs the old game.

## DVD

In April 1999 *Edge* expected the first games to come on CD-ROM and reported that the console was "not expected to play DVD movies out of the box", with an add-on module to follow. By September Sony had changed course: the machine would play DVD films as it came. *Edge* watched a demonstration disc at the Tokyo Game Show and judged the picture equal to dedicated players "in the £500 region", with Dolby Digital and DTS sound through an optical output.

## Launch

Sony aimed to ship a million consoles in the first week in Japan. Shops in Akihabara handed out numbered tickets, and the police stepped in on launch day. According to Sony's own figures, reported by *Edge*, 480,000 machines were sold through its website and 620,000 through shops between 4 and 6 March.

The US launch on 26 October 2000 was smaller than planned: Sony cut its launch shipment from a million units to 500,000. Europe followed on 24 November. In Britain the £299 price drew what *Edge* called "a series of indignant headlines", and Sony's UK marketing director, Alan Welsman, defended it: "There is no gain in us pricing it higher than we think the market can bear."

## Programming it at home

Sony gave European owners a way to program the machine from the first day. Every European console came with a demo disc carrying Yabasic, an open-source BASIC interpreter that an SCEE team led by Mike Dean had ported with its author's permission, adding a text editor, an HTML viewer and support for USB keyboards. "Most of the people who are into games programming now grew up on Commodores, BBCs, and Spectrums," Dean told *Edge*. "Their way into programming was through simple BASIC languages that came free with the machine." The examples included a spinning 3D object and a particle demonstration, though Dean warned that "People won't be able to make multi-level 3D dungeons with Yabasic".

Sony went further with Linux for PlayStation 2, released first in Japan and then, in 2002, in Europe and North America. The European kit cost €249 plus VAT and contained a 40 GB hard drive, an Ethernet adaptor, a USB keyboard and mouse, and a monitor cable. It had shipped over 9,000 in Japan and 2,000 in Europe by mid-2002. "This isn't something we want to promote as a mainstream release," Sarah Ewen, who ran the programme, told *Edge*. "It's designed for those people who already know a bit about Linux or those who are interested in programming on their PlayStation2s." *Edge* noted that the kit marked the first time Sony had released the PS2's hardware manuals outside its licensed developers.

## Games

The PlayStation 2 kept the first machine's publishers and gained Sega's. When [Sega](/vault/companies/sega) left the hardware business in January 2001, it announced *Virtua Fighter 4* for the PS2. [Capcom](/vault/companies/capcom)'s *Devil May Cry* began there in August 2001, and [DMA Design](/vault/companies/dma-design)'s *[Grand Theft Auto III](/vault/games/gta-iii)* came out on the PS2 that year. Online play arrived with a separate network adaptor, on sale in the US and Japan by the autumn of 2002, while Europe was still waiting.

Looking back in 2013, *Edge* counted "all 3,800 games" and singled out Fumito Ueda's *Ico* and *Shadow of the Colossus*, Hideki Kamiya's *Okami*, Keita Takahashi's *Katamari Damacy*, Harmonix's *Guitar Hero*, Sony London Studio's *SingStar* and Rockstar's 3D *GTA* games.

## Later years

A slimmer model with built-in Ethernet followed in 2004, according to public sources. The machine went on selling long after the PlayStation 3 arrived in 2006; public sources give 4 January 2013 as the end of production. *Edge* called it "the highest-selling videogame console of all time — 155 million and counting" in October 2013. Sony's corporate figures now give cumulative shipments of more than 160 million.
