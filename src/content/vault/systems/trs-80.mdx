On 3 August 1977 Radio Shack, the electronics chain owned by [Tandy](/vault/companies/tandy), held a press party in New York to announce a computer. Its first newsletter to owners thanked "you modern day pioneers who are willing to buy this exciting product sight unseen in most cases". The TRS-80 came fully built and tested, with a screen, a cassette recorder and a 300-page manual, for $599.95, and you bought it over the counter in the same shops that sold Radio Shack's own stereo receivers and radio scanners, which the newsletter mentioned in the same breath. By Radio Shack's own advertising in *BYTE* that October, that meant "over 6000 locations in nine countries".

Radio Shack never said how many it sold, and the guesses show how quickly it took off. In the autumn of 1978 *Creative Computing* estimated 8,000 to 20,000. In August 1979 *SoftSide* put the figure at "more than 150,000". In June 1980 *BYTE* reported a Radio Shack executive as saying the company had manufactured 370,000 TRS-80s by 1 March 1980, at a rate of 600 to 700 a day. Its users called it the "Trash-80", though *Electronic Fun with Computer Games* noted in 1982 that they said it about their "beloved machine".

## Fast facts
- **Manufacturer:** Radio Shack, a division of [Tandy Corporation](/vault/companies/tandy).
- **Announced:** 3 August 1977, New York.
- **CPU:** [Zilog Z80](/vault/hardware/z80) at about 1.77 MHz: a 10.6445 MHz crystal divided by six.
- **RAM:** 4K as standard, 16K inside the case, up to 48K with the Expansion Interface.
- **ROM:** Level I BASIC in 4K; Level II BASIC, written by Microsoft, in 12K.
- **Display:** 64×16 characters (Level II adds 32×16), with block graphics at 128×48.
- **Sound:** none.
- **Storage:** cassette; floppy disks through the Expansion Interface.
- **Price:** $599.95 with screen and cassette recorder.

## Everything is memory

Tandy's technical handbook states the design principle plainly: "the TRS-80
microcomputer system is Memory Mapped". The Level I memory map shows what that
means in practice.

| Address | Contents |
|---|---|
| `0000–0FFF` | Level I ROMs |
| `3800–38FF` | **Keyboard** |
| `3C00–3FFF` | **Video Display** |
| `4000–41FF` | RAM used by BASIC |
| `4200–` | "Useable RAM starts here" |

The keyboard is not a port. It is 256 bytes of address space you read, and the
screen is 1,024 bytes you write, so a program can put a character on screen with
a single store instruction. The handbook draws the distinction itself: "In
memory mapping, the CPU knows where the data is. In a port, the CPU does not
know and couldn't care where the data is located." The Level I machine used
exactly one port, for the cassette recorder.

## A keyboard that doesn't know its own letters

The 53 keys are "single-pole, single-throw normally open", wired as a matrix, and
the handbook is clear about what they do not do:

> this Keyboard does not output ASCII. It is scanned, like calculator-type
> keyboards. Each key represents a switch across a matrix node. When closed, the
> switch will short out a horizontal line to a vertical line. ROM software will
> detect the node short and generate ASCII equivalents for that particular key.

There is no keyboard encoder chip. The machine reads a grid of shorted wires and
works out in software which letter that was. Owners met the other side of that
bargain. In November 1978 Radio Shack's newsletter described "what we call
'Keybounce' — multiple letters from one keystroke", blamed dirty key contacts,
and promised a cassette program that would extend the ROM's "debounce" routine.

## Six RAMs, seven bits

The best example of building to a price is in the video section. A character
code needs seven bits. The TRS-80 stores six.

> There are seven RAMs. Six are used for ASCII storage, and the seventh is used
> as a graphic/alphanumeric definition bit. There are eight data lines. Notice
> the line labeled Bit 6. It is sourced by NOR gate Z30 at pin 13. If Bit 5 and
> Bit 7 are low, then Bit 6 will be high. **Z30 is a sneaky way of squeezing a
> seventh ASCII bit out of six RAMs.**

Bit 6 of every character on screen is not stored anywhere. It is computed, at
display time, from the state of two other bits. The seventh RAM is spent not on
the code but on a flag saying whether this cell is a letter or a graphic. Saving
one memory chip per machine was worth designing the character set around, and it
is why a stock Model I cannot show lower-case letters.

The graphics that flag selects are equally frugal. Each character cell is split
into six smaller rectangles, two across and three down, so the 64×16 text
screen becomes the 128×48 block-graphics grid the machine is remembered for. The
"graphics generator" chip, in the handbook's words, "does not generate
anything. Rather, it steers the ASCII addresses around to simulate a graphics
generator." There is no bitmap mode: every picture on a TRS-80 is built from
those blocks.

## Level I and Level II

The first machines shipped with Level I BASIC in 4K of ROM. In January 1978
Radio Shack wrote to owners announcing the next step: Level II BASIC, a 16K
memory upgrade, and an Expansion Interface that took the machine to 32K or 48K
and added a printer port and up to four mini-floppy drives. A CPU with 4K and
Level II cost $499; with 16K, $789.

The handbook explains the difference in one line: "Level I uses 4K of ROM. In
Level II, the system uses 12K of ROM." The extra 8K, it says, was "enough to give text editing;
transcendental functions; giant numeric and string arrays". Level II could also
use the display's 32-character mode, which Level I could not reach. The
code came from Microsoft; a 1980 guide to the ROM names it as "Level II ROM
BASIC written by Microsoft's Paul Allen and Bill Gates".

## Software

A machine in thousands of shops drew programmers. Scott Adams wrote
*Adventureland* for his own 16K TRS-80, the first of the adventure games that
brought the [text adventure](/vault/genres/text-adventure) to home computers, and
by early 1979 his games were advertised in the TRS-80 magazine *SoftSide*. Radio Shack sold its own programs, including the
*Scripsit* word processor, and Michael Shrayer's *Electric Pencil* was an
independent word processor for the machine. Radio Shack's own newsletter and,
from 1980, *80 Microcomputing* printed listings and hardware projects every
month.

## The FCC and the Model III

The Model I's open design, with a keyboard unit, separate screen and a ribbon
cable to the Expansion Interface, leaked radio interference. New US rules
required "home computers" made after 1 January 1981 to meet limits on
interference with televisions and radios. Jon Shirley, a Radio Shack vice
president, explained the decision to owners in March 1981: meeting the rules
took "new circuit board layouts, metal shields, added capacitors, shielded
ribbon cable (very expensive) plus a little black magic", and "there was no way
to make the Model I meet the requirements without a total redesign. Since the
Model III was, in effect, a totally redesigned Model I, our decision became
obvious also." Model III production with FCC certification began in January
1981.

## The rest of the family

- **Model II (1979):** a business machine announced at the National Computer Conference in June 1979, with a 4 MHz Z80, 32K and an 8-inch floppy drive, from $3,450.
- **Model III (1980):** the Model I rebuilt as one unit with the screen and room for disk drives, from $699 for 4K and Level I to $2,495 for 32K with two drives. Radio Shack said it would run "appropriate Model I software".
- **Color Computer (1980):** announced alongside the Model III at $399, but built around Motorola's 6809 and not compatible with the Z80 machines. See [Tandy Color Computer](/vault/systems/tandy-coco).
- **Model 4 (1983):** the Z80 line's successor to the Model III, on sale by mid-1983.

After the IBM PC, Tandy moved its mainstream computers to PC compatibles such as the Tandy 1000. The public sources used here do not establish when the last Z80 TRS-80 was made.
