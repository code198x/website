The **Jupiter Ace** was announced in the summer of 1982 and sold by mail order at
**£89.95** — the price included the machine, a power supply, leads for a
television and a cassette recorder, a software catalogue and a manual.

It was built by **Jupiter Cantab**, formed by **Richard Altwasser** and **Steven
Vickers** after they left [Sinclair Research](/vault/companies/sinclair-research).
Their own advertising made no secret of where they had come from: "leading
computer designers Richard Altwasser and Steven Vickers... after playing the major
role in creating the ZX Spectrum they formed Jupiter Cantab to develop their
latest brainchild the Jupiter Ace." *Computer & Video Games* introduced it in
November 1982 as the work of "two ex-Sinclair engineers".

One decision defines the machine.

## Forth in ROM

Every other home computer of the period booted into BASIC. The Ace booted into
**Forth**.

Jupiter Cantab's case for it was a performance argument. Their advertisement —
the same copy in *Computer and Video Games* for November 1982 and *Your
Computer* that December and the following January — claimed Forth is "typically
**four times as compact and ten times as fast as BASIC**", and framed the choice
as democratisation: "Before the Ace all personal computers used BASIC and Forth
was only available to a privileged few."

⚠ Both of those are the manufacturer's marketing, not a measurement, and the
library holds nothing that tests the four-times or ten-times figures. On a
machine with 3K of RAM the compactness claim is at least the one that would
matter most.

Forth is also a different idea about what a beginner's language is. BASIC gives
you a fixed vocabulary and asks you to arrange it. Forth gives you a mechanism for
**adding words to the language**, so that a program is a vocabulary you built and
the last word you define is the program. It is closer to how the machine works and
closer to how a compiler works, and it rewards a reader who wants to know what is
underneath.

What that means in practice is best shown by what the Ace does *not* have. A
period guide, *The Forth Bridge*, tabulates BASIC keywords against their Forth
equivalents, and the entries are startling to anyone expecting a language to
come finished. Against `SIN`: "Not in FORTH, but the Ace manual gives a series
definition of sine." Against `SQR`, no equivalent either — instead a
Newton-Raphson square root, written out as Forth words to be typed in:

```text
: 2OVER ( f1, f2 - f1, f2, f1) 4 PICK 4 PICK ;
: 2SWAP ( f1, f2 - f2, f1) 4 ROLL 4 ROLL ;
: SQR   ( f - square root of f) 1. 10 0 DO 2OVER 2OVER F/ F+ .5 F* LOOP 2SWAP 2DROP ;
```

A machine sold to beginners shipped without a square root, on the understanding
that you would define one — and that having defined it, you would own a language
that had it. The same book puts the pitch better than the advertising did: Forth
"enables him or her to begin from readily learnable words and then advance step
by step to programs of greater complexity — **building word on word**."

Almost nobody bought it.

## Fast facts

- **Manufacturer:** Jupiter Cantab, Cambridge
- **Designers:** Richard Altwasser and [Steven Vickers](/vault/people/steven-vickers)
- **Announced:** summer 1982 · **Launch price:** £89.95, mail order only
- **CPU:** Z80A at 3.25 MHz
- **Memory:** 8K ROM, **3K RAM**
- **Display:** 24 × 32 characters, memory-mapped, flicker-free, monochrome, with
  user-definable high-resolution graphics
- **Language:** Forth in ROM, with floating-point arithmetic
- **Also:** programmable sound generator, fast cassette interface, full upper and
  lower case ASCII

## What went wrong

The promised expansions did not arrive. A parallel printer interface was
advertised "for around £20.00" and a 16K memory expansion "for around £30.00"; by
1983 the users' club was writing that "the lack of promised add-ons from Jupiter
Cantab indicates a cash-flow problem". Third parties filled the gap instead —
Stonechip and Essex Microelectronics made RAM packs, the club itself sold an
adaptor kit that let ZX81 hardware be used on the Ace.

**Richard Altwasser left Jupiter Cantab during 1983**, Geoffrey Walker joining
Vickers as co-director. The company crashed later the same year.

*ACE* magazine's verdict, looking back in 1990, was blunt about the cause: the Ace
was "the only home computer to sport Forth as its inbuilt language. **This proved
to be its downfall.**" The same magazine filed Jupiter Cantab among the Cambridge
companies as "the ill-fated splinter group from Sinclair Research".

There was a second cause that the specification sheet does not show. A 1998
retrospective in *CU Amiga* remembered the build: a case of thin white plastic
held together with plastic rivets, and a keyboard that was an extension of the
main circuit board, its tracks shorted by conductive pads — "each key worked for
about three months, and then a new keyboard had to be bought". The Ace was made
cheaply and it showed. Meanwhile "the Spectrum eventually appeared in big
numbers, and blew the Jupiter Ace away".

By the 1984-85 price war the remaining stock was going for £35.

## The afterlife, which is the interesting part

The company died in 1983 and the machine did not.

**Boldfield** bought the bankrupt stock and kept selling — an Ace with a 16K RAM
pack for £44 plus VAT against an original £89.95 and £34.95, and software cut
from £7.95 to £3 — then assembled a list of add-ons and eventually commissioned
new software for a computer whose manufacturer no longer existed.

The **users' club** did more. Its newsletter, *ACE User* (later *ACE User / Forth
User*), edited by **John Noyce**, ran a software catalogue that paid contributors
**20% of sale price, quarterly and promptly**; catalogued third-party RAM packs,
sound boards and a colour board; sold its own ZX81 adaptor kit; and printed ROM
addresses and Forth benchmarks sent in by readers. Its 1984 editorial states the
position exactly: "**Jupiter Cantab may have crashed in 1983, but the Ace lives
on**, indeed there has been a renewal of enthusiasm for it this year."

It is a small, clear example of something this project cares about: a platform's
usable life is set by its documentation and its community, not by its
manufacturer's balance sheet. The Ace had 3K of RAM and no company, and people
were still writing new software for it two years after the company failed.

## A footnote on the ROM

[Ian Logan](/vault/people/ian-logan), who had already disassembled Sinclair's
ZX80, ZX81 and Spectrum ROMs, got at the Ace's ROM as well. The method is
recorded in the club newsletter for December 1982: he **cross-loaded it from an
Ace to a Spectrum**, the two machines' cassette standards being identical.

Which is what you would expect. The people who designed the Ace had designed the
Spectrum, and they used the tape format they already had.
