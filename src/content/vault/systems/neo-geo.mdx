The Neo Geo wasn't for normal people. At $649 for the console and $200+ per
game, it cost more than competitors combined. But it delivered something nothing
else could: actual arcade games at home. Not ports, not conversions — the same
hardware, the same games. For fighting game enthusiasts, nothing else compared.

SNK's own *Neo-Geo Specification* explains the look better than the price does.
The machine is built almost entirely out of sprites.

## Fast facts
- **Manufacturer:** SNK Corporation.
- **Released:** April 1990 (Japan), August 1990 (NA).
- **CPU:** Hitachi **HD68HC000PS-12** at 12 MHz — a CMOS 68000, as SNK's
  specification names it, rather than a Motorola part.
- **Secondary CPU:** [Zilog Z80](/vault/hardware/z80) (sound).
- **Work RAM:** 64K bytes. **VRAM:** 64K + 4K bytes.
- **Screen:** 320×224 (256 lines on PAL).
- **Colours:** 4,096 registers from 65,536 — **3,840 of them drawable**.
- **Sprites:** 380 line sprites, but only **96 across a line**.
- **Sound:** Yamaha YM2610.

## A machine with no background

Most consoles of the period draw a tiled background and put sprites on top. The
Neo Geo has three layers, and only one of them is a conventional playfield:

| Priority | Layer | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (front) | FIX | 40 × 28 characters of 8 × 8 pixels — a text and status overlay |
| 2 | 3D-LINE SPRITE | everything that moves, and almost everything that doesn't |
| 3 (back) | Background | "One color is specified from 65,536 colors" |

The background layer is a single flat colour. Scenery is not drawn on it — SNK
says plainly that "back characters, such as backgrounds, [are] also comprised
entirely of line sprites".

A "line sprite" is not one sprite in the usual sense. It is 32 characters of
16 × 16 dots stacked vertically: a strip 16 dots wide and 512 dots tall, taller
than the screen. A Neo Geo background is a row of these strips standing side by
side.

That is why the hardware needs 380 of them, and why the number sounds
extravagant next to the [Mega Drive's](/vault/systems/sega-mega-drive) 80. They
are not doing the same job. A Mega Drive spends its sprites on characters; the
Neo Geo spends most of its on the room they are standing in.

## The limit that shaped every game

380 is the total. The number that constrains a programmer is smaller:

> Up to 380 3D-LINE SPRITEs can be displayed simultaneously, but only 96 can be
> placed horizontally.

And the failure mode is unusually harsh. Exceed 96 across a line and the machine
does not drop the least important sprite:

> should over 96 line sprites be horizontally displayed at one time, line sprites
> from the highest priority will start missing. Every program, therefore, must be
> worked out with this in mind.

The sprites that vanish first are the ones at the front. On most hardware an
overloaded line loses background clutter; here it loses the fighters.

## Colour, counted honestly

SNK's summary sheet gives the figure twice, and the second version is the useful
one: "4096 colors out of 65536 colors simultaneous color display **(15 colors x
256 palettes)**". A palette holds 16 entries but "consists of 16 colors with 0 as
blank" — so 256 × 15 = **3,840** colours can actually be painted, with the
4,096 counting the transparent entries the hardware reserves.

The same shape appears on the [Mega Drive](/vault/systems/sega-mega-drive)
(64 registers, 60 drawable) and the [Game Boy](/vault/systems/game-boy) (four
shades, three on a sprite). Every one of these machines is quoted by the size of
its colour table rather than the number of colours it can show.

## Why the zooming looks the way it does

The scaling effects that made *Art of Fighting* famous are a hardware function,
and the specification explains their asymmetry:

> The sprite can be reduced from 100% to 0%. The reductions can be done in 256
> levels vertically and 16 levels horizontally, (The reason that there are only
> 16 levels horizontally is that a one-line sprite consists of 16 dots
> horizontally.)

Vertical scaling is nearly continuous because a line sprite is 512 dots tall.
Horizontal scaling is coarse because it is only 16 dots wide. The zoom is finer
in one axis than the other, and the reason is the geometry of the strip.

## The MVS connection
Neo Geo's secret was shared hardware:
- **MVS:** Multi Video System arcade boards.
- **AES:** Advanced Entertainment System home console.
- **Same games:** Identical cartridges (with adapters).
- **Cost sharing:** Development served both markets.

## Why so expensive?
The price reflected reality:
- **Cartridge size:** Up to 716 Mbit (89.5 MB).
- **Arcade hardware:** No cost-reduction compromises.
- **Memory:** More RAM than home computers.
- **Quality:** Games looked identical to arcade.

## Fighting game dominance
Neo Geo became the fighting game platform:
- **Fatal Fury series:** SNK's Street Fighter competitor.
- **Art of Fighting:** Zooming characters, dramatic style.
- **Samurai Shodown:** Weapon-based combat.
- **The King of Fighters:** Dream match crossovers.

The competitive fighting game community embraced Neo Geo.

## The game library
SNK's output was prolific:
- **Fighters:** Fatal Fury, KOF, Samurai Shodown, Last Blade.
- **Shooters:** Pulstar, Blazing Star, Last Resort.
- **Action:** Metal Slug series (later).
- **Sports:** Neo Geo Cup, Baseball Stars.

## Metal Slug
The franchise defined Neo Geo's later years:
- **Run-and-gun perfection:** Smooth animation, tight controls.
- **Visual spectacle:** Hand-drawn animation masterpiece.
- **Humour:** Personality rare in action games.
- **Series:** Six mainline entries, multiple spin-offs.

## The Neo Geo CD
Cost reduction attempt (1994):
- CD-ROM drive instead of cartridges.
- Games at $50-80 instead of $200+.
- Brutal loading times (sometimes minutes).
- Undermined the platform's appeal.

## Enthusiast culture
Neo Geo created dedicated communities:
- **Import scene:** Japanese games brought West.
- **Converters:** MVS-to-AES adapters for cheaper games.
- **Collectors:** Rare cartridges worth thousands.
- **Tournaments:** Fighting game competitive scene.

## Legacy
The Neo Geo proved a market existed for premium gaming—and defined its limits. The enthusiast audience was real but small. SNK couldn't sustain the business and eventually went bankrupt (2001), though the brand and games continue under new ownership. The Neo Geo remains the pinnacle of 2D gaming hardware.
