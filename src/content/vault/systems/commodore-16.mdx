The **Commodore 16** and **Plus/4** are remembered as the machines Commodore got
wrong — the 264 series, incompatible with the C64, discontinued quickly. This
Vault can test how that reputation varies, because it holds eighteen magazines
across ten territories that all wrote about Commodore machines.

Counting mentions of the C16 and Plus/4 as a proportion of mentions of the C64 in
the same pages:

| Magazine | Country | C16 + Plus/4 vs C64 |
|---|---|---:|
| *[Mikroszámítógép Magazin](/vault/magazines/mikroszamitogep-magazin)* | Hungary | **60%** |
| *[Commodore Computer Club](/vault/magazines/commodore-computer-club)* | Italy | 47% |
| *[Your Commodore](/vault/magazines/your-commodore)* | **Britain** | **45%** |
| *[Drean Commodore](/vault/magazines/drean-commodore)* | Argentina | 23% |
| *Mikrobáze* | Czechoslovakia | 21% |
| *[Bajtek](/vault/magazines/bajtek)* | Poland | 15% |
| *[CRASH](/vault/magazines/crash-magazine)* | Britain | 10% |
| *[ZZAP!64](/vault/magazines/zzap-64)* | **Britain** | **4%** |
| *Mikroklan* | Poland | 2% |

### How this was counted

Counted over the OCR'd text of every issue held of each title, case-insensitively,
as **total mentions** rather than pages containing a mention — the two differ by
up to seventeen points on the same magazine, so the unit is part of the result.
C16 and Plus/4: `commodore 16`, `c16`, `c-16`, `plus/4`, `plus 4`. C64:
`commodore 64`, `c64`, `c-64`, `cbm 64`. Re-derived 2026-08-31 against an index
of 1,350,695 pages.

`264` is deliberately not a search term. It matches 6,891 pages, most of them
H.264 video compression, table-of-contents page numbers and BASIC `DATA`
statements; including it moves the table by an average of 51 points. The C116 is
excluded too, being a separate machine rather than a C16 — though "the 264
series" remains the right name for the family.

⚠ *CRASH* is filed in this library under two names, and the earlier one holds
issues 1–13 of 1984–85 — precisely the 264-era issues. Counting only the larger
file gives 10.7% instead of 9.8%. The figure above counts both.

## The gap is not between countries

The obvious reading — a machine that flopped in Britain and found a home in
Eastern Europe — does not survive the table. Hungary is highest at 60%, but
Britain's *Your Commodore* is third at 45%, ahead of every non-British title
except two.

The widest gap between two titles that ran through the machines' own years is
**inside Britain**: *Your Commodore* at 45% against *ZZAP!64* at 4%. Eleven
times the attention, same country, overlapping years. And the two Polish titles
sit at 15% and 2%.

Hungary's pair is wider still — *Mikroszámítógép Magazin* at 60% against *576
KByte* at 2% — but the two are not comparable in the way the British pair is:
576 KByte began after the 264 series was over, so its 2% measures a magazine
that arrived too late rather than one that chose games. The British pair
overlaps in time, which is what makes the difference between them editorial.

What separates them is what the magazine was for. *Your Commodore* covered
Commodore's range; *ZZAP!64* covered C64 games. A budget machine with a small
games library is invisible to one and unavoidable for the other.

This is the same result this Vault keeps arriving at from different directions:
[the institution and audience of a magazine predict its
contents](/vault/culture/institutional-computer-press) better than its country
does.

## What survives the caveat

Two things do look territorial rather than editorial.

**Hungary at 60%** is the highest figure in the set, and the Hungarian press
covers the 264 series more heavily than any British general Commodore magazine.
Hungary is one of the few markets where the C16 is remembered as genuinely
common.

**Argentina had the C16 without the Plus/4.** *Drean Commodore* mentions the C16
194 times and the Plus/4 four times. [Drean built Commodore machines under
licence](/vault/magazines/drean-commodore), and its magazine suggests the cheap
member of the pair crossed and the expensive one did not.

⚠ Mention counts measure editorial attention, not sales, and a ratio against the
C64 measures attention *relative to the flagship* rather than absolute interest.
A magazine may discuss a machine because it is popular or because its readers
need help with it.

⚠ The magazines cover different year spans, and the 264 series was current only
from 1984. Titles running mostly after 1987 — *576 KByte* at 2%, *Amiga Joker*
at 4% — are low partly because they started too late to care.

## What the machine actually was

The reputation is easier to test than the hardware, so this entry has led with
the reputation. But the 264 series was not a cut-down C64; it was a different
design, and the chip at its centre is unusually ambitious.

**TED** — the MOS 7360 — does nearly everything. Commodore's specification lists
"dynamic RAM refresh, sound generation, programmable video time standards", and
elsewhere on the same page adds "system timing, dynamic RAM control, ROM
control, and keyboard scanning", across 34 control registers. Video, audio,
memory refresh, timing and the keyboard, in one part. The C64 needs a VIC-II, a
SID and two CIAs to cover the same ground.

The consequence for the processor is the reverse of the usual arrangement. On
the [C64](/vault/systems/commodore-64) or the
[Atari 8-bit](/vault/systems/atari-8-bit), the display steals cycles from a CPU
running at its normal rate. Here the CPU's normal rate *is* the fast one: TED
"outputs double clock" and drops to single clock only in the horizontal window
where it is "doing processor handshaking, character fetches, and dynamic RAM
refresh". Blanking the screen recovers most of it — with the specification
noting that "double clock is enabled at all times except refresh", because the
RAM must still be refreshed whether or not anyone is looking at it.

### Even Commodore could not agree how many colours it had

The TED's palette is a hue-and-luminance grid rather than a fixed list, and
Commodore's own publications give two different totals for it:

| Document | Figure |
|---|---|
| C16 service manual | "121 Colors (15 colors; 8 luminance levels and Black)" |
| Plus/4 service manual | "128 Colors (16 colors; 8 luminance levels)" |
| Plus/4 Programmer's Reference Guide | "can produce 121 different colors" |

Both are defensible and the reference guide explains why. The register encoding
takes "the 16 colors and 8 luminances", computed as `luminance*16+color-1` —
**128 combinations**. But one of those sixteen is black, and black at eight
different luminances is black eight times, so the count of *distinct* colours is
15 × 8 + 1 = **121**. The Plus/4 service manual counted the encoding; the other
two counted what you can see.

A machine dismissed as an afterthought had a palette its manufacturer described
three ways in three documents — and 121 colours against the C64's 16.
