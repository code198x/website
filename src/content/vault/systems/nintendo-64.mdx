The Nintendo 64 was a Silicon Graphics workstation design cut down to the price of a toy. Nintendo and SGI announced it together in San Francisco on 23 August 1993 as "Project Reality", promising a home machine for about $250. What reached Japanese shops on 23 June 1996 had one custom chip at its centre, SGI's Reality Coprocessor, and that chip controlled everything: memory, graphics, sound and video output. Even its graphics pipeline was a program. The game chose which one to load.

Nintendo made two other choices that set it apart from the CD-based PlayStation and Saturn. It kept cartridges, which load almost instantly but hold far less and cost far more to make. And it put four controller ports on the front. Shigeru Miyamoto explained why to *Edge* in 1996: "for the first time we have a machine with a fast enough CPU to handle four independent screens at speed. That's why we decided to include four joypad ports on the machine itself." Nintendo's own figures put lifetime sales at 32.93 million, about 20.6 million of them in the Americas.

## Fast facts

- **Manufacturer:** [Nintendo](/vault/companies/nintendo), with the Reality Coprocessor designed by Silicon Graphics.
- **Launched:** Japan 23 June 1996; United States 29 September 1996; Britain 1 March 1997.
- **Launch prices:** ¥25,000; $199.95; £250.
- **CPU:** a 64-bit MIPS R4300-series processor (NEC's VR4300) at **93.75 MHz**, with a 32-bit connection to the rest of the machine.
- **Reality Coprocessor (RCP):** 62.5 MHz. It contains the Reality Signal Processor (RSP), which runs geometry and audio microcode, and the Reality Display Processor (RDP), which draws pixels.
- **Memory:** 4 MB of Rambus DRAM, built from nine-bit bytes; a 4 MB Expansion Pak doubles it.
- **Texture memory:** 4 KB inside the RDP.
- **Resolution:** 256×224 to 640×480.
- **Media:** cartridge; the 64DD magnetic-disk drive was sold only in Japan.
- **Controllers:** four ports built in.
- **Sales:** 32.93 million worldwide (Nintendo).

## One chip in the middle of everything

Nintendo's *Ultra 64 Programming Manual* opens its hardware chapter with a sentence that reorganises the machine:

> The RCP is the center of the game; all data must pass through it. It acts as
> the memory controller for the CPU.

The R4300 does not address memory itself. On the far side of the processor's bus sits the Reality Coprocessor, and memory is on the far side of that. The manual describes every other part in relation to the same chip: "Attached to the RCP are memory chips, the MIPS R4300 CPU, and some miscellaneous I/O chips."

There is only one pool of memory, and everything draws on it at once. The RDP renders into a framebuffer in main memory. The video interface reads that framebuffer to make the picture, and the audio interface fetches sound data from the same memory to feed the audio DACs. The processor's code and data live there too. *64 Magazine* noticed where the savings had been made: the R4300i "only has a 32-bit system interface, to keep down costs", with two caches (16 KB for instructions and 8 KB for data, according to NEC's manual) to keep it busy between trips to memory.

Earlier machines such as the [Commodore 64](/vault/systems/commodore-64) and the [Amiga](/vault/systems/commodore-amiga) also made the processor and the video hardware share memory. On the N64 the sharing is the whole design, and the RCP is the referee.

## The graphics pipeline is a program you choose

The most consequential thing in the manual is that the N64's geometry stage is not fixed. The RSP runs microcode from its own 4 KB of instruction memory, and Nintendo shipped several versions:

> There are three different versions of RSP geometry microcode: gspFast3D,
> gspLine3D, and gspTurbo3D.

Elsewhere the list runs to six: `gspFast3D`, `gspF2DNoN`, `gspLine3D`, `gspTurbo3D`, `gspSuper3D` and `gspSprite2D`. They are not interchangeable. `gspFast3D` is "the optimized, full-featured 3D polygonal geometry microcode". `gspTurbo3D` buys speed by giving things up, and the manual lists exactly what:

> The features not supported by gspTurbo3D are: Clipping, lighting,
> perspective-corrected textures, and matrix stack operations. The quality of the
> anti-aliasing also suffers, due to the lack of precision ... This loss of
> precision can also manifest itself as various visual artifacts, depending on
> the content.

`gspSuper3D` likewise "does not support perspective corrected" textures. So two N64 games can differ in whether their textures are perspective-corrected at all. That was a choice the developer made when building the game, not a property of the console.

The routing was a choice too. The RSP could feed the rasteriser directly, or write its commands to memory for later. Nintendo suggests using the second method to give the two halves of the chip different jobs: "you could use the RSP for audio processing while the RDP is processing commands stored in DRAM." The same processor computed a game's 3D geometry and mixed its sound, so programmers had to schedule the two.

## Four kilobytes of texture

Once the RSP has transformed a triangle, the RDP draws it. Here the N64 did work that earlier consoles left undone: each texture sample is smoothed with "a 4-to-1 bilinear filter", edges are anti-aliased in two passes (once as pixels are drawn, and again by the video interface as the picture goes out), and a z-buffer sorts out which surface is in front. The ninth bit of each byte of Rambus memory exists for this. In the manual's words, the "normally hidden ninth bit is used for antialiasing and z-buffering hardware". That is why Nintendo's 1995 specification quoted the memory as "36Megabits (or 4.5 Megabytes)".

The cost was texture memory. The RDP samples textures only from its own on-chip TMEM, and TMEM holds 4 KB. The manual's advice is blunt: "On-chip texture memory is not large (4 KB). If you are stamping trees in your scene, you should render in texture order." A texture had to be small, or palettised, or loaded again and again from main memory, where each load competed with everything else for the shared bus.

## Developing on a workstation

Development hardware followed the SGI lineage. In 1995, before any target hardware existed, *Edge* reported that "Nintendo's developers have to get to grips with a Silicon Graphics Onyx", a machine far more powerful than the console. They switched features off to approximate it, and one developer said it was not working: "What they are switching off in the Onyx isn't enough."

By 1996 the manual described the finished arrangement: an N64 board on a card inside an SGI Indy workstation.

> The ROM cartridge is replaced by 16 megabytes of RAM, called the ramrom, that
> is accessible from both the Indy workstation over the GIO bus and the RCP over the PBUS

Sixteen megabytes stood in for the cartridge, four times the console's own main memory.

## The cartridge decision

Nintendo's choice of cartridges over CD was argued over in the press from the start, and *Edge* recorded costs that are easy to forget.

**Nintendo capped the size of the first games.** In May 1995 *Edge* reported "Nintendo's insistence that the first games developed for the Ultra 64 be no larger than 100 megabits before compression — a requirement that is causing considerable problems for eager programmers and designers." A hundred megabits is twelve and a half megabytes, against a CD's 650. A developer quoted later that year set the price of that format against the only real precedent: "The Neo-Geo ... had 100 megabit carts available for it, and they were very expensive." The [Neo Geo](/vault/systems/neo-geo)'s cartridges were among the most expensive games ever sold. A pre-launch specification printed by *Edge* listed cartridges of 64 to 512 megabits, that is 8 to 64 MB.

The cartridge was also fast, with no drive to spin up. The manual gives ROM transfer rates of "50 Meg/sec peak, 5 Meg/sec from typical slow ROMs", so the speed of a game's data depended on which ROM chips its publisher paid for.

*Edge* did allow the decision one merit, and it is the argument Nintendo's defenders still make: cartridges meant "developers will be forced to concentrate on game engines and playability rather than trying to fill CDs".

## Delays and launches

The machine was late. It was announced as Project Reality, renamed Ultra 64, and renamed again as Nintendo 64. *Edge* ran "Nintendo's Ultra 64 delayed" as a cover line in July 1995. In February 1996 it reported a Japanese launch set for 21 April at ¥25,000. Then Nintendo moved the date to 23 June, and Nintendo of America blamed "supply limitations on the chips because we are using state-of-the-art technology which only an SGI-licensed foundry can produce".

The Japanese launch was calm by Super Famicom standards. *Edge*'s reporter found queues in Akihabara on the morning of Sunday 23 June that "had disappeared by lunchtime". Nintendo had spread 300,000 machines across convenience stores as well as games shops and taken hundreds of thousands of pre-orders. Retailers reported 250,000 sold on the first day. Almost every buyer also took *[Super Mario 64](/vault/games/super-mario-64)*: most shops would only sell the console together with the game.

America followed at midnight on 29 September 1996. *Edge* reported that the initial allocation of 350,000 sold out within three days. The price was $199.95.

Britain waited until 1 March 1997, and *N64 Magazine*'s first issue put the price plainly: "Even at a bank-account-evacuating £250, the N64 is worth every penny." In the same weeks Nintendo cut the price to ¥16,800 in Japan and $149 in America. *Edge* noted that £250 was "extravagantly expensive" by comparison. Within three months *N64 Magazine* was reporting "the slashing of the Nintendo 64's price from £250 to £150".

## Four players on one screen

The four ports shaped the library. *Edge* previewed *[Mario Kart 64](/vault/games/mario-kart-64)* in early 1996, under the working title "Mario Kart-R", and singled out "the addition of a four-player split screen mode". Rare's *[GoldenEye 007](/vault/games/goldeneye-007)* added "a fourplayer option" before its release in 1997, as *Edge* reported from that year's E3, and Rare's *[Perfect Dark](/vault/games/perfect-dark)* built on it in 2000.

## The Expansion Pak and the 64DD

The 4 MB of main memory could be doubled by a plug-in Expansion Pak. At first it was an option: a mail-order advert in *N64 Magazine* in 2000 marked the games that were "Hi-res RAM expansion compatible". Later games depended on it. Rare's *Donkey Kong 64* came with one in the box, and *Edge*'s review of *Perfect Dark* said the game required "you to invest in a Expansion Pak if you wish to indulge in anything other than a" cut-down version.

The Pak had been meant for a bigger add-on. Nintendo planned a magnetic-disk drive, the 64DD, which sat under the console. It appeared "in near-complete form" at Shoshinkai in November 1996, with disks of about 64 MB, "approx. 32 megabytes" of them writeable. Miyamoto had told *Edge* in 1996 that he was "very interested in the potential of the writable storage device". Then its games moved to cartridge. *Edge* reported in 1997 that the new Zelda, which became *[The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time](/vault/games/legend-of-zelda-ocarina-of-time)*, "was originally slated for a 64DD release". The drive finally went on sale on 1 December 1999, only in Japan and only by subscription. Buyers took a starter kit holding the drive, a modem, an Expansion Pak and a mouse, and paid for it by the month, from ¥1,500. The fee also covered Randnet, Nintendo's online service with Recruit, which *Edge* reported would open with 100,000 memberships. *64 Magazine* wondered who it was for, now that the Pokémon games "originally planned for the DD" had all moved to cartridge.

## Sales and later history

Nintendo's figures give the Nintendo 64 32.93 million sales: 5.54 million in Japan, 20.63 million in the Americas and 6.75 million elsewhere, with 224.97 million games. That was fewer than the [Super Nintendo](/vault/systems/super-nintendo)'s 49.10 million. It was also well behind the [PlayStation](/vault/systems/sony-playstation). *Edge* observed in 1998 that the N64 "sold exceptionally well until last Christmas (especially in the US), when a lack of software enabled PlayStation to firmly cement its lead". Nintendo's ledger shows shipments falling from about 500,000 in the year to March 2001 to about 10,000 the year after, once the GameCube had arrived in September 2001.

N64 games returned in October 2021 as part of the Expansion Pack tier of Nintendo Switch Online.
