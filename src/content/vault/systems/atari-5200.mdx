The **Atari 5200** used graphics and sound hardware related to the [Atari 400 and 800 computers](/vault/systems/atari-8-bit), packaged for cartridge games in 1982. That relationship did not make it a computer without a keyboard, or a machine into which either computer cartridges or [2600](/vault/systems/atari-2600) cartridges could be inserted.

Atari's *CX5200 Field Service Manual* describes the CPU, ANTIC display processing, GTIA graphics and POKEY functions as parts of a particular console design. Software still encountered the 5200's memory map, cartridge arrangement and input devices. Shared chip names are evidence of a hardware family, rather than proof of interchangeable software. ([Original service manual](https://www.atarimania.com/documents/Atari_5200_Field_Service_Manual.pdf).)

## Position rather than four switches

The standard controller used analogue stick inputs alongside a numeric keypad and game-control buttons. Its X and Y potentiometers reported position, unlike a digital joystick's directional switches. That gave software the possibility of responding to how far the stick moved, rather than only whether left, right, up or down was pressed. The [analogue-control](/vault/hardware/analog-control) entry explains the distinction.

Games had to decide what to do with those values. An analogue input did not require every title to map stick displacement to the same speed or movement. *Electronic Games*' December 1982 guide discussed the controller in its hands-on assessment and criticised aspects of its use. Those observations belong to the reviewers' experience; they do not establish a failure rate for all surviving controllers. ([Contemporary review](https://www.vgpavilion.com/mags/1982/12/eg/programmable-videogame-systems/).)

## Revisions and separate accessories

The service manual distinguishes four-port and two-port consoles, including differences in their power and television connections. Those distinctions matter when identifying a complete system. A photograph of one revision should not be treated as an installation guide for the other.

Atari also documented the separate Trak-Ball controller. A [trackball](/vault/hardware/trackball) converts rolling motion into game input; it is not the standard joystick with a different handle. Likewise, the 2600 cartridge adapter was additional hardware, not evidence that an unexpanded 5200 ran those cartridges natively.

The later [Atari 7800](/vault/systems/atari-7800) made older-console compatibility part of the main design. Comparing the two machines makes the distinction concrete: one drew on Atari's computer architecture, while the other retained a separate environment for its established 2600 library.
