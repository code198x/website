The PC Engine was a contradiction: an 8-bit CPU with 16-bit graphics that outperformed both. Tiny HuCard cartridges and a groundbreaking CD-ROM add-on made it Japan's second-place console behind the Famicom. In America, rebranded as TurboGrafx-16, it flopped spectacularly against Nintendo and Sega.

## Fast facts
- **Manufacturer:** NEC, developed with [Hudson Soft](/vault/companies/hudson-soft).
- **Released:** October 1987 (Japan), August 1989 (NA).
- **CPU:** Hudson HuC6280 — an 8-bit CMOS processor with a memory manager and
  the sound hardware on the same die. Run at 7.16 MHz here; the part is rated
  3.58–21.48 MHz.
- **Address space:** 2 MB, via eight mapping registers.
- **Graphics:** HuC6270 video display controller.
- **Colour:** HuC6260 video colour encoder — 512 palette addresses.
- **Sprites:** 1,024 definable, 64 active, **16 per scanline**.
- **Sound:** 6 wavetable channels, on the CPU.
- **Format:** HuCard (credit card-sized cartridges), later [CD-ROM](/vault/hardware/cd-rom).

## Three chips, and a processor that does too much

The PC Engine's reputation is a paradox — "an 8-bit CPU with 16-bit graphics".
Hudson's own manuals dissolve it. The 8-bit part is the ALU, and almost nothing
else about the chip is modest.

The HuC6280 is described as containing "an 8-bit parallel processing ALU, eight
mapping registers for address expansion, a 7-bit interval timer, an 8-bit input
port, an 8-bit output port, and a programmable sound generator (PSG) on a single
chip". Two of those matter enormously.

**Eight mapping registers** give it "2M bytes of address space". A 6502-derived
core normally sees 64 KB; this one carries an MMU, which is why HuCards could
hold far more than a Famicom cartridge without the elaborate mapper chips
Nintendo's carts needed.

**The sound generator is on the processor.** Six channels using "waveform memory
method (32 words for a cycle)" — a 32-sample wavetable at 5 bits per sample, per
channel — with channels 5 and 6 able to switch to "pseudo random noise". The PC
Engine's distinctive thin, glassy timbre comes from that: not FM, not samples,
but short wavetables defined in software.

That division is the reverse of the [Archimedes](/vault/systems/acorn-archimedes),
where the *video* chip generates the sound. Here the CPU does it, and the video
work is split across two dedicated parts: the **HuC6270** draws, and the
**HuC6260** colours.

## The sprite limit, and which sprites survive

The VDC's numbers come in three tiers, and only the last one constrains a frame:

| | |
|---|---|
| Definable in VRAM | **1,024** (with 64K words of VRAM) |
| Registered in the sprite attribute table | **64** |
| On one horizontal scanning line | **16** |

Sprites are 16×16 dots, backgrounds 8×8, and both draw "16 of 256 colors".

The overflow rule is worth stating exactly, because it is the opposite of the
[Neo Geo's](/vault/systems/neo-geo):

> Up to 16 sprites can exist on one horizontal scanning line. If an attempt is
> made to display 17 or more sprites on one horizontal scanning line, the 16
> sprites having higher priority are displayed and the rest not.

Overload a PC Engine line and the *least* important sprites vanish. Overload a
Neo Geo line and, by SNK's own account, "line sprites from the highest priority
will start missing" — the front of the scene goes first. Two machines, two
opposite answers to the same shortage.

## Japanese success
In Japan, the PC Engine thrived:
- **Timing:** Arrived before Mega Drive.
- **Size:** Smallest console, appealing design.
- **Shooters:** Exceptional shoot-'em-up library.
- **Hudson support:** R-Type, Bonk, Bomberman.
- **Market share:** Solid second place.

## American failure
As TurboGrafx-16, everything went wrong:
- **Late arrival:** Launched after Genesis had established.
- **Poor marketing:** Couldn't match Sega's aggression.
- **Name confusion:** "16" suggested 16-bit CPU (it wasn't).
- **Software:** Japanese games didn't always translate.
- **Third parties:** Developers committed elsewhere.

## The CD-ROM revolution
The CD-ROM² attachment (1988) changed gaming:
- **First console CD drive:** Beat Sega CD by three years.
- **Storage:** 540MB versus cartridge kilobytes.
- **Audio:** Redbook CD music, voice acting.
- **Games:** Ys, Castlevania: Rondo of Blood, RPGs flourished.

## Shooter paradise
The PC Engine became the shoot-'em-up platform:
- **R-Type:** Definitive console port.
- **Gradius:** Excellent conversions.
- **Gate of Thunder:** CD-ROM showcase.
- **Lords of Thunder:** Heavy metal fantasy shooter.
- **Soldier series:** Hudson's exclusive shooter franchise.

## Hardware variations
NEC released numerous configurations:
- **PC Engine:** Original Japanese model.
- **CoreGrafx:** Revised, AV output.
- **PC Engine Duo:** Console + CD combined.
- **SuperGrafx:** Enhanced model, five games only.
- **PC Engine GT:** Portable with TV tuner.

## Bonk: The mascot that wasn't
Hudson's caveman mascot tried to compete:
- Solid platformers, charming character.
- Couldn't match Mario or Sonic's appeal.
- Three mainline games plus spin-offs.
- Remembered fondly but not iconic.

## Legacy
The PC Engine proved CD-ROM gaming was viable and showed that a small, well-designed console could compete. Its Japanese library—particularly shooters and CD-ROM RPGs—remains exceptional. The American failure demonstrated that hardware alone can't overcome marketing and timing.
