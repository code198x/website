The MK14 let an owner build a computer and reach straight into its memory. There was no television picture or BASIC prompt in the standard kit. You entered numbers on a small keypad, inspected them on a red LED display and told the processor where to start. The controls were sparse, but the machine exposed the work they did.

Science of Cambridge, the company that later became [Sinclair Research](/vault/companies/sinclair-research), was advertising the kit by March 1978. The headline price was £39.95; with £3.20 VAT and 40p postage, the order form asked for £43.55. Buyers supplied the soldering work. The advertisement listed 256 bytes of RAM and a 512-byte monitor program in permanent memory. ([*Wireless World*, March 1978, p. 113](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/UK/Wireless-World/70s/Wireless-World-1978-03.pdf).)

## Talking to the monitor

Here, a *monitor* means the small program that accepts commands, rather than a screen. The MK14 manual begins its operating exercise by showing how to look at a memory location and change its contents. Addresses identify locations; the numbers stored there can be data or instructions. Hexadecimal notation uses the digits 0–9 and letters A–F to write those numbers compactly. The monitor lets the owner enter a machine-code program and run it. ([*MK14 Manual*](https://archive.org/details/science-of-cambridge-sinclair-mk14-manual), part 1, sections 5–8.)

The processor was National Semiconductor's SC/MP II. The basic RAM could be extended on the board with another 256 bytes and a RAM/input-output device containing a further 128 bytes: 640 bytes altogether. Those input and output connections also made the board useful for controlling external equipment. A cassette interface and a television display required additional hardware. ([A. A. Berk, *Practical Electronics*, May 1979](https://rk.nvg.ntnu.no/sinclair/computers/mk14/mk14_pe0579.htm).)

## Landing with numbers

The manual's *Moon Landing* turns the LED readout into a spacecraft's instrument panel. It displays altitude, descent rate and remaining fuel. Keys 1–7 choose the thrust; stronger thrust spends fuel faster. The player has to reach the surface gently, and the final display reports the result. No drawn landscape is needed to make that trade-off playable. The same applications section includes *Duck Shoot*, *Mastermind* and music programs. (Part 2, “Games” and “Music”.)

The application programs are credited to David Johnson-Davies, except for individually marked contributions. His name connects this small training board to [Acornsoft](/vault/companies/acornsoft) and the [Acorn Atom](/vault/systems/acorn-atom), whose manual also made programming examples central to learning the machine. The games here were listings to enter, rather than a ready-loaded collection.

## Cheap hardware still needed support

Berk's May 1979 review welcomed the low price but found the manual difficult for beginners in places. He criticised the conductive-rubber keypad and described receiving his own kit in January after ordering the previous summer. That is evidence of one owner's experience, not a delivery time for every order. He also described revised boards and optional switches that addressed some of the shortcomings. ([Review](https://rk.nvg.ntnu.no/sinclair/computers/mk14/mk14_pe0579.htm).)

The [ZX80](/vault/systems/sinclair-zx80), sold in 1980, offered a different starting point: a case, a television display and BASIC. The MK14 had already put a cheaper, more exposed machine into customers' hands before [Clive Sinclair](/vault/people/clive-sinclair) left Radionics in 1979. It belongs to the earlier kit business, rather than the aftermath of that departure.
