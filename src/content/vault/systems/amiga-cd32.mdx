The **Amiga CD32** put an [Amiga](/vault/systems/commodore-amiga) architecture into a games console with a top-loading CD drive. Commodore’s 1993 service documentation describes a streamlined A1200-derived motherboard: a 14MHz Motorola 68EC020, the AGA graphics chipset and 2MB of Chip RAM. The console arrived with a controller and television connections, rather than the keyboard and floppy drive around which an Amiga computer was normally used.

Its double-speed [CD-ROM](/vault/hardware/cd-rom) drive changed what could be distributed in the box. It did not turn the disc’s storage into working RAM. Graphics, sound samples and program data still had to fit in memory when the game used them. ([Commodore’s service addendum, introduction and chapter 4](https://amiga.net.au/files/Tech_Amiga/Commodore_AmigaCD32_Engineering_Change_Release_1993-07-20_ocr.pdf).)

## An Amiga with a different front door

A July 1993 FidoNet message, reproduced in *Amiga Report*, relayed the British magazine *Amiga Format*’s early description. It listed the processor, AGA, 2MB of RAM and the custom chip controlling the CD drive. It also passed on a long list of forthcoming games. That list records the expectations around the machine; an announced title is not evidence that the promised edition shipped.

The same report quoted programmer [Toby Simpson](/vault/people/toby-simpson) describing *Diggers*: disc space allowed more graphics and CD audio tracks. The practical opportunity was room for more material, with a console interface built around playing it. ([Contemporary report](https://bigbookofamigahardware.com/amigareport/www.amigareport.com/ar120/p1-3.html).)

## What Akiko did

**Akiko** joined the processor, custom chips and CD mechanism, and assisted conversion from chunky pixels to the Amiga’s planar display format. In chunky storage, the information for one pixel is grouped together; in planar storage, its bits are spread across separate planes. Conversion changes the arrangement of the data. It does not itself draw a 3D scene or replace the game’s rendering code.

## Built in and optional

The service guide lists four-channel Amiga sound and audio-CD playback separately. It also distinguishes the standard console from planned computer-expansion hardware and an optional full-motion-video module. An ordinary CD32 did not acquire those expansion capabilities because its manual described them.
