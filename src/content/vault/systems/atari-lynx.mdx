The **Atari Lynx** was the first handheld with a backlit colour display, released in 1989 alongside the Game Boy. Featuring hardware sprite scaling and impressive graphics, it was technically superior but its size, battery consumption, and price prevented success against Nintendo's simpler, cheaper device.

## Fast facts

- **Released:** 1989
- **CPU:** 65C02 @ 4 MHz + custom chips
- **Screen:** 160×102, 16 colours on screen from a palette of 4096
- **Innovation:** First colour handheld, hardware scaling
- **Price:** $179 at launch (unverified); cut to $100 by mid-1991

## 4096 colours, sixteen at a time

The palette figure was the Lynx's headline and it is the one most often
misreported. RAZE put the correction in print in 1991: the machine "has a 4096
colour palette and can display 16 colours onscreen."

Sixteen is four bits, and the arithmetic closes. The same page gives the screen
as "160 [pixels] wide by 102 lines high" and the framebuffer as "8160 bytes" —
and 160 × 102 ÷ 2 is exactly 8,160. Two figures printed a paragraph apart confirm
each other, and confirm four bits per pixel.

There was a way past sixteen. "It can do better than that and display 16 colours
on every horizontal line" — reload the palette between scanlines and each row
gets its own sixteen. The cost is the reason nobody shipped action games using
it: the trick "slows the Lynx considerably making the mode unsuitable for high
speed games."

This is the same bargain the [Copper](/vault/hardware/copper) strikes on the
Amiga and badlines strike on the C64 — more colour bought with time the display
was going to spend anyway — except that on a handheld running a 4 MHz CPU there
is no spare time to spend.

## ComLynx is a serial port called RedEye

The networking feature has a plainer description than its marketing suggests. It
"is simply a bidirectional serial communication channel", running "at various
speeds from 300.5 baud to 62.5 Kbaud (around 7K a second)" — and inside Atari it
was called RedEye, which says what it physically is.

Cartridges of the period held "up to 256K of information. Paltry really." Adding
save RAM was possible and priced out of reach: a cartridge with "a save game
option (that is, built in RAM) would cost at least £50."

## Why it failed

*Compute!* ran the three colour handhelds against the Game Boy in August 1991,
and the comparison is unkind in a specific way. By then Atari had "dropped the
price to $100. That's only ten bucks more than Game Boy" — the price gap the Lynx
is usually said to have lost on had already closed, and it still lost.

What had not closed was everything else. The screen "looks washed out": "even in
a darkened airplane, Lynx's screen can't match that of Game Gear or
TurboExpress." The Game Boy managed "24 or more hours before you need to replace
the four AA cell batteries", against a Lynx that ran a colour backlight. And the
library had not arrived: "you'd think a system sold since 1989 would offer more
than 16 games", set against a Game Boy roster of "over 60 titles" with Tetris in
the box.

Three million Game Boys sold in eighteen months. The Lynx was better hardware
being beaten on every axis a person actually holds.

## Legacy

The Lynx demonstrated that technical excellence doesn't win markets. However, its hardware innovations—scaling, rotation, networking—influenced future handheld design.
