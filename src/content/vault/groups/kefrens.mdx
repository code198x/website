Amiga coders still talk about "Kefrens bars": a wall of vertical bars that twist and weave down the screen, each one seeming to stand in front of the last. The name comes from a Danish group that spent the late 1980s turning out numbered megademos, went quiet, and then in April 1993 won the Amiga demo competition at The Gathering in Norway with *[Desert Dream](/vault/demos/desert-dream)*. *Amiga Format* introduced them that summer as "a long-standing demo group who don't often release work – but when they do... you can bet that it's impressive."

## Fast facts

- **From:** Denmark.
- **Active:** from 1987 as The Starlight Project, as Kefrens from 1988; the last productions listed for the group date from December 1993.
- **Platforms:** Amiga, with a couple of Commodore 64 demos in 1989.
- **Best known for:** *[Desert Dream](/vault/demos/desert-dream)*, first at [The Gathering](/vault/events/the-gathering) 1993; the megademo series; the effect named after them.
- **Members included:** Razmo, Metallion and Mellica (founders), Promax, Laxity, Vention, R.W.O. and the musician [Jesper Kyd](/vault/people/jesper-kyd).

## Megademos

According to the scene database Demozoo, Kefrens began in 1987 as The Starlight Project, founded by Razmo, Metallion and Mellica. A demo from June 1988 announced the change: "TSP is now called Kefrens". The group's early output was the usual mix for an Amiga group of the time: intros, cracktros, compilation packs and a run of numbered megademos, each a disk of separate demo parts by different members.

Those disks travelled through [public-domain](/vault/distribution/public-domain) libraries. In July 1989 *ST Amiga Format*'s Demos Corner waded through "the usual collection of mega-copper-blitter-nutter demos" and chose a best disk of the month. On it, "Probably the most visually impressive demo was the 'Kefrens Psychoball' demo which employs sampled effects, large multi-colour blitter objects and very smooth scrolling." Demozoo credits *Psychoball*'s code and graphics to Promax, with music by Nightlight.

*Megademo VIII* followed in February 1990, released at a party Kefrens held with the group Dexion in Nykøbing Falster, Denmark. Its credits, as Demozoo lists them, show how many hands a megademo took: code by Gizwiz, ICronite, Mellica, Metallion and Promax, graphics by Box, Milkshake and The Whiz Kid, and music by Jesper Kyd and Nightlight among others. Promax also wrote tools; Demozoo credits him and Detron with the code of the assembler Asm-One under the Kefrens name.

## Quiet years and a comeback

After 1990 the output slowed. One 1991 intro was titled *Death of Kefrens – Where Are They Now?*. A smaller team of coders Laxity and Vention, graphic artist R.W.O. and musician S.L.L. made *Guardian Dragon* in 1991, *Egregious*, which they entered at [The Party](/vault/events/the-party) in Denmark that December, and *Guardian Dragon II* in 1992.

In 1993 the group was, in the words of the Italian *Amiga Magazine*, an old group "revived for the occasion". *Desert Dream*, coded and scored by Laxity with graphics by R.W.O. and others, won the Amiga demo competition at The Gathering in Lillestrøm in April. Seven weeks later, at The Computer Crossroads in Gothenburg, Sweden, Kefrens' *D.A.N.E.* came fifth in the Amiga demo competition and R.W.O. came second in the graphics competition. British public-domain libraries sold both demos. In September 1993 one of them, Visage Computers, told *Amiga Format* that *Desert Dream* and *D.A.N.E.* were its first and third most popular demos of the month.

A few more productions followed that year, among them *Ankh in Popland* for the new AGA Amigas. The public databases list nothing from the group after December 1993.

## Kefrens bars

Kefrens bars are a wall of vertical bars: each bar is a narrow coloured band with its own shading, and a column of them snakes down the screen in a curve, every bar partly hiding the ones above it. The group's name stuck to the effect, and demo coders use it for any version on any machine. Public sources do not establish which demo showed it first.

The usual way to make it on an Amiga needs almost no screen memory. Instead of a full bitmap, the program keeps one line of pixels and shows it on every line of the display. It then draws a bar into that line, at a new horizontal position, as each line of the screen is drawn. Nothing is ever erased during the frame, so each line of the picture shows every bar drawn so far, with the newest one on top. Down the screen the bars pile up into the familiar twisting wall.

The hardware that makes the repeated line possible is the bitplane modulo. Commodore's *Hardware Reference Manual* explains that when the display reaches the end of a line, "the modulo is added to the bitplane pointers", so that the next line's data is fetched from the right place. With a normal screen the modulo is 0, and the pointers run on through memory. Set it to minus the length of one line, in bytes, and the pointers go back to the start of the same line every time. Keeping a change in step with every line of the display is the kind of job the Amiga's [copper](/vault/hardware/copper) exists for: it waits for the beam to reach a line and writes the custom chips' registers there, while the processor or the [blitter](/vault/hardware/blitter) does the drawing.
