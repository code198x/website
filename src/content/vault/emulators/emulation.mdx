In May 1997 *Edge* told its readers that "anyone with a moderately fast PC and a few hundred megabytes of hard-drive space can be the curator of their own 20,000-game living museum, containing perfect replications of around 30 old computers and consoles (ie practically all of them), and over 50 coin-op games." The software doing the replicating was written by hobbyists and given away. *Edge* thought that mattered more than nostalgia: "emulation isn't (mostly) actually about nostalgia at all. It's about history."

An emulator is a program that behaves like another machine's hardware so closely that software written for that machine runs on it unchanged. The old program does not know the difference. This entry covers how emulators work, how they spread from the early 1990s, the court cases of 1999 and 2000, and where the law has settled.

## How an emulator works

Chris Hardy, who wrote Windows 95 emulators of the arcade games *Phoenix* and *Pleiades*, explained the method to *Edge* in 1997: "it's not necessary to actually know anything about how the arcade machine works internally. As long as the programmer emulates the hardware so that the game thinks it's running on a real machine, then the game will run exactly like the real thing. You don't have to worry about what to do with the sprites, what's important is that the emulator knows."

Old machines are slow, but that does not make them easy to imitate. The programmer Lee Taylor gave *Edge* the example of *[Galaxian](/vault/games/galaxian)*. The arcade board had both a character display and a sprite display: aliens in formation were characters, and an attacking alien was deleted from character memory and redrawn as a sprite in exactly the same place. A PC has one bitmapped screen, so its emulator has to reproduce both displays in software. Generally, *Edge* was told, the older the machine, the more tricks its hardware designers and programmers had used, and every trick is something the emulator has to copy.

There are several ways to run the old machine's program:

- **Interpretation.** The emulator reads each instruction of the old processor, looks up what it does and does it. Simon Goodwin, writing in *Amiga Format* in 1999, described interpreting emulators that "must laboriously look up code for each Z80 instruction".
- **Dynamic recompilation.** The emulator translates blocks of old code into the host's own machine code once and reuses the translation. Goodwin named the catch: "Programs, unlike books, can change themselves as they go along. A useful compiling emulator must check for this, or else it will carry on running the old code after it's been replaced." The cost is memory for the translated code.
- **High-level emulation.** Instead of copying every part of the hardware, the emulator recognises what the game is asking for and does it directly. UltraHLE, whose name comes from the technique, ran Nintendo 64 games in 1999 by replacing the library routines the games called, and used the PC's 3Dfx graphics card to draw the result.
- **Cycle accuracy.** The emulator reproduces the timing of every part of the machine, clock cycle by clock cycle, so that tricks relying on exact timing work too. It is slow. Richard Bannister, who converted emulators to the Macintosh, told *Retro Gamer* in 2005 that "traditional emulators often take shortcuts to run at acceptable speeds on today's hardware", and that the cycle-exact bsnes "only attains full speed on the fastest Macs Apple has produced, but the end result is as accurate as you can get". See [cycle accuracy](/vault/techniques/cycle-accuracy).

## The early 1990s

Emulators of the 8-bit home computers were already circulating by 1991. In May of that year an advertisement for an Amiga public-domain library in the first issue of *Amiga Shopper* listed a "Spectrum Emulator", a "C64 Emulator" and an "ST Emulator" among its disks.

On the PC, by 1994, Gary Penn could write in *PC Gamer* that "thanks to this one piece of hardware and two pieces of software" it was "possible to play all the games from one old format (the Sinclair Spectrum) and most of the games from another (the Commodore 64)". He also reported the first trouble: "bulletin boards and other Shareware libraries have been forced to remove such emulators, along with the ported-over games, from their catalogues, because technically they contravene the original software's copyright." Penn argued that since he still owned the machines and the tapes, the copies on his hard disc were back-ups for personal use.

## The 1997 wave

By 1997 the PC was fast enough for most of the 8-bit era, and the internet made emulators easy to share. *Edge* spent two issues surveying eighteen classic machines and the emulators for each. For the Commodore 64 it named "the big two", PC64 and C64S: PC64 cost £15 to register, while C64S cost "a rather uncomfortable $60". It called the Spectrum scene "possibly the liveliest one around", centred on the newsgroup comp.sys.sinclair, with an archive "fed by a constant stream of games, often by their original authors". For the NES the new favourite was NESticle, "apparently knocked together for fun in about three weeks" by a team called Bloodlust Software. Emulators were small enough to carry on a floppy disk. The latest *Space Invaders* emulator, *Edge* noted, took up "a mind-bogglingly tiny 3K".

The same year brought [MAME](/vault/emulators/mame), released in February 1997 by Nicola Salmoria, which gathered arcade emulation into one program. The emulators that grew into today's standard tools for single machines have their own entries: [VICE](/vault/emulators/vice) for Commodore's 8-bit machines, [Fuse](/vault/emulators/fuse) for the Spectrum, [WinUAE](/vault/emulators/winuae) and [FS-UAE](/vault/emulators/fs-uae) for the Amiga, and [Mesen](/vault/emulators/mesen) and [FCEUX](/vault/emulators/fceux) for the NES.

## 1999: the console makers respond

Emulating a machine that was no longer sold had been tolerated. Early in 1999 three programs emulated consoles that were still in the shops, and the owners objected.

**UltraHLE.** Released on 28 January 1999 by two programmers calling themselves Epsilon and RealityMan, UltraHLE ran *Super Mario 64* and *The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time* on a [Nintendo 64](/vault/systems/nintendo-64) emulator for Windows that was only about 220K compressed. Its authors took their site down within hours, said they had written it "to see if it could be done", and declared the project discontinued, but copies had already spread. Nintendo's Beth Llewelyn told *Edge*: "They obviously had to circumvent our security chip. It promotes continued piracy." To *PC Zone* she said Nintendo was "very disturbed" that the authors had distributed "a product designed solely to play infringing copies of copyrighted works", and that it was "taking measures to further protect and enforce our intellectual property rights which, of course, includes the bringing of legal action." *Edge* admired UltraHLE "as a piece of coding" but found "the issues and complexities that it introduces are less palatable".

**Connectix Virtual Game Station.** Connectix showed a [PlayStation](/vault/systems/sony-playstation) emulator for the Macintosh at Macworld in January 1999 and sold it for $50. Unlike UltraHLE it played the original game discs. Sony sued in the federal court in San Francisco on 27 January 1999. The court first refused Sony a temporary restraining order, and Connectix's chief executive, Roy McDonald, told *Edge*: "Reverse engineering is not illegal." In spring 1999 the court granted Sony a preliminary injunction. Connectix had to stop shipping the program and stop work on products using Sony's BIOS, the PlayStation's built-in system code, which it had copied repeatedly while working out how it behaved.

Connectix appealed. The US Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit reversed the injunction in its ruling of 10 February 2000. It held that "intermediate copies made and used by Connectix during the course of its reverse engineering of the Sony BIOS were protected fair use, necessary to permit Connectix to make its non-infringing Virtual Game Station function with PlayStation games", and found no evidence for Sony's trademark claim. A year later Sony bought the Virtual Game Station from Connectix, and the product was withdrawn. *Computer Gaming World* reported that "with the acquisition, Sony now owns all core assets related to the Virtual Game Station".

**bleem!** A small company, bleem!, released a PlayStation emulator for Windows PCs in spring 1999, sold for download from its website. *PC Zone* reported 600,000 hits on the site's first day and 150,000 downloads of the demo. Sony failed to get a temporary restraining order against it too, and *Edge* reported that the finished program shipped after 500,000 demo downloads. Sony's case went on, alleging that bleem! had infringed its copyright in the BIOS. Bleem! filed counterclaims, some over Sony's efforts to have its stand removed from the E3 trade show in May 1999. After the Connectix ruling, bleem!'s Sean Kauppinen told *APC*: "The biggest thing it [the Connectix case] does is set a judicial precedent for us." In May 2000 the company announced bleemcast!, which ran PlayStation games on Sega's [Dreamcast](/vault/systems/sega-dreamcast); it closed in 2001. Looking back in 2004, *Computer Gaming World* wrote that Sony had "sued Bleem and Connectix into submission a few years back".

The Vault's entry on [Sony v. Connectix](/vault/culture/sony-vs-connectix) covers the case in more detail.

## Where the law settled

The period cases drew a line that still holds in outline, though the details depend on the country:

- **The emulator itself.** In the United States, writing an emulator by reverse engineering was ruled fair use in *Sony v. Connectix*. *Edge* put it plainly in 2002: "It is completely legal to create an emulation program but there's still a grey area when it comes to playing a game using one." *APC* warned in 2000 that Australian copyright law gave reverse engineering much narrower protection, so the same emulator might not be safe there.
- **System ROMs.** Many machines need their original firmware to run, and that firmware is copyrighted. Some owners licensed it. UAE, the Amiga emulator, "refuses to work without a valid image of the Amiga Kickstart ROM", and in 2000 Cloanto was selling a licensed Kickstart with UAE as *Amiga Forever*. Amstrad allowed the CPC's ROMs to be distributed with emulators as long as its copyright message went with them.
- **Games.** The games are copyrighted too. Sharing copies of them, and of system ROMs, without permission brought much of the legal action, from the shareware libraries of 1994 to the fan site Dave's Classics, which Sony sued in 1999 for distributing the PlayStation BIOS.

In 2006 the Internet Archive won the renewal of an exemption from the US Digital Millennium Copyright Act that lets libraries and archives break the copy protection on obsolete software to preserve it (see [Internet Archive](/vault/communities/internet-archive)).

## Beyond playing

Emulators changed how old software is studied and played. Save states and frame-by-frame control made [tool-assisted speedruns](/vault/culture/tool-assisted-speedrun) possible. In 2004 *Retro Gamer* described a much-shared *Super Mario Bros. 3* video that had been assembled from save states and several sessions, which many viewers took for a real run (see [speedrunning](/vault/communities/speedrunning)). Front ends such as [RetroArch](/vault/emulators/retroarch) put many emulators behind one interface. The [Internet Archive](/vault/communities/internet-archive) runs old computers inside a web browser, and FPGA systems such as the [MiSTer](/vault/hardware/mister-fpga) recreate the hardware itself in programmable logic. The Vault's [emulation techniques](/vault/techniques/emulators) entry goes further into the methods.
