In July 1984 *Sinclair User* told its readers about "the latest upset in the industry". In March a company called Pulsonic had launched Spectrum and Commodore games at "an absurdly low £2.95". Then Mastertronic went "one better", "breaking the £2 barrier" with a range of games "at an unprecedented £1.99 each", and Atlantis Software matched it at four new games a month. In the same issue, HMV's price list had full-price Spectrum games at £5.50 to £7.95, and *The Hobbit* at £14.95.

The newcomers had not come from software. Pulsonic belonged to Warwick Leisure, which made the budget record albums sold in Woolworth; two of Mastertronic's directors owned a video wholesaler; Atlantis was run by a distributor of horror films on video. All of them, *Sinclair User* said, had decided that games "have been over-priced and consequently have not achieved volume sales". They called their products "pocket-money software", and sold them where pocket money was spent: "newsagents, garage forecourts, supermarkets and off-licenses."

## Fast facts

- **Began:** March 1984 (Pulsonic, £2.95), with Mastertronic's £1.99 range soon after.
- **Price points:** £1.99 for most of the 1980s; £2.99 for many labels from 1986 onwards.
- **Where sold:** Newsagents, garages, supermarkets and motorway service stations as well as computer shops.
- **Main machines:** Spectrum, Commodore 64 and Amstrad CPC cassettes; Amiga re-releases from 1990.
- **Major labels:** [Mastertronic](/vault/companies/mastertronic), [Firebird](/vault/companies/firebird) Silver, [Code Masters](/vault/companies/codemasters); later [Kixx](/vault/companies/kixx), Encore and The Hit Squad for re-releases.

## "They hate us"

The established industry did not welcome it. Derek Meakin, head of the National Micro Centres, warned in April 1984 that "a price-cutting war could well bankrupt the more vulnerable ones", that freelance authors would not spend months on a program for "meagre royalties", and that "standards will slide rapidly". [Imagine](/vault/companies/imagine-software) had announced a price cut that March and then withdrawn it "to safeguard the growth of the software industry". Asked how the older publishers felt about Mastertronic, its chairman Frank Herman said: "They hate us."

*Sinclair User* had some sympathy with both sides. Most of the first cut-price games were, "to be blunt, fairly dire": "tired variations on well-worn themes", with titles such as *Spectipede*, *Gnasher* and *Monster Munch*. But it thought the losers would be publishers who had been selling "sub-standard software at exorbitant prices". "Standards will not drop because standards were never that high to begin with, only the prices were."

Buyers took to it. Within three weeks of launch, Mastertronic claimed sales of more than 150,000 games, "more than some software houses sell in a year". In July 1985 it claimed audited sales of two million games in its first twelve months.

## Why budget needed volume

In August 1985 *Sinclair User* printed a costing for a typical **full-price** game at £6.95. The magazine called its figures "speculative" and said they applied to "a small company with few overheads".

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Retail price | £6.95 |
| Sold to the distributor for | £3.82 |
| Left to the software house | £3.13 |
| Blank cassette, duplicating and inlay | 50p |
| Artwork and advertising | £1.00 |
| Bulk delivery to distributors | about 5p |
| Royalty to the author | 40p |
| Administration, telephone and overheads | 30p |
| **Profit to the software house** | **88p** |

The magazine printed the cassette line as "fifty per cent"; its total of 88p only works if it meant 50p. Distributors, it explained, took an average 55% discount, and authors' royalties were usually worked out on what was left, not on the shop price.

A £1.99 game could not carry £1.00 of artwork and advertising. It had to sell in far larger numbers, and the successful ones did. The same article reported that *Booty* had sold more than 100,000 copies for Firebird, and that Mastertronic's best seller, *Chiller*, had sold more than 100,000 on the Commodore 64 and 30,000 on the Spectrum.

## How games got published

Budget publishers needed a steady flow of new games, and most arrived in the post. Mastertronic told *Sinclair User* it looked at around 200 submissions a month; in May 1985 it had seen 220 and picked two, "one which was almost marketable and one requiring more development". It published four to six games a month. John Maxwell of Mastertronic explained the pace:

> We can only spend ten minutes on each and many come in without instructions or any form of documentation and so cannot be evaluated properly.

The magazine warned that anyone sending an adventure should include a map, because without one it "would be impossible to evaluate within the 10 minutes available".

For a new author, budget was the easier way in. Mike Cohen of Lothlorien said that the full-price market had become "more critical", while "budget software surged forward and youngsters got their games accepted because of the budget ranges". *Sinclair User* agreed that "it is easier to get your first attempts accepted by a budget software house", and named Mastertronic and Firebird's Silver range. James Leavey of Firebird called budget "an opportunity for new software writers to enter the market".

Not everyone was persuaded. [Andrew Hewson](/vault/people/andrew-hewson) of [Hewson Consultants](/vault/companies/hewson-consultants) "feels strongly against pocket money software", the article said: "We're not interested in the budget range because of the aura associated with it." A good game, he thought, deserved a reasonable price. Two years later Hewson launched a budget label of its own, Rack-It, at £2.99.

## The labels

Firebird, owned by British Telecom, launched its Silver range in November 1984 with twenty games at £2.50; by mid-1986 Silver games cost £1.99, and by 1988 the label was called Silverbird. Mastertronic added labels: Mastertronic Added Dimension (MAD) at £2.99 from 1986, Entertainment USA for American games and Bulldog at £1.99. In 1986 the Darling brothers, whose company had supplied more than half of Mastertronic's first 30 games, left to found Code Masters, also at £1.99. *CRASH* reported late in 1987 that Code Masters claimed to have doubled its share of the budget market, from 6.3% to 12.8%, between August and October that year.

Quality varied widely. *CRASH*'s Spectrum scores for Mastertronic's £1.99 games ran from 94% for *Knight Tyme* to 21% for *1985*. Firebird's *Booty* scored 93%; its Silver *Pneumatic Hammers* 18%. On the Commodore 64, *ZZAP!64* gave Mastertronic's *Kikstart II* 86%, and Code Masters' *BMX Simulator* started a long series of *Simulator* games, while the Oliver twins' *[Dizzy](/vault/games/dizzy)* became its best-known character.

## Selling full-price games again

Budget prices also gave old full-price games a second life. The idea was there from the start: in 1984 a video distributor's label, Advanced Computer Entertainment (no relation to the later magazine *ACE*), cut ten Commodore games from £6.90 to £2.99, the only one of *Sinclair User*'s four cut-price publishers "to have lowered the price of existing software". Firebird said in 1985 that it might take on full-price games that had "exhausted the market at that level".

From the end of 1987 the big publishers did it with labels of their own:

| Label | Owner | Launched | Price | Early titles |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ricochet | Mastertronic | 1987 | £1.99 | Ultimate's *[Knight Lore](/vault/games/knight-lore)* and *Alien 8* |
| Encore | [Elite](/vault/companies/elite-systems) | 1988 | £1.99 | *Frank Bruno's Boxing*, *Airwolf*, *Bombjack* |
| [Kixx](/vault/companies/kixx) | [U.S. Gold](/vault/companies/us-gold) | 1988 | £2.99 | *Gauntlet*, *Metrocross* |
| The Hit Squad | [Ocean](/vault/companies/ocean-software) | 1989 | £2.99 | *Green Beret*, *Rambo* |

Elite promised in 1988 that its full-price games would not appear on Encore until three years after their first release. Kixx, formed by former Mastertronic staff, planned 24 titles in its first year from the U.S. Gold and [Gremlin](/vault/companies/gremlin-graphics) back catalogues, about a third of them new games. *ZZAP!64*'s reviewer thought Ocean's *Rambo* was "the sort of game which rerelease labels were made for". In 1990 Ocean launched a 16-bit Hit Squad for the Amiga, at £7.99, with *Wizball*, *Super Hang-On*, *R-Type* and *Batman: The Caped Crusader*.

By then £2.99 was a common budget price. *ACE*'s guide to football games in February 1990 listed the original *Football Manager* at £2.99 on cassette, next to *Tracksuit Manager* at £9.99.
