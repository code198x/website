Not everyone could afford £9.99. Budget labels like Mastertronic, Codemasters, and Kixx offered games at £1.99-£4.99, making gaming accessible to those priced out of full-price releases. Quality varied wildly—some budget titles rivalled premium games, while others justified their low price. The format proved gaming didn't require premium pricing.

## Fast facts

- **Price point:** £1.99-£4.99 (vs £9.99+ full price).
- **Peak:** Mid to late 1980s.
- **Markets:** UK, Europe primarily.
- **Mix:** Original games and rereleases.

## Why budget needed volume

Set the costing below against the sales figures and the model explains itself. A
**full-price** £6.95 game, on *Sinclair User*'s speculative arithmetic, left its
publisher **88 pence**. Everything above that went on the distributor's cut, the
cassette, duplication, artwork, advertising and the author's royalty.

At £1.99 there is no room to shrink any of those except by selling far more
copies. Which is what the budget labels did: the same article reports *Booty*
past 100,000 units for Firebird, and Mastertronic's best seller past 100,000 on
the Commodore 64 with another 30,000 on the Spectrum.

Budget was not a cheaper version of the full-price business. It was a different
one, run on volume.

## What a game cost to sell

*Sinclair User* published a full costing in August 1985, for a game priced at
**£6.95** — full price, not budget. The magazine described the figures as
speculative and applying to "a small company with few overheads".

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Retail price | £6.95 |
| Sold to distributor for | £3.82 |
| Leaving the software house | £3.13 to cover everything |
| Cassette, duplicating and inlay | half of that |
| Artwork and advertising | £1.00 |
| Bulk delivery to distributors | about 5p |
| **Royalty to the author** | **40p** |
| Administration, telephone, overheads | 30p |
| **Profit to the software house** | **88p** |

Read that table and the budget label stops looking like generosity and starts
looking like arithmetic. A **£1.99** game cannot carry £1.00 of artwork and
advertising. Something has to go, and what went was the marketing, the
packaging and the evaluation time — replaced by volume.

## The volume model
Mastertronic's John Maxwell told the same feature how the other side worked. The
company processed around **200 submissions a month** — 220 in May 1985 — and
published **four to six**. From that month's 220, **two** were picked: one almost
marketable, one needing more work. At any time 40 or 50 people had games in
progress.

> We can only spend ten minutes on each and many come in without instructions or
> any form of documentation and so cannot be evaluated properly.

Ten minutes per submission is not carelessness; it is the only rate at which
200 a month can be read at all. It also explains the standing advice in the
adventure columns to send a map with an adventure — without one, a game could
not be assessed inside the window it was going to get.

The volume paid off when it worked. *Booty* sold over **100,000 units** for
[Firebird](/vault/companies/firebird), and Mastertronic's best seller *Chiller*
passed 100,000 on the Commodore 64 and 30,000 on the Spectrum.

## The on-ramp, and the people who disliked it
The budget labels became the way in for new programmers at precisely the moment
the full-price market closed. Mike Cohen of Lothlorien put the shift plainly:

> Up to a year ago, we were being sent games which we could put out. The market
> then became more critical and a higher standard is now demanded. Meanwhile,
> budget software surged forward and youngsters got their games accepted because
> of the budget ranges.

*Sinclair User*'s own advice followed from it: it is easier to get a first game
accepted by a budget house than a full-price one, and it named
**Mastertronic** and **Firebird's Silver range** as the two to try.

Not everyone approved. [Andrew Hewson](/vault/people/andrew-hewson) of
[Hewson Consultants](/vault/companies/hewson-consultants) was against the whole
idea — "We're not interested in the budget range because of the aura associated
with it" — on the grounds that a good game deserved a reasonable price. It is a
real industry disagreement, recorded at the time, and both positions are
defensible: Hewson is defending author earnings, and at 40p a copy on a £6.95
game he had a point.
