An Amiga owner reading *The One* in April 1992 could ask **17-Bit Software** for disk **1604** and receive [Voyage](/vault/demos/voyage). The magazine described its spacecraft, robots, starfields, animated block-man and music. A production from a demo competition in Denmark had become something a British reader could order by number.

The Wakefield library distributed demos, music, animations and utilities. It supplied a route between the people making those works and owners who did not attend a [demo party](/vault/communities/demo-parties) or exchange disks with their creators.

## Finding something worth ordering

A catalogue number made an order precise. A magazine review helped explain whether the disk sounded interesting, while a library chart showed what some other customers were choosing.

*Amiga Format*’s August 1992 chart placed [Hardwired](/vault/demos/hardwired) and **Odyssey** above the production it listed as *Voyager*, disk 1604. These are the library’s reported sales positions. They tell us how one dealer presented its stock, rather than how every Amiga owner ranked the demos. (*Amiga Format* 37, p. 186.)

The system also meant that a work could acquire several useful identifiers: its title, its creators and a dealer’s disk number. Those numbers belonged to a particular catalogue, so a number from 17-Bit was not automatically meaningful at another library.

## Music could introduce its maker

[Allister Brimble](/vault/people/allister-brimble) later told *Amiga Format* that he sent tunes to 17-Bit and that the library published them on an early disk. When the associated people moved into commercial game development as [Team17](/vault/companies/team17), they already knew his music.

That is a participant’s account of one career route. It shows what distribution could do beyond supplying a file: it could make a contributor’s work familiar to somebody who might later commission more of it.

## A library and a studio

17-Bit’s library role deserves to be kept visible beside Team17’s games. Selecting, describing, copying and posting software was a different job from building a commercial game.

The wider [public-domain library trade](/vault/distribution/public-domain) explains the terminology. “PD” was often used for software that could be copied under its author’s terms, including works which still had copyright. The copying service and the permissions attached to each work were separate matters.
