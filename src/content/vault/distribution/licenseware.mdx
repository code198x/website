**Licenseware** was a distribution model primarily on the Amiga where PD (public domain) libraries sold games and software at low prices (£2-5) with royalties paid to the original authors. It created a middle ground between truly free PD software and commercial publishing, enabling amateur developers to earn money without securing a traditional publishing deal.

## How the period defined it

*Amiga Shopper*, September 1991, positions licenseware by what it is not:

> Yet another way of obtaining cheap software is to buy 'licenseware'. This is
> not freely copyable, but instead of having fancy packaging and oversized boxes,
> disks are distributed with the documentation as 'read.me' [files]

Not free like PD, not boxed like commercial. And it was a formal enough category
that a development system's licence named it: AMOS required an acknowledgement if
you released "into the public domain or as shareware or licenseware", with
separate terms again if "you are selling the program commercially". Three tiers,
distinguished in a contract.

**One caveat on curation.** The same column's reviewer found a licenseware
program with "a few bugs in it" — of two review copies, "one Gurued virtually at
once" — and another that "refused to run". A royalty to the author did not
imply testing.

- **Price:** £2-5 typically

## Legacy

Licenseware anticipated modern indie distribution—platforms where anyone could sell their work without traditional gatekeepers, at prices they controlled, with transparent royalty splits. It was itch.io thirty years early.
