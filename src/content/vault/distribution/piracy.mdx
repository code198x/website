Home computers made copying a matter of a few keystrokes. A few keystrokes duplicated software that cost weeks of pocket money. The result was an underground economy of copied games, organised cracking groups, and endless industry-pirate conflict. For many users, piracy was how software spread—the ethical debates came later, if at all.

## Fast facts

- **Era:** 1980s-present.
- **Methods:** Disk copying, crackers, BBS.
- **Response:** Copy protection arms race.
- **Culture:** Cracktros, warez scene.

## 1988–89: the argument gets a date

The entry's era line — "1980s–present" — flattens a turn that happened in
British computing across about eighteen months, and both sides of it are visible
in the magazines.

**The trade body's campaign.** By May 1989 the Federation Against Software Theft
was buying full-page advertising in the games press:

> It's no game any more. For every game you copy you are depriving programmers
> of their livelihood.

Note what it appeals to. Not the law, not the publisher's balance sheet, but the
individual programmer's living — in a market where the programmer was often a
teenager whose name readers knew from the loading screen.

**The legislation.** The Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 is what stands
behind that confidence, and its effect reached beyond copiers to the tools. The
maker of the [Multiface](/vault/hardware/multiface) told its own customers that
"because of possible implications of the coming new Copyright Act the MULTIFACE
may soon be NO LONGER SOLD in the UK", and ran a closing-down discount on the
strength of it.

A hardware manufacturer expecting to be legislated out of its home market dates
the shift more precisely than any account of changing attitudes.
