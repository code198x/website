In 2007 Valve's Gabe Newell explained the appeal of making games like television. Producing a series in instalments would let developers take more risks, he told *Edge*, because "if you fail, you've just failed for a week rather than four years." Valve had already released *Half-Life 2: Episode One*, the first of three planned follow-ups to its 2004 shooter. The third was never made.

That gap between promise and delivery runs through the history of episodic games. Selling a game in parts, on a schedule, promised players a lower price, a story to follow over months and something to talk about between instalments. It promised developers money while they were still working and a chance to change course. It also bound them to deadlines that big games rarely meet.

## Fast facts

- **The idea:** release a game in instalments over months, each sold separately or as a "season".
- **Before it:** shareware episodes, sold together once the first had been tried.
- **Early attempts:** Capcom's *Eldorado Gate* (Dreamcast, Japan, from 2000); Valve's *Half-Life 2* episodes (2006–07); Ritual's *SiN Episodes* (2006).
- **Best known for it:** [Telltale Games](/vault/companies/telltale), from *Sam & Max* (2006) to *The Walking Dead* (2012).
- **Later examples:** *[Life is Strange](/vault/games/life-is-strange)* (2015); *[Hitman](/vault/games/hitman)* (2016).

## Shareware's episodes

The word came from [shareware](/vault/distribution/shareware). Apogee and id Software split their games into episodes, gave the first away and sold the rest. In 1993 Apogee's advertisements in *Computer Gaming World* offered the first episode of *Wolfenstein 3D*, "Escape from Wolfenstein", for $4 shipping or as a free download; "Episodes two through six are sold separately". The episodes were finished before the first was given away, though. A player who liked the free one could order all the rest at once. Nothing arrived on a schedule.

## Chapters every two months

One of the first games planned to come out over time was Japanese. In May 2000 *Edge* previewed Capcom's *Eldorado Gate*, a role-playing game for the Dreamcast with character designs by Yoshitaka Amano. Capcom would release seven chapters, one every two months, at ¥2,800 each, and planned two more sets of seven to finish the story in 2004. [Yoshiki Okamoto](/vault/people/yoshiki-okamoto) had written the first six chapters and would write the seventh only after he had seen players' reactions. *Edge* called it "an early realisation of a future form of videogaming", but did not expect it to reach Britain in that form: "the western marketing and retail machine is simply not up to the task." The chapters did appear in Japan; Brazil's *Super GamePower* listed the second for December 2000 and the third for the following February.

## Television's schedule

The idea came back in the West once games could be sold online. In 2004 [Telltale](/vault/companies/telltale), founded by former LucasArts staff, told *Edge* it would sell adventures in episodes, each standing alone but with storylines running across a series like "a well-structured sitcom", and would treat its website and partners as "virtual TV channels where they can 'tune in' to the latest episodes".

[Valve](/vault/companies/valve) used [Steam](/vault/distribution/steam) to try the same thing with a shooter. In October 2005 Newell told *Computer Gaming World* that Valve planned an episode "about once every three months", with "seasonal box sets" in the shops afterwards, and compared the plan to television. *Half-Life 2: Episode One*, once called *Aftermath*, came out in 2006, a six-hour game at £20 in the shops. *Edge* liked it but found it a "peculiar way to start a trilogy". *Episode Two* followed in 2007, packaged with *Portal* and *Team Fortress 2* in *The Orange Box*.

Valve also sold other developers' episodes. Ritual Entertainment's *SiN Episodes: Emergence* came out on Steam in May 2006, the first part of a planned series. *Edge* gave it six out of ten and wrote that "Sin Episodes promised us part one of an epic, but we're in danger of getting a generic formula refined over several iterations". No second episode was released. In 2007 Ritual was bought by MumboJumbo, a maker of casual games.

Publishers were warier. Remedy's *Alan Wake* was at first meant to appear on Xbox Live Arcade as two-hour episodes, one a week for ten weeks, but Microsoft decided otherwise, and *Edge* reported that "the publisher's conservatism eventually trumped innovation". The game came out whole in 2010, though it kept the cliffhangers and "previously on" recaps of a television series.

## Telltale's seasons

Telltale made episodes its business. Its first *Sam & Max* episode, *Culture Shock*, came out in the autumn of 2006 for $9, the start of a season planned at one episode a month. *Tales of Monkey Island* came in five episodes from July to December 2009.

*The Walking Dead*, in five episodes during 2012, showed what a season could do for a story. Players made hard choices about who to save, and Telltale collected their decisions and showed each player how everyone else had chosen. By the spring of 2013 *Edge* counted more than 8.5 million episodes downloaded and more than 80 game of the year awards. Dontnod's *[Life is Strange](/vault/games/life-is-strange)* followed the same pattern in 2015, five episodes from January to October; *Wireframe* later thought the episodic release was "part of the original Life Is Strange game's charm". IO Interactive released *[Hitman](/vault/games/hitman)* (2016) one location at a time, and *Wireframe* judged that "an episodic approach to Hitman worked in its favour".

## The schedule problem

Keeping to a timetable proved hard. By early 2014 *Edge* worried that Telltale was "being stretched too thin": the second episode of *The Wolf Among Us*, promised within one to two months of the first, had slipped into the new year. Telltale grew quickly on licensed series, and in September 2018 laid off almost all of its staff, leaving *The Walking Dead: The Final Season* to be finished by Skybound Games. The [Telltale](/vault/companies/telltale) entry tells that story.

Valve explained its own retreat in 2020, after the release of *Half-Life: Alyx*. The level designer Dario Casali told IGN, as *Wireframe* reported, that the team kept adding more to each episode "and then we realised these episodes are turning more into sequels". After *Episode Two*, he said, "Maybe this episodes thing, it was a good concept, but we're not executing [it] terribly well as far as getting things out quickly enough." There was no *Episode Three*.
