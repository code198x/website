Before digital downloads, magazines brought software to readers. Cover-mounted disks—later CDs—transformed computing publications from pure text into software delivery systems. Demos introduced players to new games, public domain software built communities, and the occasional full game justified the cover price alone.

## Who made them

A cover disk was not something the magazine ran off in the office. *The One* printed
its supply chain on the Coverdisk page: "Coverdisks compiled by Grants Computing",
with faults going to a separate address entirely — "The One Coverdisk Returns, PC
Wise, Dowlais Top Business Park, Dowlais, Merthyr Tydfil" in August 1993.

Eighteen months later the address had changed to Diskxpress in Bourton-on-the-Water.
Duplication was a contract, and contracts moved.

The editorial office was emphatic about the division. Readers with a bad disk got
"humble apologies, but PLEASE DON'T SEND THEM TO US! Instead, send them to this
address with a SAE for 28p" — the magazine's own capitals. Issue 59 puts it more
bluntly: "any coverdisk problems sent to us will remain unanswered."

## The 16-bit coverdisk

The disk became central on the Amiga and Atari ST, where it had room the cassette
never had — 880KB on an Amiga disk, 720KB on an ST one.

| | Cover disks | Cover tapes |
|---|---|---|
| Storage | Roughly ten times a tape's practical payload | Limited |
| Access | Random, seconds | Sequential, minutes |
| Demos | Larger, closer to the finished game | Simpler |
| Count | Multiple disks per issue, later | One tape |

Magazines competed on them. *Amiga Format* mounted multiple disks and covered
the widest range; *Amiga Power* curated; *CU Amiga* leaned on games. The disk was
a reason to pick one magazine off the shelf over another.

## What fitted on one disk

⚠ **The "full commercial game on the cover" story is a later and rarer thing than
it sounds.** In May 1993 *Amiga Format* was still answering the question in the
negative:

> What? You don't get the whole game on the Coverdisk? The reason is the size —
> the full game almost completely fills a disk.

The disk that month carried "only a three level demo of the full 30 level
shareware game", with "instructions on where to send the £5 shareware fee...
built into the game." The magazine's own framing was that this made it normal, not
mean: "So you only [get] a taste of the full game, just like proper, commercial
software."

Nor was the disk guaranteed to run. The compatibility note is candid: "All the
contents have been tested on all Amigas, but if you have a slightly weird one
(2Mb of chip RAM on a 1.3 machine, or all four floppy drives) you may have to
disconnect your expansions to get the Coverdisk to work."

And the standing advice, in the magazine's capitals: "BACK UP YOUR COVERDISK
IMMEDIATELY!!! ... [MAKE] SURE YOU WRITE-PROTECT YOUR DISK BEFORE USE!" This was
a working disk, expected to be written to and ruined.

## A market for amateur software

The coverdisk was not only a channel publishers pushed demos down. It bought
original work from readers, at rates worth naming. *Amiga Format*, December 1990:

> UP TO £800 UP FOR GRABS! [T]hink you've got what it takes to write for [the]
> Amiga Format Coverdisk? ... We're after Workbench [ha]cks, demos, utilities,
> games, game [con]tests... You could earn up to £800!

For an amateur Amiga programmer, that was a publishing route with a cheque at the
end of it — closer to the type-in listing economy than to a publisher's
promotional demo, and running on the same disk.

## Preservation

Because the disks were pressed in magazine numbers and kept by readers, they hold
material that survives nowhere else: demos of games that shipped differently or
not at all, one-off utilities, competition entries, and public domain software
that had no other distribution. A coverdisk archive is a snapshot of what a
platform's amateur scene was doing in a given month.

## Fast facts

- **Era:** 1985-2005 (peak); central on the Amiga and ST from 1987.
- **Media:** Floppy disks (880KB Amiga, 720KB ST), then CDs.
- **Content:** Demos, utilities, full games.
- **Impact:** Major distribution channel.
