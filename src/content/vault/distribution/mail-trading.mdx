Before the internet, there was the post office. Mail traders copied software onto floppy disks and mailed them across continents. Contact lists circulated in magazines and on disks. A package from Germany might take two weeks but contained treasures unavailable locally. This postal network distributed the demoscene, spread games, and built global friendships.

## Send your list for mine

The exchange did not open with software. It opened with an inventory, and the
phrasing is near-identical across unconnected advertisers in one 1989 issue of
*Your Sinclair*:

> I have over 600 games to swop. Many recent titles. Send your list for mine.

> I have over 1,200 games to swop with you. Send your list for mine.

> Do you want to swop games with me? If you do then send a list to...

A list cost a stamp and revealed what you had without giving it away. Only after
the lists crossed did disks or tapes move. The numbers traders advertised ran
high — 350, 600, 1,200, "over 1600 games to swop incl Typhoon, mostly new" — and
should be read as claims in a classified ad rather than audited libraries.

Some traded originals and made that a term of the deal: "Originals only. My five
for your..." — a named-title exchange at a stated rate, which is a different
transaction from bulk copying.

## The magazine was the directory

There was no membership and no gatekeeper. You printed your full postal address
in a national magazine and waited. Trading ads sat inline with equipment
for-sale ads and pen-pal ads in the same column, unremarked.

That adjacency is not incidental. The channel was social before it was technical,
and the ads refuse to separate the two — "Pen pals wanted to swop games, music.
Have over 600 games to swop", or, two issues earlier, "Do you like heavy metal? If
you do then write a letter to me so we can swop music tapes." A few lines away, a
twelve-year-old seeks a pen pal "who has a Spectrum computer. Interests include
badminton, Speccys, soccer and Neighbours." Software moved along a friendship
network, not a distribution one.

*Your Sinclair* page in 1989 carries British traders alongside Hugo Diogo in

user wants to swap software with people all over the world." British readers were
writing abroad through the small ads from the mid-1980s.
