Some sums come out exactly. Six divided by two is three, and nothing is left over.

Most do not. Seven divided by two is where the trouble starts, and every program has to
decide what to do about it.

## Division can keep the fraction

Ask for it plainly and you get the fraction:

```text
SHOW 7 / 2
```

<Output>{`3.5`}</Output>

That is often what you want: half a second or a position between pixels.
This pseudocode keeps the fraction. Real languages and machines have their own
division rules and finite precision; we will name those when we use them.

Sometimes you need whole things: sealed bags, players in a team or tiles in a row.
Then you want to know how many fit and what is left behind.

## How many whole ones fit

`ROUND DOWN` gives the greatest whole number no larger than the input:

```text
SHOW ROUND DOWN (7 / 2)
```

<Output>{`3`}</Output>

Three whole twos fit into seven. The brackets say *work the division out first*, which is
the rule from Unit 1 earning its keep.

For positive values, that looks like dropping the fractional digits. Negative values
show the difference: `ROUND DOWN (-7 / 2)` is **-4**, not -3. On a number line,
-4 is below -3.5; -3 is above it. Dropping the fraction would move towards zero.

## What is left over

For this introduction, use a non-negative whole number of things and a positive
whole number to divide by. `REMAINDER` gives the part that did not fit:

```text
SHOW 7 REMAINDER 2
```

<Output>{`1`}</Output>

Seven sweets shared between two children: three each, one left in the bag. `ROUND DOWN`
gives the three, `REMAINDER` gives the one.

The two answers together account for everything:

```text
original = whole groups * group size + remainder
7 = 3 * 2 + 1
```

The remainder is at least zero and smaller than the positive group size.
We have not defined a rule for negative remainder inputs here; check the rule
of the language you use.

## A remainder of nothing

When a number divides exactly, nothing is left:

```text
SHOW 8 REMAINDER 2
SHOW 9 REMAINDER 2
```

<Output>{`0
1`}</Output>

Eight is even, so halving it leaves nothing behind. Nine is odd, so one is stranded.

A remainder of 0 against 2 identifies an even whole number. Testing a counter
against 4 identifies multiples of four, including zero; where the counter starts
decides when that first event happens.

For a non-negative position on a 32-column screen, `position REMAINDER 32` gives
a wrapped column from 0 through 31: 31 stays 31, 32 becomes 0 and 33 becomes 1.
Use the remainder value as the new column. Testing whether it equals zero only
identifies exact multiples of 32. Negative positions need a stated rule too.

## Rounding to the nearest

`ROUND DOWN` always goes down. `ROUND` goes to whichever whole number is nearer:

```text
SHOW ROUND 3.2
SHOW ROUND 3.7
```

<Output>{`3
4`}</Output>

Use `ROUND` when you want the closest answer, and `ROUND DOWN` when you want to know how
many whole ones fit. They are different questions. For the non-negative values in these examples, we choose the higher whole number
when exactly halfway: `ROUND (7 / 2)` is **4**. Other languages may use another
halfway rule. We have not chosen a rule for negative halfway values here.

<details>
<summary>Do 7 / 2, ROUND DOWN (7 / 2), 7 REMAINDER 2 and ROUND (7 / 2) answer the same question?</summary>

No. The results are **3.5**, **3**, **1** and **4**. They give the full quotient,
the number of whole groups, the leftover and the nearest whole number under our
stated halfway rule. Three groups of two plus one leftover account for all seven.

</details>

## When it's wrong, see why

- **You lost a fraction you wanted.** `ROUND DOWN` discards it on purpose. If you needed
  3.5, do not round at all.
- **Everything came out one too small.** You rounded down where you wanted the nearest.
  `ROUND DOWN 3.7` is 3, and `ROUND 3.7` is 4.
- **The remainder was bigger than you expected.** With our non-negative whole inputs
  and positive divisor, the remainder is smaller than the divisor. If it is not, check which number went where: `7 REMAINDER 2` and
  `2 REMAINDER 7` are different questions.
- **You divided by zero.** Division by zero has no defined result in these examples. A program
  can reject that input before dividing; check that the divisor is positive here.

## What you've learnt

- **`/`** keeps a fractional quotient in this notation; target division rules may differ.
- **`ROUND DOWN`** goes to the greatest whole number no larger than the input.
- **`REMAINDER`** gives the part left over for our non-negative whole inputs and positive divisor.
- Together those two account for the original number.
- A **remainder of 0** means it divided exactly, which can identify multiples of two or four.
- **`ROUND`** goes to the nearest whole number, which is a different question again.
