## What this is

A **byte** holds eight bits. A program can read those bits as an unsigned number,
a character code or part of a picture. Here we change selected bits, shift the
row, and compare two ways to represent fractions.

## Who it's for

You will use binary place values, small sums and yes/no comparisons. We recall
the relevant rules as we use them. [Counting in Twos](/foundations/counting-in-twos/)
offers more practice with bytes and their readings;
[Decisions](/foundations/decisions/) explains joined questions. The bit tools
and question-joining words have different jobs, which the first unit separates.
Prior attendance is not required. BASIC and assembly remain independent entry points.

## The shape

- **Switch by switch** — `BITAND`, `BITOR`, `BITXOR` and `BITNOT`, and what a mask is.
- **Slide the whole row** — logical shifts within a chosen width, when they match
  arithmetic, and why speed depends on the processor and instructions.
- **A letter is a number** — character codes, the difference between ASCII `"7"`
  and the number 7, and the range checks that make a case-changing mask useful.
- **Numbers that aren't whole** — binary floating-point: some fractions fit exactly,
  while others need rounding within the format’s precision and range.
- **Nailing the point down** — fixed-point: agree a scale, read the stored value,
  and choose precision, range and rounding for the job.

A system track explains its language’s operations and representation rules. Use
these ideas alongside a real program, or return when a game gives you a reason
to inspect its bits. [Gates and the Adder](/foundations/gates-and-the-adder/)
is an optional look at a Boolean model of arithmetic beneath the instructions.
