Unit 4 showed a flexible representation with finite precision. Another option is
**fixed-point**: agree on one scale and store a whole number of those smaller steps.
That can simplify the arithmetic, but it does not make every fraction exact.

## Agree on a scale

Pick a scale, say **256**, and agree that each stored unit means one *256th*.
For this example, use a **sixteen-bit unsigned integer**, from 0 to 65535.
That gives us two bytes to hold both whole and fractional parts.

Then 1.5 is not stored as "1.5" at all. It is stored as 1.5 × 256, which is **384**. That is an
ordinary whole number.

To read the real value back, divide by the agreed scale:

```text
SHOW 384 / 256
```

<Output>{`1.5`}</Output>

The point is not floating. It is fixed at "divide by 256", and everyone who touches the value
agrees to it.

The fraction is being carried as a **whole number**. That is the trick, and it is also the name:
**fixed-point**.

## Adding at one scale

When two values share a scale, their stored integers can be added directly.
The answer keeps that scale, provided it still fits in the chosen width.

Add 1.5 and 0.5. Store both scaled, as 384 and 128, then add the integers:

```text
SHOW 384 + 128
```

<Output>{`512`}</Output>

512 is 2 at our scale, so 1.5 + 0.5 came to 2 using nothing but whole-number addition.

## Reading the value is not dropping the fraction

A power-of-two scale makes the split visible. At scale 256, the high byte
counts whole units and the low byte counts 256ths. For 384, those bytes are 1 and
128: one whole plus 128/256, or **1.5**.

A right shift by eight keeps only the whole part: **1**. It does not decode the
full value 1.5. The discarded low byte held the fraction.

Multiplying an integer by 256 can be expressed as a left shift by eight if the
result fits. That does not mean the full operation of reading a fractional
value is the same as an integer right shift.

<details>
<summary>At scale 256, does shifting stored 384 right by eight give 1.5?</summary>

No. It gives the whole part, 1. Reading the full stored value means 384/256 = 1.5.
The low byte, 128, accounts for the half that a right shift removes.

</details>

## What it costs you

Fixed-point makes several choices explicit.

**You remember the scale.** The machine sees 384 and thinks 384. Only you know it means 1.5.

**You keep the scales matching.** Every value in a sum has to share one scale, or the answer is
nonsense.

**You choose how to round.** At scale 256, 0.1 would need a stored integer of
25.6. If we round to the nearest integer, we store 26, which means 26/256 =
0.1015625. A half fits exactly; a tenth does not at this scale.

The scale need not be a power of two. At scale 10, a tenth is exactly one stored
unit. Pick the scale and rounding rule for the job. Operations such as multiplying
two scaled values also need care with the resulting scale and intermediate range.

## Choosing the scale

The scale decides what you get and what you give up.

A bigger scale buys finer fractions. At ×256 you can hold 256ths, which is far finer than a pixel.

It costs range. Our largest stored integer is 65535, so at scale 256 the largest
value is 65535/256 = **255.99609375**. More fractional steps mean fewer whole units
in the same storage. Choose the width and scale together.

Matching-scale integer addition can be cheaper than software floating-point on a
Z80. That is a reason to consider fixed-point, not a guarantee that it is best for
every game. Check the precision, range and work needed on the target machine.

For another explicit conversion from fractions to whole numbers, see
[Meet BASIC’s secret-number expression](/systems/sinclair-zx-spectrum/basic/meet-basic/unit-07-choose-a-new-secret/).
Its table explains how `INT` removes the fractional part; you can read that section

## When it's wrong, see why

- **A value printed as a big whole number**, like 384 rather than 1.5. You forgot to divide by the
  scale. The machine only ever sees integers.
- **Two values fought.** Adding a ×256 number to a ×16 number gives nonsense. Everything in a sum
  must share the **same scale**.
- **The numbers ran out of room.** A scale eats range, so a value that fitted as a plain integer
  may not fit once scaled.
- **A fraction vanished.** Scaled values are still whole numbers, so values between the steps of
  your scale need rounding. Check the chosen rule and allowed error.

## What you've learnt

- **Fixed-point** stores a fraction as a **whole number** at an agreed scale.
- **Addition at one scale** uses integer addition, while the result fits.
- Extracting the **whole part** with a shift is different from reading the full fraction.
- You carry the bookkeeping: remembering the scale, and keeping every value in a sum on the same
  one.
- Its **precision, range and rounding** depend on the chosen width and scale.
- Its speed depends on the **target and operations**, so measure the work that matters.

## Where this leaves you

That is the primer. You can look at a number the way the machine does, whole or fractional,
positive or negative, in bits or in hex, and you can mask it, shift it and scale it.

From here the road goes down into a real machine, where hex addresses, bit flags, signed offsets
and fixed-point are not background facts. They are the language the code is written in.
