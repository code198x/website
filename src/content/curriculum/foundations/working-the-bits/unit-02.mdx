One move left. The bit logic in Unit 1 changed which switches were on. This tool does not care
which are on. It slides the **whole row** sideways, every bit shuffling one place over
together.

It is called **shifting**, and the interesting part is what the sliding does to the number.

Here we use **eight-bit unsigned values**, from 0 to 255. A shift keeps eight bits,
puts 0 in each newly empty position, and drops bits that leave the row. This is a
**logical shift**. Those rules matter when a value is near the ends of the range.

## Slide left, and the number doubles

Push every bit one place to the **left**. Each switch moves into the next place up, and a 0
drifts in to fill the gap on the right.

Every place is worth twice the one to its right. So moving every bit up one place makes the
whole number worth **twice as much**, provided the result still fits in eight bits.

Take 5, which is `00000101`. Slide it left and it becomes `00001010`, which is 10:

```text
SHOW 5 SHIFTLEFT 1
```

<Output>{`10`}</Output>

The number after `SHIFTLEFT` is how many places to slide. One place, one doubling.

## Each slide is another doubling

Shift left again and you double again. Slide by three places and you have doubled three times
over, which is times **eight**.

While the result fits, a shift left by *n* multiplies by 2 to the power *n*.
One place is ×2, two places ×4, three places ×8:

```text
SHOW 5 SHIFTLEFT 3
```

<Output>{`40`}</Output>

Three slides: `00000101` becomes `00101000`, and 5 times eight is 40.

But 200 is `11001000`. Shift it left once and the stored byte becomes `10010000`,
which is 144, not 400. The top 1 no longer fits in the byte. A processor may save
that shifted-out bit separately: the Z80 copies it to its carry flag.

## Slide right, and it halves

Slide the row the other way and every bit drops to a lower place. A **0 fills the top position**.

There is a catch. The bit on the far right has nowhere to go, so it **falls off the end**. A
right shift is a divide by two that throws away any remainder, which is exactly what
whole-number division does for these unsigned values.

```text
SHOW 5 SHIFTRIGHT 1
```

<Output>{`2`}</Output>

Not 2.5. `00000101` became `00000010`, and the bottom 1 fell off. That lost bit is the
remainder. The bit is outside the result byte; the Z80 can retain it in the carry flag.

This is not a rule for every signed right shift. Read `10000000` as signed −128:
a zero-fill right shift makes `01000000`, which is +64, not −64. An **arithmetic
right shift** keeps the top bit’s old value instead, producing `11000000`, or −64.
Check which operation your language or processor supplies.

## Why the machine uses it

Some older processors, including the Z80, have no general multiply instruction.
Shifts can help build scaling operations from simpler instructions.

The quickest choice depends on the processor, the value’s width and the instructions
used. On a Z80, `ADD A,A` doubles the value in register A in four clock periods;
`SLA A` shifts it left in eight. Their result bytes agree, but some flags differ.
A shift is a useful tool, not a promise of the fastest code.

## When it's wrong, see why

- **A left-shifted number came out far too small.** Bits slid off the **top** of the stored byte.
  Shift too far and the value overflows its byte.
- **A right shift lost part of the answer.** It did, on purpose. The bottom bit falls off, so a
  right shift discards the remainder. 5 ÷ 2 gives 2, not 2.5.
- **`× 2` did not match a shift.** For our unsigned values, left shift agrees with multiplication
  only while the result fits. Check the stored width before replacing arithmetic with a shift.

## What you've learnt

- **Shifting** slides every bit one place at once, the whole row together.
- **Left** is **×2** while the result fits; a shift by *n* places scales by 2ⁿ under that condition.
- Our **zero-fill right shift** divides an unsigned value by two, dropping the remainder.
- A signed **arithmetic right shift** preserves the old top bit instead of filling with zero.
- Speed depends on the **processor and instructions**, not just the name of the operation.
