Decimal uses ten digits. Binary uses just two. The place-value idea stays the
same: a digit's position tells us what it is worth.

## Ten is a habit

We count in tens. Three hundred and sixty-five is 3 hundreds, 6 tens and 5 ones. Each place
is worth ten times the place to its right.

Decimal is a familiar choice, but it is not the only one. Nothing about the
numbers requires groups of ten.

## The machine has two

A **bit** is a binary digit: **0** or **1**. We draw those two possibilities as
a switch, off or on.

That is a model for the information. Electronic circuits represent the digits
with physical states: the Spectrum manual describes low and high voltage. The
bit is the information; the voltage is how a circuit represents it.

One bit cannot say much. To count past one the machine does what you do when you run out of
fingers on one hand. It uses more.

## Counting past one

Line up several bits and give each one a place. Each place has two states rather than ten,
so each place is worth **twice** the one to its right. The places run 1, 2, 4, 8, 16,
doubling as you go left.

Read the switches **1 0 1**. That is one 4, no 2, and one 1. Together they make **5**.

Write `BIN` in front of a row of switches to write a number the machine's way:

```text
SHOW BIN 101
```

<Output>{`5`}</Output>

The pattern *is* the number. `BIN` does no sum you could not do yourself. It reads the
switches the machine's way and shows you the answer in yours.

## Turn another switch on

You can watch place value work. Add a switch on the left, in the **8s** place:

```text
SHOW BIN 1101
```

<Output>{`13`}</Output>

Five became thirteen. The new switch sits in the 8s place, so it added eight. Every switch
is worth its own place and nothing else.

## Work back from a number

To write 45 in binary, try the places from largest to smallest. We do not need
128 or 64. Take 32, leaving 13; take 8, leaving 5; take 4, leaving 1; then take 1.

```text
Places: 128 64 32 16 8 4 2 1
Bits:     0  0  1  0 1 1 0 1
```

So 45 is `BIN 00101101`: 32 + 8 + 4 + 1. Check a conversion by adding the
selected places back together. We wrote eight digits to show an eight-bit pattern.

## Eight of them make a byte

Bits rarely travel alone. The machine handles them in eights, and eight bits have a name you
will meet on every page from here. They are a **byte**.

How high can eight switches count? Turn them all on:

```text
SHOW BIN 11111111
```

<Output>{`255`}</Output>

128, 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. Add those up and you get **255**. Eight bits give
**256 patterns**, including all zeros. Read as **unsigned** whole numbers, with
no minus sign, they cover 0 through 255. A different reading can give the same
bits another meaning; Unit 4 introduces a signed reading.

`BIN` marks how we write a number, not how much memory we reserve. `BIN 101`
and `BIN 00000101` both mean 5. Leading zeros help us show a chosen width, but
do not change the value or allocate storage.

<details>
<summary>Does BIN 00000101 hold a bigger number than BIN 101? Can BIN 100000000 fit in one unsigned byte?</summary>

Both first patterns mean **5**. The second question has a different answer:
`BIN 100000000` is **256**, which needs nine binary digits and does not fit
in our eight-bit unsigned field. A number being valid notation does not make
it fit every storage choice. What a real program does with an out-of-range
value is a separate rule to check.

</details>

## When it's wrong, see why

- **A digit that isn't 0 or 1.** A switch has two states, so those are the only digits `BIN`
  takes. A `2` in the row is not a binary number.
- **The number is far bigger or smaller than you expected.** You may be reading the row
  backwards. The rightmost switch is the 1s, and the places grow as you move left.
- **A leading zero did nothing.** That is correct. `BIN 0101` and `BIN 101` are the same
  number, the way 0007 and 7 are.

## What you've learnt

- We count in **tens** out of habit, not because numbers demand it.
- A **bit** is a binary digit, 0 or 1; the switch is our picture of it.
- Each place is worth **twice** the one to its right, so a row of switches spells an ordinary
  number.
- Eight bits make a **byte**: 256 patterns, read here as unsigned 0 to **255**.
- **`BIN`** marks binary notation; leading zeros change its written width, not its value.
