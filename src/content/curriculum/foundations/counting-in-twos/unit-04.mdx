Every number so far has been zero or up. Programs go down too. A score drops, a player moves
left, a countdown runs out. They need **negative** numbers.

Here the machine has a real problem. It has no minus sign. It has switches, and a switch is
only ever on or off. So how do you write **−1** with nothing but bits?

The answer is called **two's complement**.

## Borrow a switch for the sign

The machine cannot add a minus symbol, so it gives up half its range and spends it on
negatives. A **signed** byte runs **−128 to +127** instead of 0 to 255. That is the same 256
patterns, split so half of them count below zero.

The **top switch** does the dividing. Off, and the number is zero or positive. On, and the
number is negative. We call that top bit the **sign bit**.

## Count backwards off the end

Here is the trick itself. What is one less than zero?

Start at `00000000` and tick down by one. You fall off the bottom and land at the top:
`11111111`, every switch on. So **−1 is the all-ones byte**. Read as a plain unsigned number,
that same pattern is 255.

The rule behind it: the pattern for **−n** is **256 − n**. For −1 that is 255:

```text
SHOW 256 - 1
```

<Output>{`255`}</Output>

The same switches carry two meanings. `11111111` is **255** read plainly and **−1** read
signed. The bits do not change. The reading does.

## The switches for minus five

Take −5. By the rule its pattern is 256 − 5, which is 251:

```text
SHOW BIN 11111011
```

<Output>{`251`}</Output>

**Read as a signed byte**, the top switch is on, so this is negative. Read unsigned,
it is still 251. The interpretation decides.

## Why bother? Addition just works

This looks like a strange way to write negatives, until you add one. Take 5 and add −5's
pattern, 251:

```text
SHOW 5 + 251
```

<Output>{`256`}</Output>

5 + 251 comes to 256, and 256 is `1 00000000`. That is a one followed by eight zeros.

If we keep the result in **one byte**, only the low eight bits fit: `00000000`,
which is zero. So 5 + (−5) = 0, worked out by addition. The `SHOW` above gives the
full sum, 256; it does not automatically throw away bits.

The extra 1 is a **carry** out of the byte. A processor can save it separately:
the Z80 has a carry flag for just that purpose. It is outside the result byte,
rather than necessarily lost.

That is the point of two's complement: adding a negative pattern and keeping the
low eight bits gives the subtraction result. The same addition method can do both jobs.

## The seam

Splitting the range leaves a join. Count up through the positives to **127** (`01111111`) and
add one more. The pattern becomes `10000000`, whose top switch is now on.

Read signed, that is not 128. It is **−128**. Push one past the biggest positive and you land
on the most negative. This is **signed overflow**: the mathematical answer, +128,
is outside the signed byte’s range. There was no carry out of the byte: the unsigned
sum, 128, still fits. Carry and signed overflow answer different questions.

<details>
<summary>For a one-byte result, does 127 + 1 carry out of the byte? Does it overflow signed?</summary>

There is no carry: 128 fits in an unsigned byte. There is signed overflow: +128
does not fit in −128 to +127. The stored bits are `10000000`, read as −128 when signed.
Compare 5 + 251 above: it has a carry, but +5 plus −5 has no signed overflow.

</details>

## When it's wrong, see why

- **The number is about 256 away from what you expected**, like −1 showing as 255. The byte is
  being read unsigned when you meant it signed, or the other way round. The bits are right and
  the reading is not.
- **A positive turned into a huge negative.** You crossed the seam and wrapped around it.
  Check whether the value could have run off the end of its range.
- **Which bit is the sign?** When reading a two’s-complement signed byte, it is the
  top one. Read unsigned, that same bit is worth 128.

## What you've learnt

- The machine writes negatives with **two's complement**. In a byte the pattern for −n is
  **256 − n**, so −1 is `11111111`.
- In a **signed two’s-complement byte**, the top bit is the sign; on means negative.
- The same switches mean a positive or a negative. The **reading** decides.
- Adding a negative pattern and keeping the **low eight bits** gives the subtraction result.
- **Carry** out of a byte and **signed overflow** are different conditions.
- There is a **seam**: counting past +127 wraps to −128.
