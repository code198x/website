The half adder produces a carry and cannot accept one. A higher column may
receive a carry, so we need a third input. For an ordinary sum of two numbers,
we start the rightmost column with carry in **0**. A full adder can accept a
different starting carry when the operation calls for it.

Call it **carry in**. Three inputs now: `A`, `B` and the carry arriving from the column to
the right.

## Three inputs, eight rows

Three questions make eight cases, as *Truth Tables* promised. Here is the whole behaviour:

Two things to notice in that grid.

**The sum column is on whenever an odd number of inputs are on.** One, or all three. That is
`XOR` again, applied to three wires instead of two.

**The carry column is on whenever at least two inputs are on.** A carry happens when the
column overflows, and with three bits arriving it overflows the moment any two of them are 1.

For every row, check the same relationship:

```text
A + B + Cin = SUM + 2 * COUT
```

With all three inputs 1, the left side is 3 and the right is 1 + 2 × 1 = 3.
The two output bits retain the whole answer for this column.

## Building it

Take it in two steps, because a full adder is two half adders with their carries collected.

First add `A` and `B`, exactly as before. Then add the carry in to that result. Each of those
additions can throw a carry, and either one is enough to carry into the next column, so an
`OR` collects them.

## Chaining them

One full adder handles one column. To add whole numbers, use one per bit and hand each
carry out to the next adder's carry in.

Each box stands for the five-gate construction above. The overview shows the
connections; its displayed values start at zero and are not interactive. The
full-adder controls above let you test one column.

This is a **ripple-carry adder**, and the name describes what happens. A carry out of the
rightmost column becomes the carry in of the next, which may produce a carry of its own, and
so on leftwards. The carry **ripples** along the chain.

## Follow a carry through three columns

Add binary `111` and `001`: seven plus one. Start on the right, at bit 0,
with no incoming carry.

| Bit position | A | B | Carry in | Sum bit | Carry out |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 (ones) | 1 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 1 |
| 1 (twos) | 1 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| 2 (fours) | 1 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 |

The carry from each row becomes the next row's carry in. Read the three sum
bits in the opposite order, S2 S1 S0: **000**. Keep the last carry on their
left and the full result is **1000**, or eight.

Nothing forces us to discard that carry. If we choose a three-bit result field,
we keep 000 there and must decide what to do with the extra 1. Keeping it
separately preserves the full unsigned result.

<details>
<summary>Does 111 + 001 give zero or eight? What if we keep only three sum bits?</summary>

The full unsigned answer is **eight**, binary **1000**. The low three sum bits
are **000**, and the final carry is **1**. Keeping only those low bits gives
zero in a three-bit field; it does not change the full arithmetic answer.

</details>

## What this cost

In this construction, a half adder uses two logical gates and a full adder
uses five. Repeating that full-adder construction eight times uses **40 logical
gate instances**. This is a count of our diagram, not a count of a processor's
transistors or a guarantee of its internal adder design.

The Boolean model also leaves out physical settling time. A real ripple path
has to meet its timing requirements; instant changes on this page do not
measure its speed. A processor contains stored state and control as well as
arithmetic. We have explained one useful part, not every layer of the machine.

## When it's wrong, see why

- **The last carry disappeared.** Check the chosen output width and whether the
  carry is kept separately. Discarding it is a storage choice, not a requirement
  of the adder.
- **A sum bit is wrong further left than where you looked.** Check the carry chain. A wrong carry can affect higher columns, but it need not reach every one.
  With A = B = 0, an incoming 1 changes that column's sum to 1 but its next
  carry is 0. Find the lowest differing column, then trace onwards.
- **You wired the carry into the wrong end.** Carries travel from the small end to the big
  end, the same direction you carry on paper.

## What you've learnt

- A **full adder** takes two operand bits and an incoming carry bit.
- Its sum is **`XOR` of all three**, and its carry is on when **at least two** inputs are.
- Our five-gate construction uses two half adders and an `OR` for their carries.
- Chaining one per bit gives a **ripple-carry adder**, where the carry travels along the
  chain.
- Keeping the final carry preserves a wider answer; discarding it keeps only low bits.
