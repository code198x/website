Here is a guard for a game loop:

```text
IF NOT (dead OR finished) THEN
  CALL keepPlaying
END
```

You can work out what it means, but it takes a moment. Two negatives fighting each other:
not one thing or the other thing.

Now here is another line:

```text
IF alive AND unfinished THEN
  CALL keepPlaying
END
```

That one you read at a glance. The claim of this unit is that **they are the same test**, and
that you can prove it under explicit assumptions rather than take it on trust.

## Proving it

For this comparison, `alive` must equal `NOT dead`, and `unfinished` must equal
`NOT finished`, using the same current Boolean values. These are names for the
complementary answers, not independently stored flags that we hope agree.
So the claim is:

`NOT (dead OR finished)`  is  `NOT dead AND NOT finished`

Every row matches. Not most rows. Every row, and the table lists all of them, so there is no
case left where they could differ.

That proves equal Boolean results for the same input values. Under the stated
complement assumptions, choose whichever wording reads better.

A table does not measure execution cost or establish a target language’s operand
evaluation rules. If a test calls a job, changes state or can fail, check that
language’s behaviour as well.

<details>
<summary>What if dead is TRUE but a separately stored alive flag is still TRUE?</summary>

With finished FALSE and unfinished TRUE, `NOT (dead OR finished)` is FALSE,
but `alive AND unfinished` is TRUE. The stale flag breaks our complement
assumption. De Morgan’s law has not failed; the renamed inputs no longer mean
the same current facts.

## The rule behind it

This is not a one-off. It works the other way round too:

<TruthTable
  inputs={["A", "B"]}
  columns={[
    { label: "NOT (A AND B)", values: [1, 1, 1, 0] },
    { label: "NOT A OR NOT B", values: [1, 1, 1, 0], same: true }
  ]}
  caption="The same trade, with AND and OR the other way round."
/>

Both together are called **De Morgan's laws**, after the mathematician who wrote them down:

- `NOT (A AND B)` is the same as `NOT A OR NOT B`
- `NOT (A OR B)` is the same as `NOT A AND NOT B`

The pattern is short enough to keep in your head. **Push the `NOT` inwards, and `AND` swaps
with `OR`.**

## What it is for

Two things, and the second is the one that pays.

**Untangling.** A `NOT` in front of a bracket is hard to read. Push it in, and often the
double negatives cancel and leave you a line that says what it means.

**Choosing your side.** Sometimes the positive version is clearer, sometimes the negative
one. De Morgan's lets you pick without changing behaviour. A guard is easier to trust when it
is phrased the way you think about the problem.

## When the tables disagree

The interesting case is when you build both columns and they do *not* match.

That is not a failure. It means the two lines are genuinely different tests, and you have
just found out which cases tell them apart. Look at the rows that differ, because one of them
is a bug you were about to ship.

This is the whole method: when you are unsure whether two versions behave the same, do not
squint at them. Build both columns and read across.

## When it's wrong, see why

- **You dropped a `NOT` while pushing it in.** Every term inside the bracket gets one. `NOT
  (A AND B)` is `NOT A OR NOT B`, not `NOT A OR B`.
- **You forgot to swap the joining word.** Pushing a `NOT` in turns `AND` into `OR` and back.
  Leaving it alone gives a different test that happens to look right.
- **The columns disagree in one row.** Trust the table. Find that case in the game and decide
  which behaviour you want.

## What you've learnt

- Matching columns prove equal **Boolean results for the same inputs**, not every runtime behaviour.
- **De Morgan's laws**: push a `NOT` inside a bracket and `AND` swaps with `OR`.
- The point is **readable guards**: say a condition the way you think about it.
- Columns that **disagree** have shown you a case worth looking at.

## Where this leaves you

You can write down every case a test has, compare two tests honestly, and rewrite one into
the other while preserving its Boolean result under the stated assumptions.
Keep the actual input meanings and target-language rules in view.
