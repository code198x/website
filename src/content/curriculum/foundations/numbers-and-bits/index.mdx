## What this is

General Programming taught the first half of computer literacy: everything is instructions. This
is the other half. **Everything the instructions touch is a number**, and underneath every number
is a row of switches, on or off.

*Numbers & Bits* teaches the ideas that make a machine legible. A number can be written in tens,
in twos or in hex and still be the same number. A byte is eight switches. A computer writes a
minus sign when it has no minus sign. And the bit logic lets a program read one switch out of
eight, or pack eight into one.

It uses the same **pseudocode** General Programming did, so nothing here is tied to one machine or
one language. Where that grammar needed new words for this subject, it gained them, and you will
meet them as they turn up.

## Who it's for

Anyone heading down into a machine, and sooner than you might expect.

Assembly needs this on every line: hex addresses, bit flags, signed offsets. BASIC reaches for it
too. The moment you draw your own character on a Spectrum you are writing eight bytes of bit
patterns, and this is what turns that eight-byte recipe into something you understand rather than
copy.

Nothing here gates a machine. If you would rather start a game first and come back when bits turn
up, do that.

## What it is not

This is about **what a number is**, not what a machine does with one.

How the screen draws, how a sound chip sings, what lives at which address: those are the machine's
own story, told when you *Meet the Machine* and can watch a byte become a pixel. Here we stay with
the numbers themselves, which are true on every machine and so taught once, before you pick one.

## The shape

- **Counting** — why ten is a habit of having ten fingers, and why the machine counts in twos. A
  number as a pattern of on and off: the bit, the nibble, the byte.
- **The same number, three ways** — decimal, binary and hex as three spellings of one value, and
  why 255, 256 and 65535 turn up everywhere once you know to look.
- **Two bytes together** — sixteen switches count to 65535, hex was ready for it, and that is
  where 64K comes from.
- **Negatives** — how a computer writes a minus sign with no minus sign at all, and the
  wrap-around that comes with it.
- **Bit logic** — the tools that test and combine switches, and how to mask one bit out of eight.
- **Shifting** — sliding the whole row left or right, and why that is multiplying and dividing by
  two.
- **Letters** — a byte holds a number, so a letter is one too. Which number is an
  agreement, and upper and lower case turn out to be one bit apart.
- **Fractions** — how a machine carries half a pixel when it counts in whole steps, and why a
  floating-point number is never exact.
- **Fixed-point** — the answer games used: fix the point at an agreed scale, and the
  arithmetic goes back to being the fast kind.

By the end, hex will not read as gibberish and a bit flag will not be a mystery. You will hold the
number sense every machine assumes. From here the road goes down: **Meet the Machine**, and the
code that speaks in exactly these terms.
