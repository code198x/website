`SHOW` displays something. We use teaching notation to reason about the result,
without installing a language or choosing a machine. Quotation marks enclose
text; `+` adds numbers. Now compare displaying a written sum with displaying
the value that sum produces.

## Text, or a value

Look at these two instructions. They are almost the same. One has quotation marks and one
does not.

```text
SHOW "2 + 2"
SHOW 2 + 2
```

<Output>{`2 + 2
4`}</Output>

The quotation marks decide the meaning. Inside them, `2 + 2` is text, and the computer
shows it letter for letter. Outside them it is a sum, and the computer works it out.

The machine is not guessing. You chose which one you meant by how you wrote it.

<Question prompt={'What does SHOW "3 + 4" display? What changes if we remove the quotation marks?'}>

With quotes, the output is the text **3 + 4**, including its spaces and plus
sign. The quotes themselves do not appear. Without quotes, `SHOW 3 + 4`
calculates the sum and displays **7**. Both instructions are valid; which one
is useful depends on whether we wanted the written question or its answer.

</Question>

## Joining text to a value

A bare `4` does not tell you much. Usually you want to say what it is, so you join some
fixed text to the value:

```text
SHOW "Two plus two is ", 2 + 2
```

<Output>{`Two plus two is 4`}</Output>

In this notation, the comma joins one thing to the next. It adds nothing of its
own: no space, no punctuation. If you want a space before the `4`, put it inside the quotation marks, the
way `"Two plus two is "` does above.

<Question prompt={'We want Score: 5, but wrote SHOW "Score:", 2 + 3. What appears, and how would you repair it?'}>

It displays **Score:5**. The comma adds no space, and the quoted text has none
after the colon. Put one space inside the quotes, after the colon:

```text
SHOW "Score: ", 2 + 3
```

Moving spaces around the comma outside the quotes does not add them to the displayed text.
The sum still produces 5; we repaired the presentation, not the calculation.

</Question>

That join is what makes output readable. `Score: 100`, `You have 3 lives left` and
`Time: 42` are all a piece of fixed text with a value joined on.

## Take the idea to a language

The idea transfers; the punctuation needs checking. In Sinclair BASIC,
`PRINT "Score: ";2+3` uses a **semicolon** to place the items immediately
together. Its comma moves to a print zone instead; it is not our SHOW comma.

[Ask and use the answer](/systems/sinclair-zx-spectrum/basic/meet-basic/unit-03-ask-and-use-the-answer/)
explains a sentence built from several pieces. Read “Give the words somewhere
to go” for its semicolons and quoted spaces. Running the complete story is
optional; that lesson introduces its own variables and input. We will give
values names in our next unit.

## When it's wrong, see why

- **You see `2 + 2` where you wanted `4`.** The sum sits inside quotation marks, so the
  computer showed it as text. Take the quotes off the part you want it to work out.
- **You see a bare number with nothing to explain it.** Join some text on to say what it
  is.
- **The text runs into the number**, like `Two plus two is4`. The space belongs inside the
  quotation marks. The computer shows exactly what sits between them.

## What you've learnt

- Output can show a **value the computer works out**, not only fixed text.
- **Quotation marks choose the meaning.** Inside them it is text. Outside them the
  computer works it out.
- **Joining** text to a value makes output readable, and the comma adds nothing of its own.
