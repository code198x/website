## Divide before comparing

`t(j)/n` is the fraction of counted rolls belonging to face j. Multiplying by 100 turns that fraction into a percentage. We round to one decimal place so the result fits in the table:

`INT (1000*t(j)/n+0.5)/10`

Multiplying the proportion by 1,000 gives tenths of a percentage point. Adding 0.5 and rounding down selects the nearest whole tenth; dividing by ten gives the displayed percentage. All counts are nonnegative, which is the range for this rounding rule. The Spectrum may print 25 rather than 25.0; they mean the same share.

For one out of twelve, the displayed result is 8.3%. The count is still exactly one. Rounding changes the presentation, not the tally stored in the array.

<Question prompt="Suppose every face appears twice in twelve rolls. What happens when you add the six displayed percentages?">

Each is shown as 16.7%. Six times 16.7 is 100.2%, even though the exact shares sum to 100%. Separate rounding caused the difference. Do not alter a tally or quietly adjust one percentage to make the displayed total come out neatly.

</Question>

## A reference, not a promise

For a fair six-sided die, each face has probability one sixth, about 16.7%. The reference below the table describes that model. It does not say each face must take exactly one sixth of this particular sample.

RUN several times and compare the tables. Twelve rolls can produce a visibly uneven spread. A balanced sample would not prove the generator fair, just as one uneven sample would not prove it unfair.

The program never divides by zero: line 600 runs after the twelve-roll loop. Keep that order in mind when we move the same calculation inside the loop. We must count a roll before calculating its share.

If a displayed percentage looks wrong, divide that row's count by the total at the top and multiply by 100. Check the arithmetic before drawing a conclusion about randomness.
