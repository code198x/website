Maths gives us ways to describe what a game does and ask what might happen next.
A chance of finding an item, a distance to a target or a change in speed becomes
something we can calculate, test and compare.

Start with the question that interests you. You do not need to complete
Foundations or a programming course before exploring these ideas. Each project
explains the knowledge it uses and names the machine used for its runnable example.

## Dice Roller: a probability experiment

What changes when you roll a die twelve times instead of twelve hundred?
**Dice Roller** lets you watch counts and percentages develop, then compare
one sample with the next. The bars stay on the same scale, so a larger count
does not automatically look like a larger share.

The five lessons below build the experiment in **Sinclair BASIC on a stock
48K ZX Spectrum**. They start with individual rolls, add six tallies and
percentages, then draw a live chart and keep the previous sample for comparison.
The final lesson saves the program and checks a fresh tape load.

[Start with Roll a die](/craft/maths-for-games/unit-01/).
For help with Spectrum keyword entry and editing, use
[Meet BASIC](/systems/sinclair-zx-spectrum/basic/meet-basic/).

Choose **1–4** for batches of 12, 60, 300 or 1,200. At a result, **R** starts
fresh, **A** adds another batch and **N** chooses a size. **Q** stops rolling
early and retains the counts; at a result or menu it quits. The previous sample
stays beside the live chart. The experiment is silent and has no score or
winning distribution.

Counts remain exact. Percentages round to one decimal place; twenty-cell bars
round to the nearest five percentage points. Small batches refresh every roll.
Larger batches refresh every ten rolls, plus the opening roll and the exact
result. Added samples can grow to 9,600 rolls.

A finite sample does not prove a generator fair, and a larger sample need not
look more balanced every time. Those distinctions matter when games use chance
for encounters, rewards or an opponent's choices: an expected share is not a
promise about a short run.

## Aiming and movement: geometry you can use

Place a point, measure a right triangle, then use sine and cosine to turn an angle
into movement. Explore radians, calculate the direction to a target with atan2,
and compare fractional movement with scaled integers.

These six lessons run directly in your browser. Each has a focused experiment,
worked arithmetic and a prediction to try before revealing the explanation.
No programming language or probability lessons are required.

[Start with Place a point](/craft/maths-for-games/unit-06/).

The experiments use world coordinates with Y upwards, named angle units and
explicit rounding rules. They model the mathematics on the host computer; they
do not measure a retro machine's performance.

[Foundations](/foundations/) explores representations and programming concepts.
[The Craft](/craft/) connects them to rules, feedback and player decisions.
