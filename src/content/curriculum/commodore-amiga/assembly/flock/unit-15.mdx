Every sheep so far has been worth the same twenty-five points, and the only reason to hurry was boredom. This unit instals a gamble. Occasionally — aiming for roughly one roll in eight — the next sheep out of the field is **black**: worth a fat bonus at the fold, and a touch quicker on her feet than the flock you've learnt to steer. Quicker sounds like a gift until you're threading the rhythm you grooved over fourteen units with a sheep who arrives everywhere half a beat early. Risk, reward, and a chime at the pen that makes the whole detour feel earned.

## Where Randomness Comes From

We use a **pseudorandom generator**: calculations transform a stored seed into the next value. This routine uses 16-bit xorshift steps (7, 9, 8). With the frame-counter mixing removed, a non-zero seed visits all 65,535 non-zero words before repeating. That long cycle is useful, but does not guarantee independent choices or invisible patterns.

<CodeFromFile src="commodore-amiga/assembly/flock/unit-15/snippets/01-newsheep-and-rnd.asm" />

The game also XORs `framecnt` into the seed before each roll. That word-sized counter advances once per main-loop update, wraps after 65,536 increments, and is mixed in when the next sheep steps up. It is game state, not an independent clock counting from power-on. Player timing can therefore change the sequence. It does not guarantee different sheep for different players: matching state, frame-counter values and random-call order repeat the calculation. If the mixed calculation produces zero, the routine substitutes `$ACE1` so it can continue from a non-zero state.

`and.w #7,d0` keeps the lowest three bits, and zero selects black. Eight bit patterns are possible; that does **not** prove each has equal probability in this game. Without the frame stir, zero appears 8,191 times in those low bits over the complete 65,535-state cycle, while each other pattern appears 8,192 times. With the stir, the call schedule changes the sequence and those full-cycle counts no longer describe it. Short runs can contain streaks either way.

`newsheep` itself is the factoring this code wanted for three units — losesheep and the pen both used to place the next sheep by hand, in duplicate. Now there is one doorway every sheep steps through, and the dice live in the doorway.

## Painting With the Copper List

A black sheep needs black art — or so you'd think. She uses the same two step images the white flock has used since Unit 4. What changes is the **palette**: her three colours live in the Copper list as plain `dc.w` pairs, and the Copper list is only data. The same trick that repoints a sprite at a new image (Unit 13's diving duck) repaints a sheep: label the palette entries, poke three words before she steps out. Wool to soot, face to pale, shading to shadow — and the un-poke when a white sheep follows, or the whole flock inherits her coat. No second art, no second sprite, twelve bytes of writes.

Her temperament is one more word: `steer` now loads its hop rhythm from a variable instead of a constant, and the black sheep's is shorter. That single number — six frames against four — is the entire difference between a sheep you steer and a sheep you *negotiate with*.

## Experiment: Load the Dice

- `and.w #7,d0` selects black when three low bits are zero. Predict how changing the mask to `#3` or `#15` affects frequency: they test two or four bits, targeting roughly one in four or one in sixteen if those patterns are well distributed. Record black and total counts over several runs, keeping the input schedule fixed for comparisons. A short run need not match those proportions. Which version feels worthwhile to play?
- `SKITTISH equ 2` — a black sheep with a three-frame rhythm. Somewhere between thrilling and unsteerable is a line; find it and ask what the bonus would have to pay to make crossing it worth your while.
- In a separate experimental copy, remove the `framecnt` stir and reset `seed` to the same value before each run. Predict the sheep-colour sequence, then compare the same input sequence. Matching seeds and random-call order can repeat the colours; matching their appearance times also requires matching game state and input/update timing. Restore the ordinary version afterwards. Repetition can be useful for diagnosis or a deliberately learnable challenge.
- Make the chime three notes. `soundtick` plays two; Unit 11's Try This sketched the sequencer. The black sheep is the right excuse to build it.

## If It Doesn't Work

- **Every sheep is black, forever?** The poke works; the un-poke doesn't. White sheep must write the white palette back — the Copper list remembers whatever was poked last.
- **No sheep is ever black?** Inspect the value returned by `rnd`, its masked low bits and the branch sense: zero takes `beq` to black. Reversing that branch selects the other seven bit patterns, not a guaranteed seven-out-of-eight schedule. A short run without black sheep can be ordinary variation; reproduce the calls before blaming the palette or generator.
- **The same sheep every game?** Inspect whether the seed advances and whether each run begins from the same state. The same starting seed and call order deliberately repeat a sequence when the frame-counter stir is removed; that is not itself a fault. With the stir present, record the frame counter at each call too.
- **She rolls black but moves like a white sheep?** `steer` must read `hopgap`, not the `COOLDOWN` constant. An assembler will happily keep the old immediate and never warn you the variable exists.

## Try This

1. **Show the gamble coming.** The flock strip at the bottom counts lives — could it show the *next* sheep's colour? One glyph redrawn at roll time. Decide whether the game is better when you can see her coming or when she's a surprise at the gate.
2. **A skittish tell.** She's quicker — but players learn that only by losing her. Give the black sheep an idle fidget: every couple of seconds with no input, a single involuntary hop. (One timer, one `bsr` into the hop you already have. Decide which direction she fidgets — toward danger is crueller and funnier.)
3. **Bank the bonus.** A hundred points for a black sheep penned — what about a *streak*? Two black sheep in a row could pay double. One word of state and one compare; ask whether the game wants memory in its gambling.

## What You've Learnt

- **Pseudo-randomness** — xorshift folds a seed into itself; the unmixed sequence is deterministic; its period and visible patterns depend on the exact shifts and word width.
- **Entropy from the player** — mixing the frame counter at event time can vary results with the player’s timing. It does not guarantee a different result or a fair distribution.
- **The Copper list is writable data, part two** — `newsheep` changes the stored palette values. The Copper must still reach those entries before the display uses the new colours; editing the list is not the same event as seeing the result.
- **Character is a constant** — one rhythm variable distinguishes the gamble from the flock; personality costs a word.
- **One doorway for every spawn** — factoring duplicated "next sheep" code into `newsheep` is what made a per-sheep roll possible at all. Features land easiest where the architecture already has a door.

## What's Next

The farmyard has its full cast and its gamble. What it doesn't have is an ending — levels that escalate, a clean-flock bonus, a game-over that means something and a victory worth the climb. Three units remain, and they're the polish that turns systems into a *game*.
