Everything works; nothing *escalates*. The same five vehicles at the same five speeds make crossing twelve the same crossing as crossing one, and a game with no curve isn't finished — it's a demo with a title screen. The finale instals the curve: a **level table** that hands each new field faster lanes, a hungrier duck and thinner nerves; a **shepherd's bonus** for bringing the whole flock home untouched; and one last pass on the thing the player touches five times a second — the hop itself.

## The Escalation Is a Table Row

<CodeFromFile src="commodore-amiga/assembly/flock/unit-18/snippets/01-the-level-table.asm" />

Here is the reward for seventeen units of putting things in tables. Everything that should get harder **already lives in pokeable data**: the vehicle speeds sit in `vehtab` (and Unit 14 made them 16.16, so *half-pixel* difficulty steps exist — level two's cart is minus one and a half, a speed that couldn't be expressed before the cow); the duck's stamina and the bolt threshold were constants promoted to variables with two edits. A level is sixteen bytes:

<CodeFromFile src="commodore-amiga/assembly/flock/unit-18/snippets/02-leveltab.asm" />

`applylevel` pokes a row over the live game — the same move that recoloured the black sheep, aimed at physics instead of palette. No `if level >= 3` scattered through the code, no per-level routines: the systems never learn that levels exist. They read their tuning every frame, and the tuning changed while they weren't looking. When the last row runs out it repeats: the curve flattens, the game stays honest, and the score becomes the measure.

Win and loss now part ways in `endtick`: a lost flock takes the long walk back to the title, but a **full fold goes to the next field** — pens emptied, farm redrawn, lives carried over. The flock is the run's resource; the levels are what spends it.

And a game earns the word *finished* only when you can drive it to *both* endings and watch them arrive. The full fold at the top of this page is the win; here is the other one — the last sheep drowned, the heart strip empty, the field it never crossed still running its traffic under the lament:

![Flock's loss: the flock strip empty, no sheep on the field, the water and road indifferent — the run lost.](/images/commodore-amiga/assembly/flock/unit-18/loss.png)

## The Shepherd's Bonus

Two instructions in `trypen`, at the moment the fifth pen fills: if `lives` still equals `FLOCK_SIZE` — five crossings, five arrivals, nobody lost — `SHEPHERD_BONUS` lands on the score. It's the risk/reward lesson from Unit 15 turned structural: the black sheep gambles a *crossing*; the clean-flock bonus gambles a whole *level*, because protecting the bonus means refusing every reckless shortcut for five sheep in a row. The best bonuses price a behaviour, not a moment.

## The Feel Pass: a Banked Hop

<CodeFromFile src="commodore-amiga/assembly/flock/unit-18/snippets/03-buffered-hop.asm" />

The promise of a final feel pass comes down to one injustice: a quick tap during the hop cooldown used to vanish. The player did the right thing at *almost* the right time and the game pretended nothing happened. The fix is **input buffering** — during the cooldown the stick is still read, and a fresh press is banked and served the instant she's ready.

Two details cost us a debugging session each, and they carry the lesson:

- **The press that caused the hop must not echo.** Bank naively and a single tap fires twice — the tail of the press is still there on cooldown frame one, gets banked, and replays. The `sawneutral` gate (the stick must come home before anything banks) is the difference between responsiveness and a sheep that overshoots every move. We shipped Unit 16 a latch pattern; here it is again, guarding *against* input instead of fire.
- **Replay what the code reads, where it reads it.** Our first buffer banked the *decoded* stick and replayed it into the decode's output — but `steer` tests left and right on the **raw** register and up and down on the decoded one — so a banked right hop silently never fired. Replay the raw word *before* the decode and every consumer sees a consistent world. When you intercept an input path, intercept it at the top.

## The Last Yard

Three small things separate "the systems all work" from "this looks finished", and each answers a question an earlier unit left open.

<CodeFromFile src="commodore-amiga/assembly/flock/unit-18/snippets/04-the-last-yard.asm" />

**The title now says PRESS FIRE** — Unit 17's most glaring omission, fixed with an 8×8 font stamped by the same glyph routine the score has used since Unit 10, winking on a 32-frame beat read straight off `framecnt`. A blink is just a draw and a clear taking turns on a clock you already had.

**The HUD shows the level** — "I died on four" finally has a four. One honesty subtlety: the level *counter* never stops climbing, but the difficulty *table row* clamps at four and the displayed digit at nine. The player's number tells the truth about how far they got; the physics quietly admits it ran out of new ideas. Those are three different caps, and conflating them is how games end up lying about one of them.

**The endings sing** — a rising fanfare for a full fold, a falling lament for an empty field, both served by Unit 17's `tunetick` from two more tiny tables:

The sequencer grew from one terminator to two, and that's the whole cost of win and lose having voices.

## Experiment: Bend the Curve

- Write a level five: gentler than four. Difficulty that *breathes* — hard field, kind field — is one more table row, and it's how the great arcade curves are built.
- `SHEPHERD_BONUS equ 1000`. Now the bonus is the game and the sheep are the obstacle. Watch how one constant rebalances every decision you make at the road's edge.
- Put the buffered hop back the naive way (bank without `sawneutral`) and play thirty seconds. The overshoot is *infuriating* — and invisible in a screenshot. Feel bugs are why you playtest with hands, not eyes.
- Make `MAXLEVEL` wrap to level one instead of repeating level four. An endless gentle loop instead of a wall — which game is better? (Trick question: it depends who's playing. That's what difficulty design *is*.)

## If It Doesn't Work

- **Level two plays exactly like level one?** `applylevel` must poke the *live* table — check it writes `vehtab`'s speed slots, not a copy, and that `nextlevel` calls it at all.
- **Level two opens with five ghost hearts in the pens?** Ours did. The residents' glyphs live in *plane 2*, and the farm-wide clear only wipes plane 1 — the pens were logically empty and visibly full. Every reset must reset every plane that holds state. (`clearfold` is the two lines that fix it.)
- **The fold never advances the level?** `won` has two consumers now — the pen logic that sets it and `endtick` that acts on it. If a stray reset clears it before `WINWAIT` elapses, the win evaporates.
- **A vehicle goes backwards on level up?** Sign error in the table: leftward lanes need *more negative* each level. `-(3<<16)/2` and `(-3<<16)/2` agree, but `(3<<16)/-2` is a typo the assembler may swallow whole.
- **She hops twice per tap?** The echo. `sawneutral` missing, or not cleared on the hop, or the buffer not cleared on `newsheep` — a banked intention haunting the next sheep is a genuinely confusing ghost.

## Try This

1. **Show the level on the title too.** The HUD carries it through the rosette — but a "REACHED LEVEL 6" line under PRESS FIRE is the classic arcade brag. Everything needed is in `blinkprompt` and `drawlevel`; the design question is whether the title should advertise your best or your last.
2. **Escalate the *world*, not just the numbers.** Level three could add a second bale row; level four could send the cow the other way. `vehtab` rows are just data — could a level table add and remove *rows*? Sketch what `applylevel` becomes when levels change the cast, not the tempo.
3. **The whole game, from memory.** Close the source. On paper, list every system in the main loop and what state it owns. If you can draw Flock's skeleton unaided, eighteen units did their job — and you're ready to design the next game instead of following it.

## What You've Learnt

- **Levels are data** — escalation as table rows poked over live state; the systems never know what level it is.
- **Architecture compounds** — the level table only costs sixteen bytes a row because vehtab, 16.16 speeds, promoted constants and `newsheep` were already there. Every earlier discipline got cheaper to build on.
- **Bonuses price behaviour** — the shepherd's bonus pays for a whole level of restraint, not one lucky moment.
- **Input buffering, honestly** — bank fresh presses during cooldowns, gate on seen-neutral, replay raw. Feel is mostly justice for inputs.
- **The last yard is small and visible** — a prompt, a number, two stings: an afternoon's work that changes whether strangers can play it at all. Finish lives at the surface.
- **A finished game is a loop with a curve** — title in, escalating levels through, title out. Eighteen units ago this was a black screen with a coloured stripe.
