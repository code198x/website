Unit 16 called Phase 1 complete, and mechanically it was. Then we tried to *win* it — drove a controller script through every coin in one run — and learnt three things in an afternoon. There was nothing to win: the third coin was exactly like the second. The spike gap was one pixel wider than the jump, so the right half of the level had never been reached. And dying once next to the obstacle could spend all three lives in three frames. This unit is everything that came out of that afternoon: a level loop, a tune, two stings, a blink — and two honest fixes the game needed before any of it could matter.

- **The speed lives in a table, and the table is the difficulty curve:**

The obstacle's move code reads `obstacle_speed` fresh every frame and never learns that levels exist; `next_level` pokes the new value while nobody's looking. No `if level >= 3` anywhere.

- **Three caps, three jobs.** The level *counter* never stops climbing. The table *index* clamps at the last entry — past level five the game repeats its hardest setting, because an unclamped index would read whatever byte follows the table and call it a speed. And the HUD *digit* shows 9 forever after. The player's number tells the truth; the physics quietly admits it ran out of new ideas. Conflate those caps and the game lies about one of them.
- **The score had the same bug, and the opposite fix.** It was a single tile too — so the tenth coin drew `DIGIT_ZERO + 10`, the spike glyph, and every coin after that garbled the readout. But where the level digit *caps* (the difficulty plateaus, so 9 is honest), the score must not: an achievement that stops counting is a lie. So the fix grows the field instead of clamping it — split the byte into hundreds, tens and units and write three tiles, and the number climbs as far as the coins do. Same latent bug, opposite answer, because the two numbers have opposite jobs.

## A Table and a Tick

The title was silent; the endings were silent. One routine fixes all three, because a melody — on the NES as on every machine — is just a table of pitch and duration with a clock walking it:

<CodeFromFile src="nintendo-entertainment-system/assembly/dash/unit-17/snippets/03-tune-tick.asm" />

The APU generates the waveform between player updates. Most calls to `tune_tick` only decrement a countdown. Loading a note writes its settings; a rest or stop silences it. There is no separate note-off write at every note boundary: the next row decides what happens. A period of zero is a **rest**: silence is a note too, so the channel gates off and the clock keeps counting.

The part worth keeping is the *two terminators*. `$FF` loops to the top — that's the title jingle, round and round until START. `$FE` stops for good — that's a fanfare or a sting, which says its piece once and goes quiet. Win and lose having voices cost the sequencer exactly one extra marker:

One confession from the building of it: our first jingle was completely silent, and the code was correct on paper. The culprit was the sweep-disable write — the classic NES idiom is `$08` (negate set, shift zero), and the emulator's sweep unit mutes pulse 1 on exactly that pattern (bug filed). `$00` disables the sweep just as thoroughly for every period in our tables, so that's what ships. When your sound is silent and your code looks right, read the sweep register twice.


## Timing investigation: which clock changed?

Before changing the game, trace one call. `start_phrase` sets `tune_timer` to 1. On the next `tune_tick`, the decrement reaches zero, so we read a row and load its duration. A duration of 6 means the next row starts **six further player updates** later. It does not mean six waveform cycles.

In this game, NMI performs display work and sets `nmi_flag`. Main notices and clears the flag, reads input, then calls `tune_tick` before dispatching the game state. The tune runs in main code, after that notification. The CPU does not execute main and NMI simultaneously, and notification does not guarantee immediate service. A busy loop can delay music; a one-bit flag cannot count every missed notification. We will investigate those problems separately.

### Explain what the comparison does not change

The display's refresh, input sampling, game updates, player updates and waveform generation are different jobs. Dash currently connects several of them through its main loop, but the APU continues generating the current waveform between calls. A late musical update can therefore extend an old note or rest; it need not corrupt the waveform.

A real regional machine change may alter the hardware clock and tuning as well as the display rate. The pulse timer frequency depends on that clock and its period value, as documented in [NESdev's pulse-channel research](https://www.nesdev.org/wiki/APU_Pulse). These exact 50/60 rates isolate one variable; they are not precision measurements of NES refresh. Frame-stepped vibrato, arpeggios or envelopes would also change speed if we tied their progress to the same calls. This player does not yet implement those effects.

Finish by explaining how the melody can keep the same pitches while its phrases become shorter. Then identify the code we would need to change to preserve musical tempo without slowing the whole game. Changing every pitch period would address a different problem. Keep the working game intact: deciding how to schedule player updates, account for uneven spacing and handle overdue work is the next experiment.

### Decide what happens when we cannot keep up

Predict what input 30 would do. Five musical updates are due, but this routine promises at most one. Its deliberate policy is to set `tempo_fault`, stop the experimental music and wait for a new phrase. The overload companion triggers that case; its event trace ends and its audio becomes silent. We have made the missed music observable rather than writing several overdue notes at once and calling that a repair.

Stopping optional music may be an acceptable diagnostic response. Silently dropping movement, collisions or score events would change the game rules. A finished game needs a policy suited to each job, and a way to make a relevant failure understandable to the player.

There is another limit: if the caller reports 5 after a long delay, the routine cannot discover that the report was wrong. Dash's existing notification flag still needs its own investigation. This companion is not a complete regional conversion or missed-frame detector.

Finish by explaining both the improvement and its cost: one phase byte carries time, while event spacing is limited by service opportunities. Identify the owner of that byte, the reset needed for a new phrase, and the observable result of overload. ### Fill first, publish last

Give the slot a `request_ready` byte. Zero means main owns the empty slot. Main fills both payload bytes, then stores 1 to publish the complete request. While ready is 1, main must leave both bytes alone. NMI consumes them and returns the slot only after its last read.

<CodeFromFile src="nintendo-entertainment-system/assembly/dash/unit-17/handoff/mailbox.asm" />

X and Y supply the low and high bytes. Carry clear reports acceptance; carry set reports a full slot without changing its contents. In this experiment, one producer runs in main and one consumer runs in NMI. The existing handler saves and restores A, X and Y around its work.

Predict the repaired outcome with the same deliberate wait between stores. NMI sees ready zero and ignores the unfinished pair. After main completes the high byte and publishes, the next NMI consumes `$00D5`. A later empty call must not play it again.

This is **atomic publication** relative to this interrupt observer: NMI sees the ready-byte store completed or not yet executed. The payload still takes several instructions to prepare. The [NES NMI mechanism](https://www.nesdev.org/wiki/NMI) interrupts CPU execution at an instruction boundary; it does not turn a sequence of stores into one operation.

### What if the slot is full?

Try requesting `$0152`, then `$017C` before the consumer has run. Predict which value survives and what main must remember.

<details>
<summary>Compare full, retry and empty</summary>

The second call returns carry set. The slot still contains `$0152`, so NMI consumes the first request intact. Main explicitly retries its retained `$017C` request after the slot becomes empty. That request is then consumed once. The following empty call performs no pulse-period writes.

This refusal is information the caller must handle. Ignoring carry would silently lose the second request. Waiting for space inside NMI would prevent main from making progress and is not a repair.

</details>

The native companion checks all six stages in both versions and repeats them from a fresh process. Its instrumented title handlers return within VBlank with registers preserved. That is evidence for these paths, not permission to add unlimited work to NMI. The consumer performs at most one request and never waits.

SEI masks IRQ, **not NMI**; it cannot protect this handoff. The [CPU interrupt distinction](https://www.nesdev.org/wiki/Interrupts) matters more than a generic “disable interrupts” recipe. Our repair leaves notification enabled and makes ownership explicit.

Finish by explaining why main may fill an empty slot safely even if NMI arrives after the empty check. Then identify what would break the argument if another producer could fill the slot. As an optional extension, consider a request containing a pointer: consuming the pointer does not mean the data it points to can immediately be reused. We need a lifetime agreement as well as a complete request.

### Count only when the count matters

For this experiment, we require one acknowledgement per notification. NMI owns a byte called `produced`; main owns `consumed`. Initialise them equally before starting the producer. Their unsigned byte difference gives the number still pending.

<CodeFromFile src="nintendo-entertainment-system/assembly/dash/unit-17/notification/counter.asm" />

`take_one` acknowledges at most one notification. Carry set means it took one; carry clear means empty or stopped by overflow. Main must inspect the separate overflow flag to distinguish those outcomes. Taking a notification does not itself run a simulation update or play a note.

Suppose main sees produced 1 and consumed 0. NMI arrives before `INC consumed`, making produced 2. Main then advances consumed to 1. **One notification remains.** Main has not overwritten NMI's byte. If a notification arrives just after an empty check, we discover it on a later call.

This relies on one producer, one consumer, ordinary RAM and a handler that does not nest. NMI suspends main; it does not run on another CPU. The handler preserves the registers main was using. [NES interrupt handling](https://www.nesdev.org/wiki/Interrupts) does not let SEI block NMI, so “disable interrupts around it” is not our repair.

Predict these three cases, then compare the companion's recorded results:

- Start at produced 255, consumed 254. Another notification arrives during the take. What remains after produced wraps to zero?
- Start with three pending notifications. One more arrives; main takes two in separate calls. How many remain?
- Start with 254 pending notifications and attempt three more without consuming. What must happen before the count becomes indistinguishable from empty?

<details>
<summary>Compare the boundaries</summary>

After the wrapped first take, produced is 0 and consumed is 255: unsigned byte subtraction gives one pending notification. The next take makes both zero. A further empty take does nothing.

The backlog case retains two notifications. We have bounded each call, not made the backlog disappear.

The first new notification takes the full case to 255 pending. The second would make produced equal consumed, falsely suggesting empty. Instead, the producer sets a sticky overflow flag and refuses further increments. Main stops taking new work once it observes that flag; an already-started take may finish. The third producer call remains stopped. Restarting would require stopping the producer before resetting both counters; this companion does not implement live recovery.

</details>

The five native cases repeat from fresh processes, preserve A/X/Y and stack use, and return from the instrumented title NMI within the measured NTSC VBlank window. That checks these paths, not every possible game workload.

A counter preserves evidence of backlog; it does not repair lateness. Repeating simulation with today's input cannot reconstruct missed button presses. Playing several late notes immediately cannot restore their rhythm. We must choose what may be merged, deferred or stopped, and keep important game events reliable. A 255-event capacity is a representation limit, not an acceptable delay.

Finish by choosing between a flag and a counter for two requirements: “redraw the latest score” and “account for every earned point”. Explain what additional data the second might need. Neither routine stores event payloads, and neither makes an unlimited catch-up loop safe.

## The Two Fixes

**The spike gap was unjumpable.** The jump carries the runner about fifteen pixels; the spikes spanned sixteen. Nobody had ever crossed them — every screenshot before this unit was taken on the left half of the level. One spike tile instead of two, and the game's geometry finally agrees with its physics. The test that catches this class of bug is brutally short: *can the game be finished?* Run it before you call anything complete.

**Dying could cascade.** Take a hit while the obstacle is over the respawn point and it hit you again the next frame, and again the frame after — three lives in three frames. The fix is a **grace window**: a second of invulnerability after every respawn, covering every damage source, made *visible* by blinking the runner while it lasts:

## If It Doesn't Work

- **The jingle plays one note and holds it forever?** `tune_timer` isn't reloaded from the duration column, or `tune_idx` never advances — the tick keeps agreeing with itself.
- **The tune is silent and the code looks right?** The sweep register. `$00` here; and remember the channel must be enabled in `$4015` too.
- **The jingle keeps playing into the game?** Nothing stops the band but you — `init_game_screen` gates the channel off and clears the sequencer pointer. The APU holding notes by itself cuts both ways.
- **Level 2 starts with a coin already collected?** The runner wasn't returned to the gate — they're standing on the respawned coin. Every reset resets the player too.
- **The level digit shows `:` or worse after level 9?** The digit isn't capped — `DIGIT_ZERO + 10` is whatever tile lives past the digits. Cap the *display*, clamp the *index*, let the *counter* run.
- **PRESS START flickers garbage or tears?** The blink writes are happening outside the NMI. Nametable writes during rendering corrupt the PPU address — the main loop owns the clock, the NMI owns the VRAM.

## What You've Learnt

- **Levels are data** — a counter, a clamped table index, and one live byte the game reads fresh every frame.
- **A sequencer is a table and a tick** — pitch, duration, rest-as-a-note, and two terminators: loop for the jingle, stop for the stings.
- **VRAM work belongs to the NMI** — the blink is a one-`AND` clock in the main loop and a few `PPUDATA` writes in vblank.
- **Winnability is a test** — the spike gap was unjumpable and no screenshot ever showed it; driving the game to a *win* found it in one run.
- **Fairness is mechanics plus visibility** — the grace window stops the cascade; the blink tells the player it exists.

## The Game Is Whole

Seventeen units. A runner with a world to cross, coins worth winning, levels that answer skill with speed, a tune at the front door, a fanfare for the fold and a sting for the floor — and a title that blinks the way every arcade machine in history has blinked.
