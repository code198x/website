One room described in text is a paragraph. Twenty rooms described in data are a world.

Lines 110–160 create the cave. Four arrays — `n(20)`, `s(20)`, `e(20)`, `w(20)` — store the exits from every room. `n(1)` is the room you reach by going north from room 1. If there is no north exit, the value is 0. A FOR loop reads all twenty rooms from DATA at lines 1010–1200.

Line 160 places the player in room 1. Lines 300–350 replace the hardcoded text with data-driven display: the room number comes from the variable `rm`, and the exits come from the four arrays.

## Arrays as a graph

A graph is a set of things connected to other things. Room 1 connects south to room 5 and east to room 2. Room 5 connects north back to room 1 and east to room 6. The four arrays encode every connection in the cave.

Look at the DATA for room 1: `0,5,2,0`. North is 0 (no exit). South is 5. East is 2. West is 0. That single line defines room 1's place in the world. Twenty lines define the whole cave.

This is the first time data represents space. In Quiz Master, DATA held questions. In Three in a Row, DATA held winning lines. Here, DATA holds a map — a network of rooms you can walk through. The structure is the same (numbers in DATA, read into arrays), but the meaning is entirely different. Same tool, new purpose.

## The cave layout

The twenty rooms form an irregular grid, roughly four columns by five rows, with some connections missing and others crossing between rows. No room is a dead end — every room has at least two exits. The layout is designed to feel like a cave system, not a chessboard.

You cannot see the layout from inside the game. You can only discover it by exploring.
