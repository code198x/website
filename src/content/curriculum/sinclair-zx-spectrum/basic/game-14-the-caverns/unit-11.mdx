## Steps as a score

The Caverns has no points, no rating system. Steps taken is the only metric, and it only appears when the game ends. Fewer steps means more efficient navigation — you interpreted the clues correctly and found the shortest path to each treasure while avoiding the hazards.

On a win, the step count is a challenge: "Can you do it faster?" On a loss, it shows how far you got: "You survived 14 steps before the creature found you." The same number means different things depending on the outcome.

## What you have built

The Caverns is complete. About ninety lines of BASIC plus twenty lines of DATA: a title screen, a twenty-room cave defined as four parallel arrays, compass-direction navigation with validation, a bottomless pit with a draft warning, a creature that wanders randomly and is detected by smell, three collectible treasures with a glint clue, an adjacency subroutine reused by all three clue types, three distinct endings with colour and sound, a step counter, and a replay loop.

This is the first game where the player navigates a world they cannot see. Every previous game showed the state on screen — the grid, the board, the scoreboard. Here, the state is hidden. The player builds a mental model from indirect clues and their own memory. That shift — from visible state to inferred state — is what makes The Caverns a text adventure and not a puzzle.

The adjacency subroutine is the technical lesson. One reusable `GO SUB` serves pit, creature, and treasure checks. The design lesson is deeper: data can represent space, and clues can replace sight.
