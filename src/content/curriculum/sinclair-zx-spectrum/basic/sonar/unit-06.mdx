## A drawn grid

The board is no longer just printed dots. Lines 220–230 draw the grid with `PLOT` and `DRAW` —
nine horizontal cyan lines and nine vertical ones, ruling an 8×8 board — and the cells are
spaced two columns apart (lines 260–270) so a number sits cleanly inside each square. `PLOT` and
`DRAW`, which you met in Reflex, turn a list of dots into something that looks like a real sonar
display. The clue colours from the last unit now sit on a proper grid.

## Losing, and the reveal

A game you can only win is not much of a game. Line 440 caps the search at 30 probes; run out and
the program jumps to a lose screen (lines 720–800) that sweeps the grid one last time and prints
where the targets *were* — `(a,b)` for every `g(a,b) = 9`. That reveal reuses the same grid sweep
yet again, this time to report where the targets sat instead of to measure a distance. The win screen (lines 670–700) rates the
player on guess count: ten or fewer is an "Expert tracker", and so on — a small reward that gives
skill something to aim at.

## Play again

Line 840 — `GO TO 10` — sends the finished game back to its own title screen, placing three new
targets and starting over. The whole program is a loop around a single grid, and that one
two-dimensional array carried everything: the hidden targets, the probed cells, the distance
clues, and the final reveal.

## What you built

Sonar is your first grid game, and the first time state lived in **two dimensions**. You used a
2D array as a board, drew it and searched it with the same nested loop, turned a miss into a
**Manhattan distance** with `ABS`, coloured the clues into a heat map, and wrapped it all in a
title, a probe limit, ratings, and a replay loop. The design idea underneath is **search** —
narrowing a hidden location from the clues it leaves.
