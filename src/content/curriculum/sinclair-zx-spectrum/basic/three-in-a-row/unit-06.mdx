## Three endings, one place

The move loop now routes to one of three ending screens — `You win!` (line 900), `Computer
wins!` (950), or `Draw!` (1000) — and each updates a running tally: `wins`, `losses`, `draws`,
started once at line 110 and shown in the status bar (line 130) and on every ending. Keeping the
score across rounds is what turns single games into a session, and it costs only three counters
that survive the replay.

## Play again

Line 1070 re-dimensions the board — `DIM b(9)` clears all nine cells back to zero — resets the
move count, and loops to a fresh game *without* touching the scores. Re-`DIM` is the cleanest way
to wipe an array: one statement empties the whole board. The title screen (lines 20–70) draws a
small sample grid with `PLOT`/`DRAW` so a new player sees the rules before the first move.

## What you built

Three in a Row is your first game with an opponent. You held the board in a nine-cell array, drew
it as a 3×3 grid, and detected a win by scanning a `DATA` table of the eight winning lines with a
reusable `GO SUB`. Then you built the computer's move as a priority of rules — **win, block,
strategy** — the first game AI in the course, and showed why sound play makes the game draw. Around
it sits a title, three endings, a kept score, and a replay loop.

The idea underneath is **board evaluation**: the program reads a position and judges what to do.
You will reach for it again in any game where the machine has to decide.
