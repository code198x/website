Begin with 1. O takes 5 and reports `centre`. Restart and choose 5: O takes 1 and reports `corner`. The earlier win and block checks still outrank these preferences.

## A fallback with a definite order

The centre belongs to four winning lines. Each corner belongs to three; each edge to two. That gives us a reason to try these groups in this order, while leaving the actual tactical checks first.

Line 4040 can choose the centre directly. The corner search reads 1, 3, 7 and 9 from DATA at 9530. Its `mv = 0` guard preserves the first free corner. RESTORE 9530 selects this small list; the opportunity routine still restores 9500 for winning triples.

If no centre or corner is free, the final loop scans all nine squares. It has no first-choice guard, so each empty square replaces mv. The last empty square wins. At this point only edges can be empty, which makes `edge` an accurate reason even though the loop visits the whole array.

## What the rules leave out

An immediate opportunity has two marks and a gap. A promising position may have only one mark in each of two lines. Our search does not recognise that longer plan. A preference can steer towards useful space without understanding what the opponent will do with it.

<Question prompt="If the centre is occupied, the only free corners are 3 and 7, and no immediate win or block exists, which corner is chosen? What if only edges 2 and 8 remain empty?">

3 is the first free corner in the DATA list. For the edge fallback, 8 replaces 2 as the loop proceeds, so 8 is chosen. Both results follow from the exact tie rules, not chance.

</Question>

Keep watching the reasons during a round. When `block` appears, find the two X marks that demanded it. When `corner` appears, check that there was no immediate opportunity and the centre was occupied. In the next lesson we will use that predictability to make two threats at once.
