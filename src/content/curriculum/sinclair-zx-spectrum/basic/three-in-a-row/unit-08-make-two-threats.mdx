Start a fresh round with R and play **1, 8, 7, 4**, waiting for each O reply. Before entering 4, stop and inspect the board.

| Your move | O's reply | Why O chose it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5 | Centre is free |
| 8 | 3 | First free corner |
| 7 | 9 | Block the bottom row |
| 4 | No reply | X completes the left column |

<Figure src="/images/sinclair-zx-spectrum/basic/three-in-a-row/teaching/fork.png" width={352} alt="X occupies 1, 7 and 8; O occupies 3, 5 and 9. Square 4 remains free to complete X's left column." caption="O blocked square 9. Square 4 still wins for X." />

## One move, two threats

After you place X at 7, the left column has X in 1 and 7, leaving 4. The bottom row has X in 7 and 8, leaving 9. This is a **fork**: two different winning moves are available next turn.

The search finds the bottom row before the left column because rows precede columns in DATA. It returns 9, and O reports `block`. That statement is truthful. It blocked one immediate threat; it did not establish that the board was safe.

Choosing 4 instead would not solve O's problem. You could then finish the bottom row at 9. Once this fork exists, one defensive move cannot fill both gaps.

<Question prompt="Would reversing the winning-table order prevent this defeat?">

It would change which threat O blocks. The other remains available. Preventing this fork requires an earlier decision, before both threats exist; changing the tie order at the final block is too late.

</Question>

## Keep the limitation visible

Our program considers immediate wins and threats, followed by positional preferences. It does not try future replies and their replies. Many careful rounds will draw, but a draw in several trials does not prove that a policy is unbeatable.

Replay without following the table. Can you identify a threat before the computer labels its response? Understanding the chosen rule is the point of the reason caption. We will keep this fallible opponent for the finished game and add a session around it next.
