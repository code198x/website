Lines 20–40 define two user-defined graphics with `DATA` and `POKE USR`: a filled circle (`CHR$ 144`) and a hollow circle (`CHR$ 145`). These become the clue pegs.

Lines 440–450 redraw the clues as coloured pegs instead of numbers: bulls as red filled circles (`INK 2`, `CHR$ 144`), cows as white hollow circles (`INK 7`, `CHR$ 145`) — one peg per bull, one per cow. Red pegs first, then white. Three red and a white reads at a glance, faster than "3 bull 1 cow".

Line 480 adds a pitch-scaled beep: `BEEP 0.05, bulls * 5`. More bulls, higher pitch. The player hears progress before reading the pegs. Zero bulls is a low thud; four bulls is a triumphant chirp.

Line 90 draws a small padlock on the title screen with `CIRCLE` and `DRAW` — a shackle above a body, tying the title to the theme. Line 560 prints the win message in green (`INK 4`); line 620 prints the loss message in red (`INK 2`). Lines 660–680 add the replay loop: "Press any key to play again", `PAUSE 0`, `GO TO 10`.

## What you have built

Locksmith is complete. Sixty-four lines: a title screen with a padlock graphic and the bull/cow terminology, a hidden four-digit code, input parsing, the digit-counting algorithm for bulls and cows, a ten-guess game loop with visible history, clues drawn as coloured pegs with pitch feedback, win and lose screens, and a replay loop.

This is the first game where the player's tool is pure logic. No reflexes, no luck, no resource management — just information and deduction. The program provides clues; the player reasons to a conclusion. That relationship between hidden state and visible clues is the foundation of every puzzle game.
