Kings earn the privilege of backward movement. An ordinary piece can only move toward the opponent's back row. A king can move in any diagonal direction. This changes two lines in the move validation.

## How It Works

**Line 980** now checks `b(q,p) < 3` before applying the forward-only rule. If the piece value is 3 or 4 (a king), the check is skipped entirely. Kings can move in any direction.

**Line 990** does the same for Player 2's kings.

The change is minimal: add `b(q,p) < 3 AND` before the existing forward check. This is conditional rule relaxation. The rule still exists for ordinary pieces, but kings are exempt.

## Conditional Rule Relaxation

This pattern appears in many games. A base rule applies to most pieces, but special pieces ignore it. In code, you wrap the existing rule in an additional condition:

**Before (all pieces must move forward):**
`IF t = 1 AND v < q THEN ...`

**After (only non-kings must move forward):**
`IF b(q,p) < 3 AND t = 1 AND v < q THEN ...`

The value 3 is the threshold. Values 1 and 2 are ordinary pieces (below 3). Values 3 and 4 are kings (3 or above). The single comparison `< 3` handles both players' kings.

## King Strategy

Kings are the strongest pieces in draughts. They can retreat, which means they can threaten pieces from behind. They can patrol the board, covering more territory. Getting a piece promoted to king is a strategic priority. But a king is still just one piece - it can be captured like any other.

The ability to move backward also changes the no-moves stalemate detection we will add later. A king almost always has a legal move, unless it is completely hemmed in.

## Try This

**Test backward moves.** Promote a piece to king and try moving it backward. It should work. Then try moving a non-king backward and confirm it still fails.

**King vs piece.** Play with one king on each side. Notice how much more mobile the king is compared to ordinary pieces. The game feels different when kings are in play.
