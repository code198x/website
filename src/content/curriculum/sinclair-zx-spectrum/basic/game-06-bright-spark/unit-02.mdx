The panels sit on screen, static. The game needs them to *flash* — a brief pulse that says "this one, pay attention."

<CodeFromFile src="sinclair-zx-spectrum/basic/game-06-bright-spark/unit-02/flash.bas" />

Lines 150–190 flash the red panel:

1. `BRIGHT 1` — switch to the bright variant of the current paper colour. Bright red is lighter, more vivid than normal red. The difference is visible at a glance.
2. Redraw the panel with the brighter colour.
3. `BEEP 0.3, 5` — a tone that lasts while the panel is bright. Sound and colour arrive together.
4. `BRIGHT 0` — switch back to normal.
5. Redraw the panel at normal brightness.

The result: a quick pulse. The panel goes bright for about a third of a second, then returns to normal.

## BRIGHT

The Spectrum has 8 ink colours and 8 paper colours — but each has a BRIGHT variant, accessed with `BRIGHT 1`. Normal red (2) is dark; bright red is vivid. Normal blue (1) is deep; bright blue is lighter.

`BRIGHT 1` applies to everything printed after it, until `BRIGHT 0` reverts to normal. The difference is subtle on some colours (white barely changes) and dramatic on others (blue and green shift noticeably). For Bright Spark, the flash effect relies on the difference being immediately obvious.

## Sound as identity

Each panel gets its own pitch: red is low (5), blue is higher (10), green higher still (15), yellow highest (20). The player memorises the sequence through two channels — colour and sound. Together they create **signposting**: the game tells the player what to pay attention to without using words.

Close your eyes and listen to the flash. Can you tell which panel it was from the tone alone? If yes, the signposting works through both channels independently.
