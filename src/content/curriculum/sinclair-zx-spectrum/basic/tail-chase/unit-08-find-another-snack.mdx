## Choose within the arena

`RND` supplies a value from zero up to, but not including, one. Multiplying by 16 gives a value below 16. `INT` takes the integer part for these non-negative values, producing 0–15; adding one gives rows **1–16**. The same calculation with 24 gives columns **1–24**.

Line 2010 checks the candidate in g. If occupied, it goes back to 2000 and chooses both coordinates again. Only a free candidate reaches the PRINT statement. It does not erase a piece of body to make room for food.

The initial body contains four cells in a 384-cell arena. Even the final twelve-cell body will leave free squares. This program therefore needs no full-board outcome. A game that could fill the arena would need to detect that condition before trying forever to find an empty square.

Repeated random trials have no fixed attempt count. A board with few free cells could spend much longer looking for food; our small body cap avoids that dense-board design.

## Random does not mean always different

RUN the program, find the food, finish and retry. A new food can appear in a previously used square. Its position should always remain inside the border and outside the current body.

The routine now uses the generator's current sequence. A fresh machine can repeat that sequence; the final title will use `RANDOMIZE` at the player's start to vary its starting point. Random placement and seeding are separate decisions.

<Question prompt="Why is a remembered earlier food position not automatically a reason to reject the new one?">

The rule rejects cells occupied by the current body. Previous food positions are allowed when free. Forbidding repeats would introduce a separate history and a different game rule.
