You've been using colour since Game 1 — red borders in Wire Panic, yellow digits in Lucky Number, green for victory. But you've never stopped to understand the system behind it. The Spectrum has exactly 15 colours. Not 256. Not millions. Fifteen. And that constraint is what makes Spectrum games look distinctly *Spectrum*.

## The Eight Colours

Every colour on the Spectrum is a number from 0 to 7:

| Number | Colour |
|--------|--------|
| 0 | Black |
| 1 | Blue |
| 2 | Red |
| 3 | Magenta |
| 4 | Green |
| 5 | Cyan |
| 6 | Yellow |
| 7 | White |

Eight colours. You've already used most of them — INK 2 for red text, PAPER 1 for blue panels, BORDER 4 for a green border.

## BRIGHT — The Other Seven

`BRIGHT 1` makes any colour brighter. Normal red vs BRIGHT red. Normal blue vs BRIGHT blue. That gives 8 normal + 7 bright = 15 usable colours (BRIGHT black is just black).

![Normal vs BRIGHT — the same colours, more vivid](/images/sinclair-zx-spectrum/basic/game-03-bright-spark/unit-02-colour/bright.png)

The BRIGHT versions are noticeably more vivid. This is exactly how Bright Spark's panels flash — the panel is drawn in normal PAPER, then redrawn in BRIGHT PAPER. The colour is the same. The intensity changes. That *pop* is what catches your eye.

That's every colour the Spectrum can display. Fifteen usable colours (since BRIGHT 1 on black just gives black again). Every Spectrum game ever made — Manic Miner, Jet Set Willy, Knight Lore — used these same fifteen colours. The constraint forced creativity: if you can't have more colours, you have to use the ones you have *well*.

## Four Commands, One System

The colour system has four parts:

- **INK** — the foreground colour (text, PLOT pixels)
- **PAPER** — the background colour (character cell fill)
- **BORDER** — the screen surround (the wide area around the main display)
- **BRIGHT** — intensity toggle (0 = normal, 1 = bright)

<CodeFromFile src="sinclair-zx-spectrum/basic/game-03-bright-spark/unit-02/ink-paper.bas" />

INK controls what you draw. PAPER controls what's behind it. BORDER controls the frame around the whole screen. BRIGHT makes either INK or PAPER more vivid.

Each character cell (8×8 pixels) has its own INK and PAPER. That's why Bright Spark's panels can be different colours — each cell in the red panel has PAPER 2, each cell in the blue panel has PAPER 1. Change a cell's PAPER and its background changes.

## Colour as Communication

In Bright Spark, each panel is identified by its colour alone. You don't read "red panel" — you *see* red and your brain maps it to button 1. In Wire Panic, the border colour told you how much time was left without words. In Lucky Number, the temperature bar shifted from blue (cold) to red (hot).

This is **colour as information**. Game designers use colour to communicate faster than text:

- **Red** means danger, heat, urgency, stop
- **Green** means safe, success, go
- **Blue** means cold, calm, distance
- **Yellow** means warning, warmth, attention

These associations aren't arbitrary — they're cultural, sometimes biological. The Spectrum's limited palette forces you to use colour deliberately, because you can't waste colours on decoration when you only have fifteen.

## What You've Learnt

- **8 base colours** numbered 0-7: black, blue, red, magenta, green, cyan, yellow, white
- **BRIGHT 1** doubles the palette to 15 usable colours
- **INK** sets foreground, **PAPER** sets background, **BORDER** sets the surround
- Each **character cell** has its own INK and PAPER
- **Colour communicates** — red means danger, green means safe, and your games should use that
