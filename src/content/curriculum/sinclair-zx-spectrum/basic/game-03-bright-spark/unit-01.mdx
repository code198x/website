Lucky Number tested your logic. Wire Panic tested your nerve. Bright Spark tests your memory.

Four coloured panels fill the screen. The computer flashes one — a burst of BRIGHT colour with a musical tone. Then another. Then another. Your job: repeat the sequence. Get it right and it grows by one. Get it wrong and the game tells you how far you got.

It sounds simple. It is simple. That's what makes it hard to put down.

## The Listing

About 100 lines. The panels are drawn with FOR loops and PAPER colours — the same loops that were black boxes in Lucky Number and Wire Panic. The sequence is stored in a string that grows by one character each round. Each character is a panel number.

## Run It

Type `RUN` and press ENTER.

Four panels appear — red (1), blue (2), green (3), yellow (4). "Watch... Round 1" appears at the bottom. One panel flashes bright with a tone. "Your turn!" — press the matching number.

![Four coloured panels — red, blue, green, yellow](/images/sinclair-zx-spectrum/basic/game-03-bright-spark/unit-01-type-it-in/panels.png)

Get it right and "Correct!" appears. The sequence grows. Round 2 — two flashes to remember. Round 3 — three. Each round adds one more to the chain. How far can you get?

Get it wrong and the game ends. The border flashes red. A low tone plays. Your score and the sequence length appear.

![Wrong — the sequence was too long to remember](/images/sinclair-zx-spectrum/basic/game-03-bright-spark/unit-01-type-it-in/wrong.png)

## What Makes It Addictive

Play three or four rounds. When you fail, notice what you feel. It's not frustration — it's *almost*. You almost had it. You can almost see the sequence in your mind. One more try and you'll get further.

This is **pattern recognition** — one of the most fundamental sources of fun in games. Game designer Raph Koster argues that fun *is* the feeling of learning patterns. Your brain encounters a sequence, builds a mental model, and tries to hold it. Each round stretches your model a little further. The moment it breaks, you know exactly where it broke — and that knowledge makes you want to try again.

The difficulty curve is perfect because it's personal. The sequence grows by exactly one each round — always just beyond your current ability. Too easy would be boring. Too hard would be unfair. One-more-than-last-time is the sweet spot. Game designers call this a **difficulty curve** — the rate at which challenge increases. Bright Spark's curve is the simplest possible: linear, one step at a time, calibrated to your own memory.
