The border in Hot and Cold shows how close you are to the treasure. But "how close" needs a number — the **distance** between the cursor and the hidden treasure.

## The Formula

<CodeFromFile src="sinclair-zx-spectrum/basic/game-04-hot-and-cold/unit-04/distance.bas" />

The cursor is at (10, 5). The treasure is at (25, 15). The distance between them:

1. `dx = x - tx` — the horizontal gap (10 - 25 = -15)
2. `dy = y - ty` — the vertical gap (5 - 15 = -10)
3. `dx^2` — square the horizontal gap (225). The `^` operator raises a number to a power.
4. `dy^2` — square the vertical gap (100)
5. `SQR(dx^2 + dy^2)` — square root of the sum (SQR(325) ≈ 18.03)

This is **Euclidean distance** — the straight-line distance between two points. Squaring removes the negative signs (a gap of -15 becomes 225, just like +15 would). The square root brings it back to a meaningful scale.

You might wonder: why not just use `ABS(dx) + ABS(dy)`? That gives **Manhattan distance** — the distance if you can only walk along grid lines (like streets in Manhattan). Euclidean distance is the "as the crow flies" distance. For Hot and Cold, Euclidean is more accurate — diagonal movement should feel closer than walking around the corner.

## Two New Commands

- **`^`** (power) — `dx^2` means "dx squared" (dx × dx). `n^3` means n × n × n.
- **`SQR`** — square root. `SQR(25)` = 5. `SQR(100)` = 10. The inverse of squaring.

Together they measure distance. This formula appears in every game that needs to know how far apart two things are — collision detection, AI targeting, proximity triggers. The maths is the same whether you're on a Spectrum or writing a 3D engine.

## What You've Learnt

- **`^`** raises a number to a power — `dx^2` is dx squared
- **`SQR`** takes a square root — the inverse of squaring
- **Euclidean distance** = `SQR(dx^2 + dy^2)` — straight-line distance between two points
- This formula is **universal** in game development
