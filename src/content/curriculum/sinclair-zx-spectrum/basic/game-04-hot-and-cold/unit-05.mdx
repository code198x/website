You have a distance number. The player doesn't want a number — they want a *feeling*. Hot and Cold converts distance into border colour:

| Distance | Border | Feeling |
|----------|--------|---------|
| > 24 | Black (0) | Freezing — nowhere near |
| 16–24 | Blue (1) | Cold — wrong area |
| 10–16 | Magenta (3) | Cool — getting somewhere |
| 6–10 | Red (2) | Warm — close |
| 3–6 | Yellow (6) | Hot — very close |
| ≤ 3 | White (7) | Burning — almost there |

Six levels. The colour shift from cold (black/blue) to hot (yellow/white) maps directly to human intuition — cold colours for far away, warm colours for close. The player never reads a number. They *feel* the distance through the colour of the screen surround.

This is **ambient feedback** — information delivered through the environment rather than through text or numbers. The entire border changes colour, which means the player can't miss it — it's in their peripheral vision even when they're focused on the cursor. You don't have to look at the feedback. It surrounds you.

Lucky Number's temperature bar was directed feedback — you looked at it to read the information. Hot and Cold's border is ambient feedback — you feel it without looking. Both are feedback. The difference is how they reach the player.

The six IF/THEN lines that create this mapping look mechanical, but the *choice* of thresholds and colours is a design decision. Why six levels instead of three? Because the transitions create a sense of gradient — you feel yourself getting warmer step by step. Why white for the closest? Because it's the most intense colour the Spectrum can display. Every threshold is a design choice disguised as a number.

## What You've Learnt

- **Mapping values to colours** turns numbers into feelings
- **Ambient feedback** — information in the environment, not in a display
- **Cold-to-hot colour mapping** uses human intuition about colour temperature
- The **thresholds** are design choices — where the colour changes determines how the game feels
