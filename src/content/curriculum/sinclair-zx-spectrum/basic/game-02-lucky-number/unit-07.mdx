The game works. It tells you "Too high!" and "Too low!" and eventually "Got it!" But it only speaks in words. Real games speak with colour too.

New lines: 80 computes the distance between the guess and the secret. Lines 90–130 set the border colour based on that distance.

Run it. Guess 50 when the answer is 73. The border turns blue — cold, far away. Guess 70. The border shifts to yellow — warmer. Guess 72. White — burning hot. You know you are close before you read the text.

## How it works

`LET d = ABS (g - n)` calculates the **distance** — how far the guess is from the secret. `ABS` gives the absolute value: `ABS(70 - 73)` is 3, and `ABS(76 - 73)` is also 3. It does not matter which direction you are wrong in — only how far.

Then five `IF` lines map that distance to a colour:

| Distance | Colour | Feeling |
|----------|--------|---------|
| Over 50 | Blue (1) | Cold |
| 26–50 | Cyan (5) | Cool |
| 11–25 | Yellow (6) | Warm |
| 6–10 | Red (2) | Hot |
| 5 or less | White (7) | Burning |

The border is **ambient feedback** — information delivered through the environment rather than text. The player reads it peripherally, without looking away from their guess. A modern designer would call this a proximity indicator; a 1983 player would call it clever.

## The design lesson

The game has two feedback channels now: words ("Too low!") and colour (blue border). Both say the same thing — "you are far away" — but in different ways. The words tell you the direction. The colour tells you the distance. Together they give the player more information than either alone.

This pattern — mapping a number to a visual range — recurs everywhere. Health bars, radar proximity, temperature gauges, even the Spectrum's own volume control in later games. You have just built the simplest version.

Try adjusting the thresholds. When should the border turn from blue to cyan? From red to white? There is no right answer — only the answer that *feels* right when you play.
