Reach the central strip and stop there for a moment. Look at the next two lanes together. A gap that admits one move may place you where the following move is blocked and waiting is dangerous.

## Trace a safe wait

The following state occurred during a complete crossing. The player is at (9,4), on the central strip. Each row below lists all six lane values in top-to-bottom order.

| Moment | Phases p(1)..p(6) | Countdowns t(1)..t(6) | Player |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before waiting | 3, 7, 0, 4, 3, 6 | 3, 3, 1, 3, 3, 1 | (9,4) |
| After waiting | 3, 7, 1, 4, 3, 5 | 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2 | (9,4) |
| After moving up | 3, 7, 1, 4, 3, 5 | 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1 | (9,3) |

On the waiting update, only lanes 3 and 6 are due. They move in opposite directions. The player is on a safe row, so neither contact query can fail.

On the next update, moving up proposes (9,3). That is lane 3, now at phase 1. Its occupied cells are 1,2,6,7,11,12; cell 9 is clear. No lane is due to move on this update, so the second query also passes.

Waiting has changed the traffic without changing the player's position. It gives a different next decision, not automatic protection for every later step.

## The same action inside a lane

Here is another observed sequence. Focus on the bottom lane, whose row is 7, direction is left and period is 2. The other lanes cannot hit a player in this row.

| Moment | Bottom-lane phase | Countdown | Player |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Before moving right | 9 | 2 | (7,7) |
| After moving right | 9 | 1 | (8,7) |
| After waiting | 8 | 2 | (8,7), caught |

Moving right succeeds: phase 9 occupies 9,10,14,0,4,5, leaving cell 8 empty. The countdown falls to one, so traffic stays put. On the following update, a wait passes the first query, then traffic moves left to phase 8. Cell 8 becomes occupied and the second query ends the attempt.

<Question prompt="In the second example, would moving right again be a way to escape instead of waiting?">

No. The destination would be cell 9, which is already occupied before traffic advances. The first query would reject it. Moving up, left or down needs its own checks; this example does not establish that every escape route is blocked.

</Question>

## Make a prediction you can test

Use R to restore a known opening. Choose a route to the central strip and try to repeat it, paying attention to the traffic rather than only the number of key presses. Real time continues between commands, and this version still misses some short taps, so manual attempts need not reproduce identical update counts.

Before leaving the strip, say which cell you intend to enter, which lane it belongs to, and whether that lane is about to move. Then name a possible next step. If you lose, distinguish entering an occupied cell from traffic arriving afterwards: the two result messages tell you which query failed.

These tables are traces from keyboard-driven runs of this program. They are not starting values to POKE into a live game. They let you reason about a complete stated position without requiring precisely timed manual replay.

The important change is the size of the decision. “Can I enter this gap?” remains necessary, but “What can I do after entering it?” helps explain the fuller crossing.
