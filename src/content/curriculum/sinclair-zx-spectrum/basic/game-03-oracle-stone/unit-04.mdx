Line 110 now picks from 1 to 10 (`INT (RND * 10) + 1`). Four new answers join the set: "THE STARS SAY YES", "NOT ON A TUESDAY", "THE ORACLE IS UNSURE", "WITHOUT A DOUBT".

The change is mechanical — more `IF` lines, wider range — but the effect is not. With six answers, the player sees repeats after three or four consultations. With ten, they can ask eight or nine questions before a repeat appears. The Oracle feels less like a machine and more like something with moods.

## Writing good answers

The best answers share three qualities:

**Vague enough to fit any question.** "YES" works for "Will it rain tomorrow?" and "Should I eat cheese?" — "THE RAIN WILL STOP AT 4PM" only works for one of those.

**Varied in tone.** Mix confident ("WITHOUT A DOUBT"), uncertain ("THE ORACLE IS UNSURE"), deflecting ("ASK AGAIN LATER"), and funny ("NOT ON A TUESDAY"). The variation makes the Oracle feel unpredictable.

**Memorable.** The player remembers "NOT ON A TUESDAY" and tells someone about it. They do not remember "MAYBE." Write at least one answer that makes people laugh.

## The pattern scales

Adding an answer costs two changes: increase the range in the `RND` formula, and add one `IF` line. That is all. The structure — random number, chained IF — handles any size set.

This pattern has a ceiling, though. At 20 answers, the `IF` chain is 20 lines long. At 50, it is unmanageable. Later games use `DATA`/`READ` for large sets — but for 10 answers, chained IF is the right tool. Simple, readable, and easy to extend by one more line.

## Try it

Write your own answers. Aim for 10 to 12. Run the program repeatedly and see if the Oracle feels like a character. If two answers feel too similar ("PERHAPS" and "MAYBE"), cut one — variety matters more than quantity.
