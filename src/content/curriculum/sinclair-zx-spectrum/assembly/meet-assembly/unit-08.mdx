Someone has changed our timed character program. **Right movement works, but left movement does not.** Your job is to find out why, make a small repair, and show that it works.

Start by reproducing the symptom. A motionless character could mean several things: perhaps the program missed a key, perhaps it kept the old position, or perhaps it failed to draw a changed position. We need evidence to tell those explanations apart.

The investigation below introduces each debugger reading when it becomes useful. Follow the stages at your own pace; switching stages keeps your source edits and notes. Explanations and repairs stay folded away until you ask for them.

## What would count as a successful repair?

Keep a short record of four things:

- The symptom you reproduced, including something that still worked.
- The first instruction whose result disagreed with what the program needed to do.
- The smallest change you made, and why it addresses that disagreement.
- The ordinary movement and boundary tests you repeated afterwards.

For the left edge, change the initial `position: defb 15` to `position: defb 0`, assemble again and stop at the left decision. Predict whether a decrement should happen. Restore your normal starting position afterwards.

A successful ordinary move does not establish that the edge is safe. Likewise, a correct position byte does not establish that the old picture was erased. Case 2 asks you to make that distinction with less guidance.

## Working with this debugger

A **breakpoint** is an address where you ask execution to stop. The buttons supply a key and run to the named label from your assembled source; they do not change the position directly. If you have already moved, the current position may differ from the starting value. Assemble again for a fresh machine.

**Step** executes the paused machine, rather than replaying a scripted answer. **Resume** returns to normal running. The source excerpt comes from the last assembly; the next instruction is disassembled from actual machine memory. Different formatting or numeric addresses do not mean they are different instructions.

The main view starts with the position byte and A. Z appears when the zero test matters; PC appears when you need to establish where the branch went. More registers and recent steps are available in the folded machine-state panel. Your prediction is kept for comparison; there is no automatic marking of your answer.

A source edit does not change the running program until you assemble it. **Revert restores the supplied faulty case 1**, not a repaired answer. Loading case 2 replaces the editor contents only when you press its button.

<details>
<summary>If a breakpoint is not reached, or stepping seems to wait</summary>

Running to a label has a fixed execution budget. “Not reached” means that execution did not arrive there within that budget; it is not evidence that the requested instruction ran. Check the source and the route that should reach it. For example, disabling interrupts prevents the timed program from progressing past HALT.

The browser frame loop is suspended while debugging. Step advances the actual machine through the emulator’s bounded stepping operation. At HALT it may still be waiting afterwards. Interrupts can also carry execution into the ROM service. Run to a named decision again to return to the part you are investigating.

The screen may show the last rendered frame while you step. Registers and RAM let you inspect an operation before a fresh frame is drawn.

</details>

## Take the method with you

Reproduce the fault. Choose an explanation you can test. Stop before the relevant work, predict a change, and compare it with what the machine did. Follow the evidence until you find the first disagreement, then repair and repeat the test.

The two exercise sources are `debug-branch.asm` and `debug-trail.asm`, deliberate faulty variants of lesson 7’s `clocked-character.asm`. They use the same Asm198x and Pasmo build commands, with the relevant filename substituted. They are investigation cases, not new game checkpoints.

This completes our introduction to instructions, memory, drawing, input, timing and investigation. Meteor Storm will combine that foundation into a game; its lessons are still in development.
