Win the night you've built and listen to what happens: DAWN BREAKS, the sweep pours gold — and the music that plays is `fanfare_held`, the *win jingle*, promoted to the biggest moment in the game because nothing better exists. It works. It's also borrowed, and you can hear the borrowing: the run's crowning moment sounds exactly like every held dusk on the way to it. The dawn deserves a phrase of its own, and this last unit writes it — then closes the module on the promise Gloaming made at its end: the file you're editing becomes the shipped game, byte for byte.

## The audition sheet

Here's how the real phrase was chosen, and the method matters more than the winner. Not "compose the right thing" — **write three candidates, one musical idea each, and choose by ear**. The candidates were sketched in a small notation this course's tooling understands (notes, durations, rests — nothing you can't read cold), and each got a WAV to audition. This is the sheet, verbatim from the prototype's files:

```text
phrase dawn_answer       ; the bookend — dusk's falling bell, reversed
  C5 400ms               ; where dusk ended, the ground note
  rest 8
  E5 300ms               ; the answer rises the motif
  rest 4
  G5 550ms               ; and opens into morning

phrase dawn_bloom        ; stepwise sunrise — climbs by step, not arpeggio
  C5 170ms
  rest 2
  D5 170ms
  rest 2
  E5 170ms
  rest 2
  G5 220ms
  rest 3
  C6 700ms               ; the sun clears the roofline

phrase dawn_amen         ; the plagal cadence — the night lets go
  F5 260ms               ; the held breath
  rest 3
  E5 260ms               ; released
  rest 6
  C5 320ms               ; ground regained
  rest 4
  C6 650ms               ; morning, wide open
```

The vote went to `dawn_answer`, and the reason is the module's last musical idea: **motif discipline**. Listen to the dusk bells again — they end on the motif falling to C5, the day giving up. `dawn_answer` starts on that same C5 and walks the motif *back up*, ending on a G5 that doesn't resolve… until the fanfare's C answers it, which is exactly what plays next in the dawn sequence. It isn't the prettiest candidate in isolation (`dawn_bloom` probably is). It's the one that makes the *game's whole soundtrack* one story: dusk asks, dawn answers, the fanfare completes. Themes beat tunes.

## Milestone 2 — the dawn you earned

The byte gate proves you rebuilt the shipped game. But this course keeps a second promise about *finished*, the one Gloaming's Unit 20 states outright: a game is not done until you can **drive it to its endings and watch them arrive**. The Long Night has the tenderest ending in the module — five watches survived, and DAWN BREAKS. So drive it there: the fifth watch, the eighth lamp, your last life — and the wisp arriving on the very same frame.

<Figure src="/images/sinclair-zx-spectrum/assembly/the-long-night/unit-10/step-02-dawn-denied.png" alt="NIGHT FALLS on a black screen, PRESS SPACE beneath — the loss screen, shown on the frame the fifth watch was completed." caption="The shipped build, on the fifth-watch tie: the last lamp of the last watch lit, the whole night held — and NIGHT FALLS anyway. Not a lost game: a stolen dawn." />

The wrong is a matter of *order*, and if it feels familiar it should — this is Gloaming's engine, and it carried Gloaming's bug forward whole. `play_step` moves the lamplighter, then moves the wisp, then asks whether the watch is complete. On the tie frame the eighth lamp lights — the night is held, and on the fifth watch the dawn is *due* — and then the wisp steps onto him; `draught_step` resolves the catch, sets NIGHT FALLS, and the dawn is never asked. The game grants defeat before it grants the morning you survived five watches to earn.

The fix is the one the whole family shares: ask the better question first.

```
play_step:
            call    player_step
            ; ... if the lamplighter's own move ended the night, stop ...
            ld      a, (lit_count)      ; the watch check, now FIRST
            cp      NUM_LAMPS
            jr      z, .watchdone       ; the watch is complete — held, this instant
            call    draught_step        ; only now does the wisp get its move
            ; ...
.watchdone:
            ; the eighth lamp: is this the last watch of the night?
```

Complete the watch and the night is held *this instant* — before the wisp is allowed its step. On the last watch, that instant is the dawn. Same tie, same last life:

### The wall the wisp walks through

The proof turns up a second inherited wrong, subtler and just as old. Hug a wall — or a pool of the solid dark — while the wisp is *phasing through it*, and a bump that should cost nothing costs a life. The wisp is not of the town; stone and dark mean nothing to *it*, and it drifts through both. But you shouldn't be able to die walking into a wall that still looks solid.

The culprit is the line the game is proudest of: `wall_at` reads collision straight off the screen. Elegant — until two things share a cell. While the wisp is drawn over a wall (or a dark pool), that cell's attribute is the *wisp's*, so `wall_at` and the `DARK` test are both fooled into waving the step through, and the mirror rule prices the bump as fatal contact. The truth didn't vanish; it moved into the wisp's buffer, `under_draught`, which still holds the ground beneath it — and *that* is what to ask:

```
            ; the wisp is on the target cell — but what's the real ground beneath it?
            ld      a, (under_draught + 8)   ; the ground the wisp stands on
            bit     WALL_BIT, a
            ret     nz                       ; wall under the wisp — blocked, harmless
            cp      DARK
            ret     z                        ; a pool of dark under the wisp — blocked too
            call    lose_life                ; open ground — the cold touches, and it costs
```

The lesson outlives the bug: **when two movers can occupy one cell, the screen stops being the ground truth** — the buffer that remembers what lies underneath is. It's the hazard hiding in the two-mover pattern you've used since Gloaming, and it hides until the proof walks you into a wall on purpose.

Both bugs shipped — in this game and in Gloaming before it — one engine, one pair of faults, carried forward together. Both were caught the only way their kind ever is: by driving the game to its endings on purpose and watching for the frame the machine does the wrong thing. `step-02` closes them, and the shipped Long Night becomes this build — the byte gate above still holds, now against a game that grants the dawn it owes you.

## When it's wrong, see why

- **The dawn hangs forever on one note.** The held G5 is two beeps back to back with `B` at maximum — a loop that reloads `B` without the second explicit beep never terminates the note.
- **The phrase is out of tune with the bells.** Motif discipline is arithmetic: the chime's C5/E5/G5 must use the same half-period constants as the tune's motif steps. If you transcribe pitches from a different octave table, the conversation becomes two strangers.
- **The chime plays but the sweep doesn't follow.** Order in `.thedawn` is the phrase, the sweep, the fanfare — a `ret` slipped between calls ends the ceremony early.
- **Silence at the dawn.** The chime runs before the state flips, inside the win beat — if you've moved it after the `game_state` write, it still plays; if you've moved it into `end_step`, it plays when the player *leaves* the dawn, which is a different (and worse) piece of theatre.
- **The dawn is denied on the last watch.** The watch check sits *after* `draught_step`, so a catch on the frame you complete the fifth watch steals the morning you earned. Move the `lit_count` test ahead of the wisp's move.
- **A wall or a dark pool kills you when the wisp is inside it.** `wall_at` and the `DARK` test read the wisp's own attribute off the screen and wave the step through; the mirror rule then prices the bump as fatal. When the target cell holds the wisp, ask `under_draught` for the real ground.

## Before and after

Module 2 ends where the prototype's own log ends: a five-watch night with behaviour, territory, grudges, escalation, a dawn, and a voice — every addition motivated by a named failure, every failure one you built and felt before you fixed it. That was the module's secret curriculum, and it's yours now: **iteration is the craft**. A finished game is a draft the playtest hasn't answered yet; the answers arrive as complaints ("a pain in the bum", "not very menacing", "lost at watch 2"); and each complaint, taken seriously, is a design lesson with your name on it. The two fixes in Milestone 2 are that lesson's last proof: even *byte-identical to shipped* was a draft, and the winnability gate was the playtest that answered it — a denied dawn and a wall that killed, closed at last.

## What you've learnt

- **Motif discipline**: phrases that share material make a soundtrack one story — dusk asks, dawn answers, the fanfare completes.
- **Audition, don't oracle**: three candidates, one idea each, chosen by ear — the working method for any creative decision a program has to embody.
- Notation-first tooling: `build198x beeper` turns readable phrases into audition WAVs and phrase assembly — tools emit *content*, engines stay yours.
- The held-note idiom, second sighting: one byte of duration means long notes are two beeps with a hidden seam.
- **The byte gate**: your build equals the shipped game exactly — convergence held across a full redesign, provable with one compare — and then, past the gate, made right where the proof said it wasn't.
- **A game is proven at its endings — and the proof finds bugs.** The denied dawn and the phase-wall death both hid until a scripted run to the ending went looking. Order the win ahead of the threat; trust the buffer over the screen.
