Gloaming's go-again hook was `best_lives`: the lives you kept on your finest win, remembered across the loop back to the title, read in lamps. For a one-dusk game it was exactly right — every run ended in a win or a loss, and "how much did winning cost you" was the only question worth keeping. This module broke it, quietly, the moment lives learnt to carry. On a five-watch night the update still fires at every held dusk, so what the title actually remembers is *the most lives you ever held a dusk with* — hold watch one without a scratch on a run that dies at watch two, and the board calls it your finest hour, forever. A metric that was the whole truth is now a boast about your opening.

The real redesign hit this exact strain, and the resolution is this unit — the shortest in the module, and the only one that takes something *away*. **A scoreboard is a design statement**: it tells the player what the game thinks deserves remembering. This game is about how deep into the night you get. So that's what survives.

## Milestone 1 — the metric swap

One step, five sites, and the diff reads like an argument. The best-lives update *leaves* the win path — working code, correct code, deleted because the question it answers is no longer the game's question. In its place: the dawn fills the row outright (`NUM_DUSKS` — the whole night held); the *lose* path gains something m1 never had — a watch survived is a watch earned, even on a lost night, so `best_dusk` takes the max on the way down; the title row moves a cell left and grows to five pips reading `best_dusk`; and the data byte swaps, name, comment and all.

That lose-path max deserves a second look, because it's the design's kindest line. Under best-lives, a lost run recorded nothing — you were only remembered if you won. Under the longest night, *every* run leaves a mark: die in watch three and the title holds three pips for you, a personal frontier to beat rather than a defeat to forget. The metric didn't just change subject; it changed **who gets remembered** — from perfect runs to all runs — and that single property is most of why "beat your best" hooks work at all.

## When it's wrong, see why

- **The title row shows garbage or overruns the screen.** The row is `NUM_DUSKS` cells wide now, from column 13 — a leftover `LIVES` in the loop count, or the old column 14 base, misdraws it.
- **A lost run never updates the best.** The max belongs in the lose path *before* the screen draws — `.gone` runs once, and after the state flips there's no second visit.
- **The dawn shows four pips.** The dawn writes `NUM_DUSKS` outright — five, the full row — not the current `dusk`, which is 4 on the last watch. (Counting from zero bites at both ends of a run.)
- **Held dusks still bump the best.** The old best-lives block survived in the win path. This unit's diff is a removal *and* an addition; if only the addition landed, the game keeps two scoreboards and the title tells whichever lie is larger.
- **The best resets between runs.** `best_dusk` must live outside everything `init_run` touches — it survives the loop by *not being reset*, same as `best_lives` before it.

## Before and after

The night finally remembers the right thing — and you've completed a full circuit of a discipline that most programmers meet only in the wild: **replacing a correct feature because the design outgrew it**. Not fixing a bug; deleting working code whose question expired, with the deletion argued line by line. Iteration isn't only addition. Every earlier unit stacked; this one *swapped* — and the game is smaller-hearted nowhere and truer everywhere for it.

## Try this: keep both books

Re-add `best_lives` alongside `best_dusk` and give the title two rows. Play a few nights, then be honest about which row your eyes check. One metric measures your route depth; the other measures a moment of caution that might have happened an hour of watches ago. The point isn't that two scores are wrong — plenty of games keep several — it's that every score you display is a claim about what matters, and claims compete.

## Try this: pips all the way down

Both this row and the lives ledger still refuse digits, and it's worth doing the maths on why that's a feature: five states read *at a glance*, in the game's own visual vocabulary, no font work — and the number can't inflate. A digit score whispers "more is possible"; a five-pip row says "here is the whole night, fill it." Sketch what this game would feel like with a six-digit score and notice everything the pips are quietly refusing.

## What you've learnt

- **A scoreboard is a design statement** — metrics tell players what the game thinks deserves remembering, and they age like any other design decision.
- **Replacement, not addition**: the only backbone diff in this module that removes working inherited code — the honest cost of a design that moved on.
- **Record on the way down too**: a best that only wins can update excludes most runs from mattering; the lose-path max is the hook's better half.
- Survivors live outside the reset tree — `best_dusk` persists for exactly the same structural reason lives carry: *where* state is initialised is *what* it means.
- Zero-based counts bite twice: the last watch is `NUM_DUSKS - 1`, and the filled row is `NUM_DUSKS`.
