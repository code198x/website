The keep looks like somewhere, and it *plays* — and it already makes sound when something happens: the coins chime as you lift them, the win flourishes, the Warden's touch tolls you into stone. But those are the sounds of **events**. Put your ear to the keep between them and there's nothing — no weight of stone underfoot, no groan of a door. This unit, the first of the **Keep's Voice** sub-arc, gives the *place* a voice to match its events, and the tool is one already ringing in your game: the single-bit beeper. It rang the coin's chime and the capture's toll a few units ago; you made it *sing* across *The Long Night* — the dusk bells, the dawn phrase. Here it stops being an event and becomes *ambience*: a click of stone underfoot, the groan of a door on its hinge. Same one bit, a wholly different job.

## One bit, one tone (already ringing)

You know this driver — it is the same `beep` the coin's chime and the capture's toll already call. Take it as a reminder, not a lesson. The speaker is a single bit — bit 4 of port `$FE`; toggle it high, low, high, fast, and the cone makes a square wave. `beep` takes two numbers — **B**, how many cycles to play (the duration), and **C**, how long to wait between toggles (the pitch; a larger `C` waits longer, so the wave is slower and the note lower):

```
beep:                               ; already in the game — the chime and the toll use it
.bp_cycle:
            ld      a, %00010000     ; speaker bit high
            out     ($FE), a
            ; ... wait C, toggle low, wait C ... B cycles in all ...
            ret
```

Everything new here is *composition* — choosing B and C to make the sounds the *place* makes, to sit alongside the sounds its *events* already make.

## Two sounds about place

A footfall is short and low — a handful of cycles of a low tone, gone almost before you notice it:

```
sfx_step:
            ld      b, 5             ; very short
            ld      c, 90            ; low
            call    beep
            ret
```

A door is a *creak* — not one pitch but a falling one. We start the delay small (higher) and let it grow (lower), a few cycles at each step, so the note groans downward like a heavy door swinging:

```
sfx_door:
            ld      c, 36            ; start higher
.sd_sweep:
            ld      b, 3
            push    bc
            call    beep
            pop     bc
            inc     c                ; lower the pitch a touch
            inc     c
            ld      a, c
            cp      130              ; until it has groaned right down
            jr      c, .sd_sweep
            ret
```

These aren't "you pressed a key" beeps. They're about the *place*: the weight of stone, the heaviness of a door. That distinction — sound as atmosphere, not as button-feedback — is the whole reason to bother.

## Wiring it in

Two call sites, and that's all. A footstep on every successful move, dropped in right after the thief is redrawn:

```
            call    draw_thief
            call    sfx_step         ; <- the footfall
            call    check_exit
```

And a creak the moment we pass through to another room, at the top of the room-transition path:

```
.enter:
            call    sfx_door         ; <- the hinges
            call    draw_room
            ; ...
```

Walk, and you hear yourself. Cross a threshold, and the door answers.

## Try this: a heavier tread

Raise `sfx_step`'s `B` to 12 and its `C` to 120. The step gets longer and lower — a booted guard, not a barefoot thief. Sound characterises *who is walking* as surely as the sprite does. Drop B to 2 and the footfall becomes a light tick; you can hear a different body just by changing two numbers.

## Try this: reverse the creak

Swap `sfx_door` to sweep the *other* way — start `C` high and `dec` it down to a small value. Now the door *rises* as it opens instead of falling. One groans shut, the other whines open; pick the one that sounds like the keep you're building. (Mind the loop test — sweeping down means `jr nc` / a different comparison.)

## Try this: give the Warden a sound

The Warden patrols in silence. Give it a voice — a slow, low pulse each time it steps, so you can *hear* it coming without seeing it. A footstep for the thing that hunts you changes the whole feel of the dark: now the crypt has a heartbeat. (Call your new sound from `warden_step`'s commit path, and keep it quieter and lower than the thief's, so the two tread differently.)

## When it's wrong, see why

- **No sound at all.** `beep` isn't being reached, or the calls aren't wired — check `sfx_step` follows `draw_thief` and `sfx_door` sits at the top of `.enter`.
- **The footstep is a long buzz.** `B` is far too high — a footfall is a handful of cycles, not a held note. Keep it short.
- **The door clicks instead of creaks.** The sweep loop runs once — check `C` starts small and the `cp` limit is high enough to sweep across a real range.
- **Every step stutters the movement.** The sound is long enough to be felt as a pause. Shorten `sfx_step`; ambient sound should sit *under* the action, not interrupt it.
- **The creak plays twice at a door.** `sfx_door` is called on both the exit test and the enter — call it once, on entry.

## Before and after

You started with a keep that sounded only when something *happened* and finished with one that sounds as you *move through it* — a click of stone under each step, a groan of hinges at each door. The tool was the beeper already ringing your chimes and tolls; the new work was two small routines and two hooks. Nothing on screen changed, and yet the keep feels twice as real, because half of believing a place is hearing it. Sound as atmosphere, not feedback — the difference between a game that pings and a place that breathes.

## What you've learnt

- **Ambient sound is different from event sound.** A footfall and a creak are about the *place*; a chime and a toll are about what *happened*. A believable keep needs both.
- **One primitive, many voices.** The same `beep` rings a chime, tolls a capture, clicks a footstep and groans a door — the composition is all in B and C.
- **A sweep is a gesture.** Falling pitch reads as weight and closing; rising reads as opening. A moving tone carries meaning a single note can't.
- **Ambience sits under the action.** Keep it short and quiet; it should colour the moment, never interrupt it.
