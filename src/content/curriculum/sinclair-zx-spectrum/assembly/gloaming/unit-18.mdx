Eighteen units, and Gloaming has never made a sound. It draws, hunts, wins and loses in total silence — and you feel the gap most at the moments that matter: a lamp catches and the world doesn't acknowledge it; night falls and the black just *arrives*. This unit gives the game the smallest voice its machine owns, and spends it in exactly three places.

## The speaker is one bit

The Spectrum's speaker hangs off **bit 4 of port `$FE`** — the same port that sets the border. Write a 1 to that bit and the speaker cone pushes out; write a 0 and it relaxes back. One write is a *click*. A **tone** is that click repeated: out, wait, in, wait, out — a square wave made by hand, and the whole of beeper audio is two numbers describing it:

```
beep:   C = the half-period  (pitch — bigger is lower)
        B = cycles to sound  (duration)
```

`beep` writes `%00010000` (speaker out, border still black — same port, different bits), burns `C` loops, writes zero, burns `C` again, and does that `B` times. Fourteen instructions, and it's the entire sound chip this game will ever have.

One line in it deserves special respect: the `di` at the top. The pitch lives in those busy-wait loops — counted in raw instruction time — and the 50 Hz interrupt has been firing throughout this whole game, every frame, since Unit 4. Let it interrupt mid-tone and random half-periods get stretched by a whole interrupt handler: the note buzzes and warbles. So interrupts go *off* while a tone sounds, *on* again after. Keep that trade in view — it means **while the beeper speaks, the game world stops**. Nothing moves, no keys read, no frames advance. For a blip lasting a twentieth of a second, that's an authentic little hitch every beeper game had; it's also why anything timed against real frames — a demo replay, a capture script, a speedrun timer — drifts slightly for every note played. Blocking sound is honest, cheap, and *costs time*; the non-blocking driver that plays music under gameplay is a later game's whole lesson.

## Milestone 1 — the lamp catches, audibly

The first voice goes where the game's heart beats: the lighting event. `blip_lit` is two chained beeps — a short C6, a slightly longer E6 — a **rising third**, the sound of something small going right. It's called in the cold-lamp branch, right beside `light_pip` and `warm_walls`: one event, three consequences — count, colour, and now sound.

Two lamps, two blips, resting on true silence. Notice what a difference the *interval* makes: a single flat note would be a beep; the little upward step is an event with a mood. On an instrument this poor — one bit, no volume, no envelope — the music theory is doing most of the work, and it costs nothing.

## Rests, and the two clocks

Before the endings get their phrases, one small routine with a big idea in it:

```
rest:
        halt
        djnz rest
        ret
```

Silence, counted in frames. Look at what that means: this program now keeps musical time with **two different clocks**. *Pitch* is measured in instruction time — busy-loops with interrupts off, thousands of counts a second, because a waveform can't wait for a frame. *Rhythm* is measured in heartbeats — `HALT`s with interrupts on, fifty a second, because the gap between notes is game time like any other. Tones live below the frame; rests ride on it. Every sound engine ever written balances these same two clocks; this one just does it in five lines you can see.

## Milestone 2 — each ending finds its phrase

The win and the loss each get a voice, built from nothing but `beep` and `rest` in sequence:

- `fanfare_held` — a **rising run up the chord**: C5, E5, G5, landing on a held C6. (Held how? `B` is one byte, 255 cycles at most — so the long note is simply sounded *twice*, back to back. The seams don't show; the wave just continues.) It plays after THE NIGHT IS HELD prints, blocking gladly — the game it would have paused is already over.
- `sting_nightfall` — the mirror: E5 stepping down to D5, then settling on a long, low A4. Where the fanfare climbs and arrives, the sting descends and *stays* — the night settling onto the square.

Three sounds, three meanings, and no two share a shape: up-quick for progress, up-long for triumph, down-and-settle for the fall. That's the entire discipline of game audio in miniature — **distinct events deserve distinct sounds, and the contour carries the meaning**. A player with their eyes shut can follow this game now.

## When it's wrong, see why

- **No sound at all.** The speaker is bit 4 — the beep must write `%00010000` and then zero. Writing to the wrong bits changes the *border*, which is at least a strong visual clue about where your OUTs are landing.
- **A click instead of a note.** The bit flipped once, not repeatedly — the `djnz .bcyc` is the tone; without the loop you have percussion.
- **The note warbles or buzzes.** Interrupts are still on — the `di`/`ei` pair is missing or the `ei` comes too early. Fifty times a second, the handler is stealing time mid-half-period.
- **The fanfare's phrasing is a smear — no gaps between notes.** `rest` isn't resting: without interrupts enabled, `halt` waits forever for an interrupt that can't come. The `ei` at the end of `beep` is what makes the following `rest` possible. (And if `rest` is missing its `ret`, it falls straight through into whatever follows — an entire extra phrase, uninvited.)
- **Everything works but the game stutters oddly during play.** It's meant to — each blip steals about five frames. If it grates, shorten the blip (`B` down), not the pitch loops.

## Before and after

The game found its voice in three phrases and about sixty lines. The instrument couldn't be poorer — one bit, toggled by hand, silencing the whole machine while it speaks — and that poverty is precisely what it teaches: pitch is a busy-loop, rhythm is the heartbeat, meaning is contour, and every note has a cost you choose to pay. Gloaming now announces its progress, celebrates its win, and mourns its loss. What it doesn't yet have is a front door: it still boots straight into the square, mid-story, no name, no invitation.

## Try this: retune the blip

`blip_lit`'s two notes are four bytes. Try a *falling* third — swap the pitches — and feel how wrong it is: the lamp now sounds like a mistake. Try two identical notes: flat, bureaucratic. The rising interval was doing more work than its size suggests. Then make a blip of your own — a fifth (C6 up to G6, `C = $45`), a quick octave — and keep whichever one makes lighting the eighth lamp feel best.

## Try this: give the catch a voice

One event still passes in silence: the wisp's touch. Write `sting_caught` — something short and low, two falling notes, cousin to the nightfall sting — and call it at the top of `lose_life`. Now the ember going out lands in the ear as well as the eye. (Notice you place it *once*, in `lose_life`, and both contact directions inherit it — the same event-placement discipline as ever.)

## Try this: hear the two clocks

In `fanfare_held`, change the rests from `B = 3` to `B = 25` — half a second of silence between notes. The phrase falls apart into disconnected beeps: rhythm was carrying the melody's sense of *line*. Now set the rests to zero (delete the `rest` calls): the notes fuse into a portamento smear. Somewhere between lies phrasing — and now you've tuned it by ear on both clocks.

## What you've learnt

- The beeper is **bit 4 of `$FE`**: a square wave made of two writes and two waits, pitch and duration as two bytes.
- **`di` while a tone sounds** — and blocking audio stops the world, a cost measured in frames.
- **Two clocks**: pitch counts instructions below the frame; rhythm counts heartbeats on it.
- An 8-bit duration holds a long note by **sounding it twice** — seams don't show in a square wave.
- **Contour is meaning**: rise for progress, climb for triumph, settle for loss — distinct events, distinct shapes.

## What's next

The game is complete and it *feels* nearly finished — but boot it and you're simply *in the square*, no name spoken, no moment to breathe before the night starts moving. **Unit 19** builds the front door: GLOAMING on a dusk screen, PRESS SPACE, a chime — and the setup code you've been growing since Unit 1 becomes `init_game`, called by a title that owns the act of beginning. The state machine gets its first state.
