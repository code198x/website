The wisp can pin him and it costs nothing but nerves. Cornered against a wall with the cold at your side, you wait, it holds, and eventually you slip away — the two contact vetoes from last unit keep the *cells* honest, but nobody pays. This unit prices the touch, and in doing so finishes what phase 4 came for: Gloaming becomes a game you can lose.

The pieces are almost all reused, which by now is the module's signature. Lives are the tally's machinery run in reverse. The respawn is the movement dance pointed at a constant. The lose screen is the win screen dressed in black. The only genuinely *new* thing this unit teaches is a design lesson — and true to form, we'll learn it by building the wrong version first.

## Three embers

Lives are shown the way this game shows everything: cells on the ledge.

```
LIVES      equ  3
LIFE_PIP   equ  %01010000      ; BRIGHT red PAPER — a small ember
LIFE_BASE  equ  $5800 + 28     ; row 0, the ledge's right shoulder
```

`draw_lives` is `draw_pips` with three substitutions, and losing one is `light_pip` run backwards — the same count-as-cursor trick, downward: decrement `lives`, and the *new* count is the index of the ember to put out. Three, two, one, dark.

## The anatomy of losing a life

`lose_life` is the whole unit in one routine, and its shape is worth reading before the diff:

1. **Take the ember** — decrement, blank the pip at the new count.
2. **If none remain, night falls** — more on that below.
3. **Otherwise, the lamplighter returns to the start** — and look at *how*: `restore_under`, set position, `save_under`, `draw_lamp`. That's Unit 8's dance, verbatim. A respawn isn't a special operation; it's an ordinary move whose destination happens to be a constant. Because it goes through the same four beats, the world stays whole — the cell he was pinned against heals, the start cell banks correctly, and nothing about the buffers needs to know a life was lost.

Both contact points — the wisp reaching him, him walking into the wisp — now call it. The touch has one price, whoever moves.

Every line of that code does what it should. The catch is legitimate, the ember goes out, the respawn is clean — and the game is unplayable, because the respawn sends him to a fixed, known cell and the thing that hunts him is *standing next to it*. This exact failure is in the shipped game's source as a comment, because it happened: the moment the draught learnt to hunt (rather than drift), leaving it in place after a catch turned one mistake into a stripped set of lives. Mechanics can be individually correct and *jointly* vicious — you find that out by watching them together, which is what playtesting is.

## The win's mirror image

`draw_lose_screen` is `draw_win_screen`'s dark twin, and the contrast is the design. The win *restored* — lifted him out and printed over a golden, finished world. The loss **erases**: one `LDIR` washes all 768 attribute cells to black — lamps, walls, warmth, the tally, the embers, everything — and prints NIGHT FALLS into the void. (The pixels are all still there, black on black; night didn't destroy the town, it just took the light. Which is, of course, the story.) A new state, `STATE_LOSE`, joins the machine, and the Unit 15 gate holds this screen exactly as it holds the win.

One structural detail matters more than it looks: `play_step` now checks `game_state` after the player's beat *and* after the wisp's. Contact can end the game mid-frame — and a frame must not keep happening to a game that's over. Without those guards, a lamplighter who spends his last life walking into the wisp would still hand the wisp its beat, and the wisp would calmly take a step on the blacked-out screen. End states aren't just reached; they have to be *respected*, immediately, by everything still in the pipeline.

## When it's wrong, see why

- **Night never falls.** Contact isn't registering — both contact points must `call lose_life` (Unit 16's vetoes, now with a price), and the wisp must be able to reach him at all.
- **The wrong ember goes out, or the bar corrupts leftward.** Index with the *new* count, after the decrement — `light_pip`'s trick, mirrored. Off by one here blanks `LIFE_BASE + 3` on the first hit: a cell that was never an ember.
- **The strip-mine is back.** The recoil block is missing or doesn't reset `draught_timer` — position alone isn't enough; a stale timer near zero gives the recoiled wisp an instant first step.
- **A catch leaves a wisp-shaped hole, or a phantom, at the catch site.** The recoil must run through `restore_draught` before moving its position — it's a mover like any other, and teleports go through the dance too. Same for him: a respawn that skips `restore_under` leaves him standing in two places.
- **The fallen screen still shows the pips, or the game keeps playing behind it.** The blackout's `LDIR` count is short (767 from `$5800` covers everything), or the mid-frame state guards are missing from `play_step`.

## Before and after

Phase 4 opened with a square that couldn't oppose you and closes with a game that can kill you — and the distance was covered almost entirely with parts you already had: pips in reverse, the dance as a teleport, the win screen in black. The one new thing was the one that couldn't be reused: the *design* discovery that correct mechanics compose into broken games, that a catch must cost the catcher too, and that you learn this by sitting still and watching — the cheapest playtest there is. Gloaming is now, in the full sense, a game: a goal, a threat, a win, and a loss.

## Try this: turn the mercy dial

`LIVES` is one number and the whole HUD follows it. At `1`, Gloaming is a roguelike — one touch, night falls; at `5`, it's gentle enough for a first evening. Notice the recoil changes what the number *means*: each life is a guaranteed full chase, so `LIVES 3` is really "three acts". Tune it with that in mind.

## Try this: make the hit land

An ember going out is quiet for something that cost a third of the game. In `lose_life`, before the respawn, flash the border red — `ld a, 2 / out ($FE), a`, a short busy-wait, back to black. One jolt, felt in the hands. (Next unit gives the game a *voice*, and the catch will get a sound; the flash is the visual half of the same idea.)

## Try this: only the night is deadly

Change the player-side contact from `call lose_life` back to a plain `ret` — walking into the wisp is refused but free; only *its* movement onto you costs. The game softens in a way the numbers don't capture: brushing past danger becomes a skill instead of a suicide. Then decide which game is yours. Both are one line.

## What you've learnt

- **Lives are the tally in reverse** — count-as-cursor, decrementing, embers out.
- A **respawn is an ordinary move** — through the same restore/save/draw dance, so the world survives it.
- **Correct mechanics can compose into a broken game** — the strip-mine was three sound rules and zero play.
- **A catch should cost the catcher** — recoil turns a death spiral into a three-act structure.
- **End states bind mid-frame** — check the state between actors, or the dead game keeps moving.

## What's next

Winnable, losable, and completely silent — Gloaming hasn't made a single sound in seventeen units. Phase 5 makes it feel finished, starting with **Unit 18**: the Spectrum's beeper — one bit on one port, toggled by hand — and three small voices built from it: a blip for a lamp lit, a fanfare for the night held, a sting for the fall. The machine has been drawing its own font since Unit 15; now it clears its throat.
