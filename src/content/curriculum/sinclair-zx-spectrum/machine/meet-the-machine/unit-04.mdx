There is no sound chip in a 48K Spectrum.

## What there is instead

One bit. Bit 4 of a single output port drives the loudspeaker, and writing to it moves the speaker cone to one of two positions. That is the entire audio hardware.

No digital-to-analogue converter. No envelope generator. No waveform table, no channels, no volume control. Compare that with the machine's contemporaries — the C64 had a three-voice synthesiser with filters — and the gap is not small.

## So how did it make notes?

By moving the cone back and forth at the right rate. Push, pull, push, pull, fast enough and evenly enough, and the air carries a tone.

The rate is the note. Middle C is 261.63 Hz, so the speaker has to change position 523 times a second — once every 6,689 T-states, using the clock from unit 2. That number is not an approximation someone settled for; it is what the ROM's `BEEP` routine actually counts.

## Which means the processor is the sound chip

This is the part that matters, and it follows directly.

A sound chip is given a note and gets on with it while the processor does something else. The Spectrum has nothing to hand the note to. **The processor has to be there, personally, at every single toggle** — 523 times a second for one middle C, counted out in T-states.

The ROM's own beeper routine turns interrupts off before it starts, because an interrupt arriving mid-note would delay a toggle and put the pitch out.

So on a stock Spectrum, sound is not something that happens alongside your program. It is something your program stops to do. `BEEP` in BASIC freezes everything until the note finishes, and that is not sloppiness — there is nothing else it could do.

## What people did anyway

That should have been the end of it, and it wasn't.

If the processor is going to be there at every toggle, it can decide what to do at each one. Interleave the toggles for two different notes and you hear two notes. Vary the proportion of time spent high against low and you get something that sounds like a change in volume. Beeper engines — Octode, Stocker and their relatives — got multiple channels, percussion and recognisable timbres out of a bit that is only ever on or off.

There is even a little more hardware there than "one bit" suggests. The speaker bit and the cassette output bit share a pin through a voltage divider, so the pair of them produce four distinct voltages rather than two. Two of those are below the threshold that makes the speaker fire, but engines that drove both bits together squeezed an extra usable level out of the difference. It is audible on real hardware.

None of it was free. A game playing that kind of music is spending most of its processor on it.

## What to take from this

The Spectrum's sound is a direct consequence of a design decision to leave sound hardware out. Everything anyone ever got from it was the processor being somewhere at exactly the right moment, and paying for it in the frame budget.
