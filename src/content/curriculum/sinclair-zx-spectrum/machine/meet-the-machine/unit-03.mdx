The Spectrum's screen is two things stored separately, and they are not the same size.

## Pixels first

The picture is 256 by 192 pixels, one bit per pixel. A bit is on or it is off. That comes to 6,144 bytes, and those 6,144 bytes contain no colour information whatsoever — only which pixels are lit.

## Colour second, and coarser

Colour lives in its own table of 768 bytes, immediately after the pixels. Not one entry per pixel: **one entry per 8×8 square**. The screen is 32 squares across and 24 down, and each of those squares gets a single byte describing how the whole square is coloured.

That byte holds four things:

| Bit | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 7 | FLASH — swap the two colours periodically |
| 6 | BRIGHT — use the bright variant |
| 5–3 | PAPER — the colour of the unlit pixels |
| 2–0 | INK — the colour of the lit pixels |

Three bits gives eight colours: black, blue, red, magenta, green, cyan, yellow, white. The BRIGHT bit gives a brighter version of each. Eight times two is sixteen — except black is black either way, so the famous sixteen colours are really fifteen.

## The rule that follows

Read the table again and the constraint falls out of it. Within any 8×8 square you get **one ink and one paper**. Not one per pixel — one for the whole square.

And BRIGHT is a single bit for the square, applying to both colours at once. You cannot have bright ink on ordinary paper. The two colours in a square are always drawn from the same eight.

So: sixteen colours on the screen, eight available inside any one square, two actually used there.

## What that looks like

Draw something that crosses a square boundary and its colours are decided by whichever square each part falls in. Move it one pixel and a whole 8×8 block changes colour at once.

This is attribute clash, and it is the single most recognisable thing about Spectrum graphics. The colour fringing around a moving sprite, the way a character seems to smear the background as it walks — none of that is a limitation of the artist. It is 768 bytes of colour trying to cover 49,152 pixels.

Programmers spent fifteen years answering it. Some designed sprites to fit the grid exactly. Some drew everything monochrome and used colour only for backgrounds that never moved. Some accepted the clash and made it part of the look.

## The other half of the trade

There is a compensation, and period code used it hard.

The colour table is 768 bytes against the bitmap's 6,144 — one eighth the size. Rewriting every colour on the screen costs roughly what rewriting a single row of characters' pixels costs. Given the frame budget from the last unit, that is an enormous difference.

So colour became the cheap thing to animate. Flashing, pulsing, colour-cycling effects, whole rooms changing hue between frames — all of it is code touching the small table and leaving the big one alone.

## What to take from this

Colour on the Spectrum is not a property of a pixel. It is a property of the square a pixel happens to be in, and there are only 768 squares. Every graphical decision on this machine — sprite size, character design, level layout, when to move something and when not to — is downstream of that one fact.
