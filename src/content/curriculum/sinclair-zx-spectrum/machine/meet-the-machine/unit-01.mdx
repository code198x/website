Switch a 48K Spectrum on and it shows almost nothing: a pale grey screen with a single line of copyright along the bottom. That is a fair introduction to a machine which did nothing at all until it was told to.

## The announcement

Clive Sinclair announced the ZX Spectrum on **23 April 1982**, at the Earls Court Computer Show. Two models: **£125** for 16K of RAM, **£175** for 48K. Orders, the announcement said, would be delivered in two weeks.

They weren't. Production didn't start until July, held up by a problem with the ULA — the single custom chip that drew the screen, read the keyboard and generated the sound. You'll meet the ULA properly later; for now it's enough to know that the part which made the machine cheap was also the part that made it late.

## Why there were so many

£125 was the point. It was not the best home computer you could buy in 1982, and nobody claimed it was. It was the one a family could afford, which meant it went into hundreds of thousands of houses where a computer had never been.

That matters for a reason beyond history. A machine that sells in those numbers gets written for — by teenagers in bedrooms as much as by studios — and the software written for it had to work within the same limits you're about to meet.

## Why it's worth your time now

The Spectrum is small enough to understand completely. Not "well enough to get by" — completely. One CPU, one custom chip, 48K of memory, and a manual that fits in your hands.

Almost nothing you use today is like that. That's the reason to spend time here: not nostalgia, but the rare chance to hold a whole machine in your head at once.

A machine that arrived at £125, aimed at people who could not previously afford a computer, and sold in the numbers that decision bought.
