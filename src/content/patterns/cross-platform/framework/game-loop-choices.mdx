A game needs a way to accept actions, change its state and show the result. It may also need a clock. Those decisions belong together, but they are separate pieces you can combine.

## What makes the next thing happen?

An untimed adventure can print a description, wait for a command, carry out that command and print the result. Nothing in its rules needs to change while you decide. Adding a frame wait would not make the game more correct.

A chess game can also wait for a move while a clock counts down. Accepting the move and running out of time are different events, either of which can end a turn. A turn-based game can certainly have a timer.

An action game might advance its simulation at a chosen rate while drawing and reading controls on their own schedules. A menu might wait for a new press while music continues playing.

| Decision | Useful choices | What to keep separate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Advance play | Command, move, turn, simulation step, event | A display refresh need not advance the rules |
| Measure time | No clock, elapsed ticks, countdown, scheduled event | A deadline need not make the game real-time |
| Accept input | Blocking command, held state, new press, buffered action | Holding a button need not repeat an action |
| Show the result | Redraw after a change, animate, queue display work | A display boundary is not automatically the game clock |

Start with what your game must do while the player thinks. A blocking reader is useful when nothing else needs servicing. If a countdown, animation or sound player must continue, service that work while waiting or use an interrupt with a clear ownership agreement.

## Add a deadline to a turn

Keep the current turn and its remaining time in separate state. Accepting an action changes the turn; a timer updates the remaining time and requests a turn change when it expires. Reset the deadline when a new turn begins if that is the rule you want.

Decide what happens if an action arrives at the deadline. Process it before expiry, after expiry, or compare a recorded arrival time: each is a policy with consequences. Do not let the order of two unrelated routines accidentally decide who wins.

The frame-paced examples below show one useful arrangement. Their waits decide when another update may begin; they do not guarantee that the work will fit.

## More ways to wait for a player

A BASIC adventure can let the firmware read a command and redraw after it changes the world. An Ace FORTH word can wait for a fresh key. A console may have to keep producing a picture while the player decides. Those are useful loops too, even when no countdown is running.

The [MTX caller loads through BASIC](https://github.com/code198x/code-samples/blob/cb326fb96138e63ea9008f1ac46d1d6f0f7804fb/memotech-mtx/patterns/assembly/framework/input-loop/README.md); [the Einstein caller boots from disk through MOS](https://github.com/code198x/code-samples/blob/cb326fb96138e63ea9008f1ac46d1d6f0f7804fb/tatung-einstein/patterns/assembly/framework/input-loop/README.md). Their turn state changes on a new press while the display keeps being serviced.

## Fit a routine into the loop

Read a controller once and share that sample with movement and actions. Keep state changes in the update. Draw from the resulting state. A hardware sprite write, a software blit and a buffered PPU request have different costs and timing restrictions even when they serve the same job.

The examples show a complete caller and a small wait routine. Copy both while learning, then retain the machine setup and ownership agreements your project needs. Replacing a wait is not enough to port a game.

## Choose a policy for missed time

These callers perform one update after each accepted boundary or clock sample. They do not rush through an unlimited backlog. A later game may use elapsed time, bounded catch-up, slower simulation than rendering, or deliberate slowdown. Choose that policy for the game, then measure its worst case on the target.

The byte-clock examples require fewer than 256 frames between samples. The polling examples do not count lost frames. A sound player, drawing routine or interrupt service must agree with those limits rather than silently taking ownership of the clock.

## Read the evidence narrowly

A verified run establishes this source, startup path, configuration, input sequence and workload. It can expose useful emulator failures. It does not establish every display mode, peripheral, timing edge or game on that emulator.
